# Credibility rubric

**This is for you, not the user.** Use it to choose which sources to present,
to order them, and to form your own view of a source. Never show the user the
rubric, its level names, or a score. When it helps, explain in plain words why
a particular source is strong or weak for their kind of research ("For a lab
report, the study itself is better evidence than a news story about it").

Every source you present has already cleared the source bar in `sources.md`.
This rubric sorts the ones that clear it.

## Signals, and how to check each one

Look at several signals together. **No single signal makes a source strong:**
a DOI, a famous name, or a "peer reviewed" label alone is never enough.
Every signal you rely on has to be checked in this session; if you couldn't
check one, say so rather than assume.

- **Peer review.** Confirm the journal on its own site, Crossref, OpenAlex,
  or a similar database. Warning signs of an untrustworthy journal: it
  guarantees acceptance or a very short review time (days), its fees are
  unclear, its publisher can't be identified or contacted, or it isn't
  indexed anywhere you can find.
- **DOI.** A working DOI shows a source was formally published and can be
  found again. It doesn't show quality: preprints and untrustworthy journals
  have DOIs too. Open it before you give it.
- **Publisher or venue.** A university press, a recognized journal, a
  government agency, or an established research organization is a good sign,
  not a guarantee. Fame isn't proof: well-known outlets publish weak work,
  and untrustworthy journals copy respectable names.
- **Author.** Their expertise on this topic, their institution, and whether
  others in the field cite them.
- **Evidence and methods.** Fact or opinion? Is there data, and how was it
  gathered (sample size, how participants were chosen)? Are claims supported,
  or is the language emotive?
- **Primary or secondary.** Is it the original research or record, or a
  report about it? A news story about a study is secondary to the study.
- **Date.** Current enough for the topic? Fast-moving fields need recent
  work; history and the humanities can rely on older scholarship.
- **Possible bias and corroboration.** Who funded it, who gains from its
  conclusion, and do other credible sources agree?

## Three levels

- **Strong evidence:** the best kind of source for this research type, with
  its key signals checked.
- **Usable with care:** credible but limited for this purpose, for example a
  news report of a study, a preprint, a doctoral dissertation, or a dated or
  narrow study. Include it only with a plain reason why it's there and what
  to watch for.
- **Not evidence on its own:** can point to better sources or give context,
  but shouldn't carry an argument by itself.

## Levels by research type

| Research type | Strong evidence | Usable with care | Not evidence on its own |
|---|---|---|---|
| Empirical study or lab report | Peer-reviewed original research; systematic reviews | Preprints; government data without analysis; older studies | News about a study; textbooks for specific findings |
| Argumentative essay | Peer-reviewed research; government and research organization reports | Reputable news for current events; preprints | Opinion pieces (even in reputable outlets) |
| Literature review | Peer-reviewed research and reviews; scholarly books | Preprints; reports from research organizations | News coverage of the field |
| Historical or interpretive | Primary sources from the period (letters, newspapers, records); scholarly books and articles | Scholarly reference works; older scholarship | Popular histories and undocumented websites (below the bar: useful for leads only, not as sources) |
| Exploratory | Peer-reviewed research and reviews; scholarly books | Reputable news; research organization reports | Encyclopedias (useful for leads only) |

## Using it

- **Source lists:** lead with strong evidence for the research type. Every
  source that isn't peer-reviewed research (a survey report, a news article,
  a preprint, a working paper) gets one plain line saying why it's there
  and what to watch for, about the source itself, never how it fits the
  user's position or sub-questions (R2.4), not just a summary of its findings ("A survey, not
  a study of learning: useful for how many students use chatbots, not for
  whether it helps them"). When intake recorded a
  source requirement ("at least 3 peer-reviewed"), say which sources meet
  it.
- **A source the user brings:** ask for their assessment first (R2.3). Then
  share your view in plain words, informed by the rubric. Your view is about
  credibility and fit for their research, never about whether the source
  supports their position.
