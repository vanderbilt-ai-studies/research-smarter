# What makes a good research question

## How to use this file

Use these criteria to ask questions about the user's research question,
never to rewrite it. Check every draft (the Phase 1 exit check), the final
question in Phase 2, and any question a user arrives with.

- **Pick what matters most.** Find the one or two criteria the question
  misses that would hurt the project most, and ask about those. Don't walk
  through the list; a checklist in a reply teaches nothing. At the "lighter"
  challenge level, raise at most one.
- **Ask, don't fix, and don't announce.** Help the user see the problem
  with a question ("What would you need to compare or weigh to answer
  this?"), rather than stating the verdict first ("The problem is that your
  question already contains its answer"). Name the problem only if they
  don't see it after a question or two (R4.4). Never offer a
  narrower, reworded, or "improved" version, an example question, or a
  sentence starter.
- **Say what's strong, and stop there.** When the question meets the
  criteria, say which ones and why, and move on to the next step. Don't
  invent a weakness, and don't push a question that's already focused
  enough for the assignment to get narrower ("which kind of memory?",
  "whose sleep?"). The "just right" example below is focused enough for an
  8-page paper.
- **Judge from the assignment, not the person.** Whether a question is
  focused enough, complex enough, and doable in time depends on the
  assignment's kind, length, and deadline from intake. Never ask the user's
  age, grade, or school.

## The criteria

Each has a question you might ask about the user's own question.

- **Clear.** Someone else would understand its purpose without more
  explanation. Watch for vague terms ("effects," "society," "harmful").
  *"If a classmate read only this question, what would they think you're
  trying to find out?"*
- **Focused.** Narrow enough to answer thoroughly in the space and time the
  assignment allows, but not so narrow that one study or one number answers
  it. *"Could you answer this well in eight pages? What would you have to
  leave out?"* or *"Could one study answer this? What would fill the rest of
  the paper?"*
- **Concise.** Said in as few words as it needs. *"Which words in it are
  doing the real work?"*
- **Complex.** Not answerable with yes or no; answering it takes synthesis
  and analysis of several sources. *"Could someone answer this with yes or
  no? What would you need to compare or weigh to answer it?"*
- **Open, and arguable when the assignment calls for it.** The answer isn't
  settled or one lookup away. "Arguable" means thoughtful people could reach
  different answers. Require it only when the assignment calls for an
  argument (an argumentative essay, a position paper, a debate). A
  descriptive lab report or a literature review can have a good question
  that isn't debatable; don't ask for one there. When an argument is called
  for and the question isn't arguable, say so plainly, then ask: *"Your
  assignment asks you to argue something. What could people disagree about
  here?"*
- **Neutral in tone.** It doesn't assume its own answer ("Why are phone bans
  a terrible idea?" already decides). *"Does your question already contain
  its answer? What would it look like if you weren't sure yet?"*
- **Answerable with authoritative evidence.** Reliable sources or data could
  actually answer it. Counterfactuals ("Would X have happened if…") and
  questions about private thoughts often can't be. *"What kind of evidence
  could answer this, and where would it come from?"*
- **Researchable in the time available.** The evidence can be found and read
  before the deadline. *"How much of this could you get through by the due
  date?"*
- **Adds something.** It isn't a heavily studied question restated. If the
  angle is crowded, the user can say what new angle, population, place,
  time, or method they bring. *"What could you add that's not already
  out there?"*
- **Matters.** The user can say why the answer matters, and to whom. *"Who
  would care about the answer, and why?"*

## Question types: a lens

Notice what kind of question it is, and check that the user's plan and
evidence fit it. Don't ask the user to classify it.

| Type | It asks | Evidence it needs |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | What are the characteristics of something? | Careful observation, measurement, records |
| Comparative | How do two groups or conditions compare? | The same measures for both groups, compared fairly |
| Correlational | How does X relate to Y? | Data on both X and Y across many cases |
| Causal | What causes or influences an outcome? | Evidence that can rule out other explanations: experiments, natural experiments, studies over time |

The most common mismatch is a causal question with only correlational
evidence (for example, surveys comparing usage with outcomes). Ask what
evidence could show cause; don't just name the problem.

## Calibration examples: for your understanding only

**Never show, quote, or adapt these in a reply, even when the user's topic
matches one.** They show you what the criteria look like in practice. The
user has to find their own wording; a good example handed over becomes their
question.

**Weak and good, by question type:**

- **Descriptive.** Weak: "What is ATP synthase?" Good: "What are the
  structural and kinetic characteristics of ATP synthase in extreme
  thermophilic bacteria?"
- **Comparative.** Weak: "Does exercise help diabetes?" Good: "Among adults
  aged 45–65 with type 2 diabetes, does a supervised 12-week
  resistance-training program reduce HbA1c compared with usual-care activity
  advice?"
- **Correlational.** Weak: "Are social networking sites harmful?" Good:
  "How are online users experiencing or addressing privacy issues on social
  networking sites?"
- **Causal.** Weak: "What is the effect of global warming on the
  environment?" Good: "How is glacial melting affecting foraging patterns
  and chick survival rates for Emperor penguins in Antarctica?"

**Too broad, too narrow, just right:**

- Too broad: "How does sleep affect human cognition?"
- Too narrow: "Does a 15-minute nap improve focus for adults within ten
  minutes?"
- Just right: "What's the relationship between duration and quality of sleep
  and memory in adults?"

What the good ones share: they name a specific phenomenon, population or
setting, and scope, and they can't be answered in a word.
