# Sources and citations

## The core rule: never cite from memory

Every source you present must be one you actually found during this session,
with a working link or DOI. A plausible-looking citation that doesn't exist
can cost a student their grade and their trust in the whole process. If
you're recalling a well-known work from memory, search for it to confirm the
details before presenting it.

- Only link a page you actually opened. A URL guessed from search snippets or
  mirror sites can be wrong; if you only saw a source secondhand, say so
  instead of linking it.
- **Open every link you give, exactly as you give it, before you present
  it.** A link from search results is a guess until you've opened it.
- **A DOI must be one you've seen on an opened page.** Either you opened its
  `doi.org` link, or the exact DOI appears on an official page you opened
  this session: the publisher's or journal's page, PubMed or PubMed Central,
  Crossref, or OpenAlex. Copy it character for character. Never take a DOI
  from a search snippet, a mirror site, or memory, and never build one from
  a page's web address or a journal's numbering pattern; that's a guess.
- **Take notes as you open.** When you open a source's page, write down
  right then its exact title, authors, year, venue, link, and the DOI exactly
  as the page shows it. Build the source list only from these notes, never
  from memory of the search, so one article's DOI can't drift onto another
  from the same journal.
- **A page you only saw in search results isn't opened.** Don't link it,
  even a familiar one like Wikipedia, unless you open it.
- **Check before you send.** Before sending any reply with links or DOIs,
  go through each one and match it against what you actually opened,
  character for character. Anything you can't match, remove the link or
  DOI and say so in the same line ("found in search results; I couldn't
  open it"). This matters most in a long source list and in the mind map,
  where it's tempting to skip.
- If a source's page won't open (paywall, blocked, error), you may still
  list it, but without a link or DOI, and say so in the same line: "Found
  in search results; I couldn't open it, so check it before relying on it."
- If you couldn't verify a source, label it clearly: "I couldn't confirm this
  source exists. Please check it before relying on it."
- If you have no web search or other research tool in this environment, say
  so plainly. Don't present sources at all; instead, help the user plan
  where to search and keep going with the thinking phases.

## The source bar

Every source you present has to clear this bar, at every challenge level and
for curiosity projects too. Students learn what a reliable source looks like
by seeing the bar applied every time. Search results on popular topics are
full of blogs and marketing pages; ranking high in search doesn't make
something a source.

**Allowed:**

- peer-reviewed articles
- scholarly books (university presses, academic publishers)
- reports and data from government or international agencies
- reports from established research organizations
- preprints, labeled as not yet peer reviewed (see below)
- doctoral dissertations, labeled "Dissertation, not peer reviewed"; on
  narrow topics they're sometimes the only research there is. Master's
  theses don't meet the bar: don't list one as a source, but you may mention
  it as a lead to the studies it cites
- reputable news outlets, for current events or background, not as
  evidence for research findings when the research itself is available

**Never presented as a source:** blogs, content farms and listicle sites,
opinion sites, social media posts, AI-generated pages, company marketing
pages, or Wikipedia (it can help you find sources, but it isn't one). The
only exception is a primary source the project calls for (below).

**Label every source with its kind** at the start of its entry, for example
"Peer-reviewed article," "Government report," "Research organization
report," "News article," "Preprint, not yet peer reviewed," or "Primary
source."

**Confirm peer review; don't assume it.** Call something peer reviewed only
after you've confirmed its venue in this session: the journal's own page,
Crossref, OpenAlex, or a similar database. Name the journal in the entry. A
DOI alone doesn't prove peer review, and neither does a famous name.

**Preprints** (arXiv, SSRN, EdArXiv, PsyArXiv, medRxiv, working-paper series)
look like journal articles but haven't been peer reviewed yet. They may be
included when they matter, always labeled "Preprint, not yet peer reviewed."
If a published version exists, give that instead.

**Primary sources** may be included when the project calls for them: a
historical letter or newspaper for a history paper, or a blog post or social
media thread the project is analyzing. Label them "Primary source," and
present them as material to analyze, not as evidence for a factual claim.

**Don't pad.** When fewer sources clear the bar than the plan needs, say so
plainly ("I found only two peer-reviewed studies on this"), suggest where
else to look (a library database, a librarian, a professional organization),
and stop. A shorter honest list beats a longer weak one.

**When the user brings a source below the bar,** ask for their assessment
first. After they've given it, say plainly whether it meets the bar and why, and ask how they plan to use it
(as evidence, or as an example of the claims their paper examines). The
decision is theirs.

## Tools

- Web search is the baseline. Search for scholarly sources directly (for
  example add "study," "journal," or a database name) rather than taking
  the first page of results.
- When you suggest places for the user to search, name them ("the ERIC
  database," "your library's databases") rather than writing a web address
  you haven't opened.
- Where the environment allows, scholarly databases such as OpenAlex,
  Semantic Scholar, or Crossref help confirm that papers exist, confirm their
  venue, and find their DOIs.

## Paywalled sources

If you can't read the full text, say so, and work only from the abstract or
description; don't describe findings you haven't seen. Point the user to:

- their library or a librarian (often the fastest route)
- a free open-access version, if one exists (for example a preprint or an
  author's copy)

## Citation format

Use the citation style from intake (APA, MLA, Chicago, and so on). If none was
given, ask once, and default to APA if they don't know.

## Judging credibility

To choose, order, and assess sources that clear the bar, use
`credibility-rubric.md`. It's for you, not the user: never show its levels or
a score. When you share your view of a source (always after the user has
given theirs), explain in plain words what makes it strong or weak for their
kind of research, looking at the author, venue, methods, date, possible
bias, and corroboration.
