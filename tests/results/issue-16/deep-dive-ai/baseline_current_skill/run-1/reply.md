## Sources

**Peer-reviewed research.** All six are in journals I confirmed through Crossref and OpenAlex, so you have more than the 3 peer-reviewed sources your assignment requires.

- **Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*). Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), Article e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
  A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students. While they had GPT-4, grades went up: 48% with a standard ChatGPT-style tool and 127% with a tutor version built to protect learning. Once access was taken away, the standard-tool group scored 17% lower than students who never had access. The tutor version largely avoided that drop.

- **Peer-reviewed article** (*Interactive Learning Environments*). Yang, T.-C., Hsu, Y.-C., & Wu, J.-Y. (2025). The effectiveness of ChatGPT in assisting high school students in programming learning: Evidence from a quasi-experimental research. *Interactive Learning Environments, 33*(6), 3726–3743. https://doi.org/10.1080/10494820.2025.2450659
  153 high school programming students. Those using ChatGPT reported lower flow, lower self-efficacy and lower learning achievement than students taught with conventional methods. In interviews, students said it fell short of what they expected.

- **Peer-reviewed article** (*ZDM – Mathematics Education*). Bulut, N., & Borromeo Ferri, R. (2026). AI-powered learning: Exploring secondary school students' use of ChatGPT for solving mathematical modelling problems. *ZDM – Mathematics Education, 58*(5), 993–1009. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11858-026-01803-6
  An exploratory study that looked at the chat logs of ten 10th-graders working in pairs. Students rarely checked or interpreted what ChatGPT gave them. The authors describe "uncritical acceptance and limited verification," though the tool did reduce workload.

- **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*). Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
  A randomized experiment with 117 university students doing a writing task. Some had ChatGPT, some a human expert, some writing-analytics tools and some nothing. The ChatGPT group improved their essay scores the most, but their knowledge gain and transfer were no better than the other groups'.

- **Peer-reviewed systematic review** (*Smart Learning Environments*). Zhai, C., Wibowo, S., & Li, L. D. (2024). The effects of over-reliance on AI dialogue systems on students' cognitive abilities: A systematic review. *Smart Learning Environments, 11*(1), Article 28. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40561-024-00316-7
  Reviews 14 studies. It finds that over-reliance on AI chatbots is linked to weaker decision-making, critical thinking and analytical reasoning, because users prefer fast shortcuts.

- **Peer-reviewed article** (*Societies*). Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), Article 6. https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006
  A survey-and-interview study of 666 people of different ages, so not only students. People who used AI more often scored lower on critical thinking, linked to "cognitive offloading" (letting the tool do the thinking). Younger participants relied on AI more. The design shows a correlation, not cause and effect.

**Preprints, not yet peer reviewed**

- **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (Version 2) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.09047
  Two lab experiments and a field study. Students who used the chatbot *in place of* learning tasks (for example, generating solutions) covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used it *alongside* learning (for example, asking for explanations) understood more. Overall, students tended to use it the first way. *Why it's here:* it is the closest match to your sub-question about how students use chatbots. Peer reviewers haven't checked it yet, and the abstract doesn't say how old the participants were.

- **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Vanzo, A., Pal Chowdhury, S., & Sachan, M. (2024). *GPT-4 as a homework tutor can improve student engagement and learning outcomes* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.15981
  A randomized trial in four high school English-as-a-second-language classes. GPT-4 homework sessions, set up to act as a tutor, replaced regular homework, and students showed learning gains, especially in grammar. *Why it's here:* it's one of the few high school homework trials. It tests a specially designed tool, not ordinary ChatGPT, and the abstract gives no sample size.

**Research organization reports**

- **Research organization report.** Sidoti, O., Park, E., & Gottfried, J. (2025, January 15). *About a quarter of U.S. teens have used ChatGPT for schoolwork – double the share in 2023*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork-double-the-share-in-2023/
  A national survey of 1,391 teens (fall 2024). 26% used ChatGPT for schoolwork. 54% said using it to research topics is acceptable, but only 18% said the same for writing essays. *Why it's here:* it shows how many teens use chatbots and what they think is fair, not whether chatbots help them learn.

- **Research organization report.** McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
  A survey of 1,458 teens (fall 2025). 54% have used chatbots for schoolwork, and 10% for all or most of it. 59% say students at their school use AI to cheat at least somewhat often. *Why it's here:* same as above. It's useful for current usage numbers, but everything in it is self-reported.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plentiful:** studies showing a gap between doing better *with* the tool and learning *without* it (Bastani, Fan), and evidence that over-reliance goes with weaker thinking (Zhai, Gerlich).
- **Thin:** studies that directly compare "checks work" students with "gets answers" students in high school. Bastani compares tool *designs*, not student habits. Lehmann compares *uses*, but it's a preprint and I couldn't confirm the participants' ages.
- **Mostly missing:** long-term studies, since most run for a session or a semester. Several strong studies are also on university students, not high schoolers.
- **A warning:** a widely shared 2025 meta-analysis of ChatGPT and learning (Wang & Fan, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*) kept appearing in my searches. Crossref lists it as **retracted**, so I left it out, and you shouldn't cite it even though news sites still describe it.

## Questions these results raise

- None of these studies sorts students by intent the way your position does. Most compare tools or assign how students use them, so "kids who check their work" may be hard to find as a group in the evidence.
- Bastani's guarded tutor helped no matter what each student intended. That points to a third factor besides student habits: how the tool is built.
