## Sources

**Peer-reviewed research** (all six count toward your "at least 3 peer-reviewed" requirement)

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*). Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   A randomized field experiment with nearly 1,000 students in grades 9–11 at a high school in Turkey. Some students practiced with a plain ChatGPT-style tool, some with a tutor version that gave hints instead of answers, and some with no AI. The plain-tool group scored 17% worse than the no-AI group on a later exam taken without AI. The tutor group scored about the same as the no-AI group. In the chat logs, the plain-tool students' most common message was "What is the answer?" A correction was published later, but it only fixed an author's affiliation.

2. **Peer-reviewed systematic review and meta-analysis** (*Computers & Education*). Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   Pools 69 experimental studies, mostly with university students. ChatGPT improved academic performance overall and reduced mental effort. The authors caution that weak methods and the way results were tested call for "cautious interpretation." I read only the abstract.

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Educational Psychology Review*). Fütterer, T., Bardach, L., Kuhn, J., Keller, S. D., & Gerjets, P. (2026). Enhancing school students' self-regulated learning through generative AI support: A randomized controlled trial. *Educational Psychology Review, 38*(1), 42. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-026-10133-8
   371 students in grades 7–9 used one of two specially designed AI supports or standard ChatGPT, over six class sessions. Neither designed version beat standard ChatGPT on subject knowledge, effort, or strategy use. I read only the abstract.

4. **Peer-reviewed conference paper** (ACL 2025 proceedings). Vanzo, A., Pal Chowdhury, S., & Sachan, M. (2025). GPT-4 as a homework tutor can improve student engagement and learning outcomes. In *Proceedings of the 63rd Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (Volume 1: Long Papers)* (pp. 31119–31136). https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.acl-long.1502
   A randomized trial in four high school English-as-a-second-language classes that swapped regular homework for interactive GPT-4 homework sessions. Students were more satisfied, learning didn't suffer, and one group learned significantly more. I read only the abstract.

5. **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*). Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2024). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   A randomized experiment with 117 university students on a writing task. It compared ChatGPT, a human expert, writing analytics tools, and no help, and looked at motivation, how students managed their own learning, and performance. The title names the risk it found. Note these are college students, not high schoolers. I read only the abstract.

6. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Frontiers in Psychology*). Zhu, Q., Li, X., Dong, Y., Chang, P., & Fan, M. (2026). Not all cognitive offloading is equal: Distinguishing dependent and autonomous offloading to generative AI. *Frontiers in Psychology, 17*, 1878629. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1878629
   A survey of 589 university students and early-career workers, taken at three points in time. Handing your core thinking over to AI was linked to worse self-reported thinking outcomes. Using AI as a scaffold while doing the thinking yourself was linked to better ones. It measures what people *report*, not tested learning. I read only the abstract.

**Preprints, not yet peer reviewed**

7. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (Version 2) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   Why it's here: it compares ways of *using* AI head-on, which is close to your sub-question 2. Watch for: it's about college students learning to code, and it hasn't been through peer review. Two lab experiments found no overall effect on learning. Students who used AI to generate solutions covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who asked it for explanations understood more. AI also widened the gap between students who started with more knowledge and those who started with less.

8. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Abdelghani, R., Kaiser, P., & Murayama, K. (2026). *Regulating the AI tutor: Intentions, help-seeking, and self-regulated learning in adolescent GenAI use* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2606.08568
   Why it's here: it's one of the few studies that reads actual chat logs from ninth graders. Watch for: it's labeled a work in progress, the chat coding was done by AI, and there was no comparison group. 98 German ninth graders used an AI math tutor before an exam. Most said beforehand that they wanted scaffolded help, but their chats were mostly requests for answers, with almost no checking of their own work. Their scores went down from pretest to posttest.

**Research organization reports**

9. **Research organization report (survey).** McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
   Why it's here: it shows how many U.S. teens use chatbots and what for. Watch for: it measures use and opinions, not whether anyone learned anything. Of 1,458 teens surveyed in fall 2025, 54% had used chatbots for schoolwork and 10% did all or most of their schoolwork with them.

10. **Research organization report (survey).** Schwartz, H. L., & Diliberti, M. K. (2026). *More students use AI for homework, and more believe it harms critical thinking: Selected findings from the American Youth Panel* (RR-A4742-1). RAND Corporation. https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4742-1.html
   Why it's here: it's recent national data on homework specifically. Watch for: it surveys ages 12–29 together and reports beliefs, not measured learning. Homework use rose from 48% to 62% during 2025. It recommends separating "cognitive offloading" (AI does the mental work) from "cognitive augmentation" (AI pushes students to work harder). The report page doesn't break out high school numbers, and I didn't check its PDF.

## Coverage report

- **Where I searched:** about 20 searches of the OpenAlex scholarly database, Crossref to confirm journals and citation details, and a few web searches for reports. I covered both sub-questions using your keywords plus terms like "cognitive offloading," "help-seeking," and "secondary school."
- **Plentiful:** studies of college students, and surveys of how much teens use AI.
- **Thin:** studies of high schoolers that measure actual learning. Bastani, Fütterer, Vanzo, and Abdelghani are the main ones. Almost none study homework done at home; most happen in class or in a lab. I didn't find a study that classifies students by whether they *chose* to check their work or to get answers and then compares how much they learned. Bastani comes closest, but there the difference came from how the tool was built, not from what students chose.
- **One warning:** a widely cited 2025 meta-analysis of ChatGPT and learning (Wang & Fan, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*) was **retracted in 2026**. Don't use it if you come across it.
- **Couldn't reach:** the OECD report page blocked me, and most articles above I read only as abstracts.

## Questions these results raise

- Several of the best studies change the *tool* (hints instead of answers) rather than looking at what students *choose*. That bears on whether your position is about students or about tool design.
- Lehmann's prior-knowledge finding hints that the "checkers" might simply be students who already know more.
- In both Bastani and the Abdelghani preprint, students misjudged how they were using the tool or how much they learned.
