## Sources

**Peer-reviewed research**

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey. Students using a standard ChatGPT-style tool did better during practice but scored 17% lower than the control group once the tool was taken away. A version with built-in tutoring safeguards "largely mitigated" that drop. *A correction was published in August 2025. I didn't read it.*

2. **Peer-reviewed article.** Fütterer, T., Bardach, L., Kuhn, J., Keller, S. D., & Gerjets, P. (2026). Enhancing school students' self-regulated learning through generative AI support: A randomized controlled trial. *Educational Psychology Review, 38*(1), Article 42. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-026-10133-8
   371 students in grades 7–9 were randomly assigned to one of two AI setups designed to support self-regulated learning, or to plain ChatGPT. Neither designed setup beat plain ChatGPT on knowledge or effort. Exploratory analyses found that students who engaged more meaningfully with the AI kept their interest longer.

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Klarin, J., Hoff, E., Larsson, A., & Daukantaitė, D. (2024). Adolescents' use and perceived usefulness of generative AI for schoolwork: Exploring their relationships with executive functioning and academic achievement. *Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, 7*, Article 1415782. https://doi.org/10.3389/frai.2024.1415782
   Two Swedish surveys of teens with average ages of 14 and 17. Teens who struggled more with planning and self-control found AI more useful for completing assignments. In the younger group, achievement wasn't significantly linked to AI use. The surveys were done early in the chatbot era.

4. **Peer-reviewed article (meta-analysis).** Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, Article 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   Pools 69 experiments and finds that ChatGPT improved academic performance and reduced mental effort. The authors warn about weak methods in the studies they pooled. Most of those studies were at the university level. *Paywalled, so I read only the abstract.*

**Working papers and preprints (not peer reviewed)**

5. **Working paper, not peer reviewed.** Strömberg, D., Lei, V., & Wu, Y. (2026). *The generative AI learning penalty: Evidence from Chinese secondary education* (CEPR Discussion Paper No. 21577). Centre for Economic Policy Research. https://ideas.repec.org/p/cpr/ceprdp/21577.html (full text: https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f240545.pdf)
   Follows 26,811 students in grades 7–12 over 30 months. Adopting AI raised homework scores by 18% but lowered monthly exam scores by 20%. The losses were concentrated in the roughly 80% of users whose homework times looked like outsourcing. AI users who spent normal amounts of time on homework had small losses.
   *Why it's here: it's the largest real-world study of high schoolers I found. Watch for changes if it gets peer reviewed.*

6. **Preprint, not peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2024). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   Two lab experiments found no overall effect on learning. Exploratory analyses found that students who used AI to generate solutions covered more topics but understood each one less well. Students who asked it for explanations understood more.
   *Why it's here: it compares different ways of using AI directly. The split between those two ways comes from exploratory analysis, not the main test.*

7. **Preprint, not peer reviewed.** Huntington-Klein, N. (2026). *Little impact of ChatGPT on high school test scores.* arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08812
   Uses the summer drop in ChatGPT traffic to find US areas with heavy school use of AI. Test scores in those areas weren't significantly different from other areas.
   *Why it's here: it looks at unsupervised use across the US. It measures area averages, not individual students.*

8. **Preprint, not peer reviewed.** Bartoš, F., Martinková, P., & Wagenmakers, E.-J. (2025). *Adjusting for publication bias reveals no evidence for the effect of ChatGPT on students' learning performance, learning perception, and higher-order thinking.* PsyArXiv. https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/8vs32_v1
   Re-analyzes a widely cited meta-analysis that reported big benefits. After correcting for unpublished null results, the authors say the evidence for benefits disappears. That meta-analysis, by Wang and Fan, was [retracted in April 2026](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-026-07310-z).
   *Why it's here: it's a check on how much to trust pooled "ChatGPT helps" numbers.*

**Reports**

9. **Research organization report (World Bank working paper).** De Simone, M. E., Tiberti, F. H., Barron Rodriguez, M. R., Manolio, F. A., Mosuro, W., & Dikoru, E. J. (2025). *From chalkboards to chatbots: Evaluating the impact of generative AI on learning outcomes in Nigeria* (Policy Research Working Paper No. 11125). World Bank. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:11125
   A randomized trial of a six-week, teacher-supervised AI tutoring program for secondary students. English scores rose by 0.23 standard deviations.
   *Why it's here: it's a high school trial with a positive result. It tested a structured program, not homework done alone.*

10. **Research organization report (survey).** McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI.* Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
    A fall 2025 survey of 1,458 US teens. 54% had used chatbots for schoolwork help, and 1 in 10 do all or most of their schoolwork with them.
    *Why it's here: it shows how common the behavior is. It doesn't measure learning.*
