**Peer-reviewed studies with high school or middle school students**

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*). Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   This was a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey. During practice, students with a plain ChatGPT-style tutor scored 48% higher, and students with a tutor built with learning safeguards scored 127% higher. Once the AI was taken away, the plain-tutor group scored 17% lower than students who never had it. The safeguarded tutor largely avoided that drop. PNAS published a correction to this paper in August 2025 (https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2518204122). The site blocked me, so I couldn't read what the correction changed.

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Educational Psychology Review*). Fütterer, T., Bardach, L., Kuhn, J., Keller, S. D., & Gerjets, P. (2026). Enhancing school students' self-regulated learning through generative AI support: A randomized controlled trial. *Educational Psychology Review, 38*(1), Article 42. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-026-10133-8
   371 students in grades 7–9 were randomly assigned to two AI setups designed to support how they learn, or to standard ChatGPT. Neither designed setup beat standard ChatGPT on knowledge, effort, or study-strategy use. Exploratory analyses suggested that students who engaged more meaningfully with the AI kept their interest longer.

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*International Journal of Science Education*). Aptyka, H., Großschedl, J., & Hartelt, T. (2025). Bugbear or surefire success? Secondary school students' conceptual learning about evolution with ChatGPT. *International Journal of Science Education, 48*(15), 2376–2395. https://doi.org/10.1080/09500693.2025.2524083
   241 upper secondary students learned about evolution using either ChatGPT or ordinary internet searches. ChatGPT gave only a "limited enhancement" to their understanding. Students' attitudes toward AI-supported learning still became more positive, and the authors flag that as a risk.

**Peer-reviewed research mostly on university students**

4. **Peer-reviewed article, systematic review and meta-analysis** (*Computers & Education*). Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   This review pooled 69 experiments, mostly at university level. Overall, ChatGPT improved academic performance and reduced mental effort. The authors warn that many of the studies may have measured the quality of ChatGPT's output rather than what students actually learned.

5. **Peer-reviewed article, meta-analysis** (*Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*). Wu, X., Zhu, P., Zhang, J., Yin, M., & Wang, Y. (2026). ChatGPT's impact on student learning outcomes: A meta-analysis of 35 experimental studies. *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 13*(1), Article 684. https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-026-07019-z
   This review pooled 35 studies with 4,193 participants and found a moderate positive effect (g = 0.670). Educational level did not significantly change the effect.

6. **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*). Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2024). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   117 university students revised essays with help from ChatGPT, a human expert, a writing-analytics tool, or nothing. The ChatGPT group improved their essay scores most, but they gained no more knowledge and no more ability to transfer it than the other groups.

7. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers in Human Behavior*). Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, 108386. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386
   91 university students researched a science issue using either ChatGPT or Google. The ChatGPT users felt less mental strain but produced weaker reasoning and arguments.

**Preprints, not yet peer reviewed**

8. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed** (arXiv). Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047v2) [Preprint]. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   Why it's here: it's the closest study to your exact question about how students use the tool. What to watch for: it isn't peer reviewed yet, and the participants were German and Dutch university students learning Python, not high schoolers. Two lab experiments found no overall effect. In exploratory analyses, students who used the AI to generate solutions covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who asked it for explanations understood more. The AI also widened the gap between students who started with low and high prior knowledge.

9. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed** (arXiv). Vanzo, A., Pal Chowdhury, S., & Sachan, M. (2024). *GPT-4 as a homework tutor can improve student engagement and learning outcomes* (arXiv:2409.15981) [Preprint]. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15981
   Why it's here: it's a randomized trial that actually used high school homework. What to watch for: the sample was only four classes, the subject was English as a second language, and the tutor was custom-built rather than plain ChatGPT. Treatment students had greater grammar gains and were more engaged.

## Questions the results raise

- Bastani and Lehmann suggest that what students *do* with the tool matters, which fits your idea. Fütterer found that a tool designed to guide students did no better than plain ChatGPT, so the design of the tool may not be enough on its own.
- The positive meta-analyses mostly measure performance with the AI available or right afterward. Bastani's result appeared only after the AI was taken away. When the test happens may matter as much as how the AI was used.
- Lehmann found that the AI widened gaps between stronger and weaker students. That raises the question of which students tend to check their work in the first place.
