### Peer-reviewed studies

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*). Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students. They had either a standard ChatGPT-style tool, a "tutor" version that gave teacher-designed hints instead of answers, or no AI. Both AI groups did better during practice. Once the AI was taken away, the standard-tool group scored 17% lower than students who never had it, and the hint-based tutor mostly avoided that drop. *I read the abstract only; the full text is paywalled. A correction was published in August 2025, and I haven't read it.*

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Educational Psychology Review*). Fütterer, T., Bardach, L., Kuhn, J., Keller, S. D., & Gerjets, P. (2026). Enhancing school students' self-regulated learning through generative AI support: A randomized controlled trial. *Educational Psychology Review, 38*(1), Article 42. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-026-10133-8
   371 students in grades 7–9 used either standard ChatGPT or one of two specially designed AI versions over six physics or English lessons. The designed versions showed no significant advantage on knowledge, effort, or strategy use. Exploratory results suggested that students who engaged more meaningfully with the AI kept their interest longer.

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers & Education*). Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   This pools 69 experiments from 2022–2024, mostly at universities. It finds ChatGPT improved academic performance and reduced mental effort. The authors warn that many of the studies were too small, and that some tests may have measured the quality of ChatGPT's output rather than what students learned.

4. **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*). Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   A lab experiment with 117 university students on a writing task. The ChatGPT group improved their essays the most, but they gained no more knowledge or transfer than the other groups. The authors raise the concern that students may stop monitoring their own thinking.

5. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Social Sciences & Humanities Open*). Barcaui, A. (2025). ChatGPT as a cognitive crutch: Evidence from a randomized controlled trial on knowledge retention. *Social Sciences & Humanities Open, 12*, 102287. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssaho.2025.102287
   120 undergraduates studied either with ChatGPT or without AI. On a surprise test 45 days later, the ChatGPT group scored 57.5% and the traditional group 68.5%. The ChatGPT use was unrestricted, not a particular way of using it.

6. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers in Human Behavior*). Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, 108386. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386
   91 university students researched a science question using either ChatGPT or Google. The ChatGPT users felt less mental strain but produced weaker reasoning and arguments.

### Preprints (not yet peer reviewed)

7. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed** (arXiv). Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047v2) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   Two pre-registered lab experiments found no overall effect of chatbots on learning. Exploratory analyses and a field study found the effect depended on use: students who had it generate solutions covered more topics but understood each one less, and students who asked for explanations understood more. These are university students. The usage findings are labeled exploratory, which means weaker evidence than the main pre-registered test.

8. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed** (PsyArXiv). Bartoš, F., Martinková, P., & Wagenmakers, E.-J. (2025). *Adjusting for publication bias reveals no evidence for the effect of ChatGPT on students' learning performance, learning perception, and higher-order thinking* [Preprint]. PsyArXiv. https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/8vs32_v1
   This reanalyzes a large meta-analysis that reported big learning gains from ChatGPT. Once it adjusts for the tendency of positive results to get published more often, the benefit disappears. It's here because it's a statistical critique of how this research gets summarized, not a new study of students.

### Research organization reports

9. **Research organization report** (RAND). Schwartz, H. L., & Diliberti, M. K. (2026). *More students use AI for homework, and more believe it harms critical thinking: Selected findings from the American Youth Panel* (Report No. RR-A4742-1). RAND Corporation. https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4742-1.html
   A December 2025 survey of 1,214 students aged 12–29. Homework use of AI rose from 48% to 62% since May 2025, and 67% said AI use harms critical thinking. 45% counted getting direct answers as cheating, while nearly 80% said using AI to understand an assignment isn't cheating. It's a survey of what students do and believe, not a measure of learning, and its age range goes well past high school. *I couldn't open the PDF, so I have no high-school-only figures.*

10. **Research organization report** (Pew Research Center). McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
    A survey of 1,458 U.S. teens aged 13–17 in fall 2025. 54% have used chatbots for schoolwork help, and one in ten do all or most of their schoolwork with them. 59% think cheating with AI happens regularly at their school. Like RAND, it describes use, not whether learning improves.

### Questions these results raise

- Several studies separate tools *designed* to give hints from open-ended chatbots, rather than separating *students* who check versus copy. That's a different line from the one your position draws.
- Most of the studies measure university students in labs, so it's an open question how far they carry over to teenagers doing homework at home.
- #3 and #8 disagree about how big the average effect is, and how a meta-analysis deals with publication bias shapes its conclusion.
