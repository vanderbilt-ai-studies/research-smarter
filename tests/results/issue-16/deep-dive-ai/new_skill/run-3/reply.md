**Peer-reviewed experiments with secondary-school students**

1. **Peer-reviewed article (PNAS).** Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), Article e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   This was a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Türkiye. They had either a plain ChatGPT-style tutor or one designed with learning safeguards. Grades rose while students had access. Once the tool was taken away, the plain-tutor group scored 17% lower than students who never had it, and the safeguarded version "largely mitigated" that drop. *I could only read the abstract; the publisher's page blocked me.*

2. **Peer-reviewed article (Educational Psychology Review).** Fütterer, T., Bardach, L., Kuhn, J., Keller, S. D., & Gerjets, P. (2026). Enhancing school students' self-regulated learning through generative AI support: A randomized controlled trial. *Educational Psychology Review, 38*(1), Article 42. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-026-10133-8
   371 students in grades 7–9 were randomly assigned to two specially designed AI tutors or to standard ChatGPT, over six physics or English lessons. Neither designed tutor beat standard ChatGPT on effort, subject knowledge, or strategy use. Exploratory analyses suggested that students who engaged more meaningfully with the AI fared differently. *Abstract only.*

3. **Peer-reviewed article (Interactive Learning Environments).** Yang, T.-C., Hsu, Y.-C., & Wu, J.--Y. (2025). The effectiveness of ChatGPT in assisting high school students in programming learning: Evidence from a quasi-experimental research. *Interactive Learning Environments, 33*(6), 3726–3743. https://doi.org/10.1080/10494820.2025.2450659
   According to search summaries, this is a study of 153 twelfth-grade students in programming. The ChatGPT group scored lower on achievement and self-efficacy than the group taught the usual way. *I couldn't open the article or its abstract, so check these details before relying on them.* It's quasi-experimental, meaning students weren't randomly assigned.

**Peer-reviewed meta-analyses (studies that pool many experiments)**

4. **Peer-reviewed article (Journal of Computer Assisted Learning).** Liu, Z., Zuo, H., & Lu, Y. (2025). The impact of ChatGPT on students' academic achievement: A meta-analysis. *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning, 41*(4), Article e70096. https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.70096
   This pools 37 studies from 2022–2025 and finds "a moderately positive impact" on achievement. It also breaks results down by education level, learning approach, and the role ChatGPT was given. *I saw only part of the abstract.* It mixes all education levels.

5. **Peer-reviewed article (Journal of Intelligence).** Zhao, Y., Yue, Y., Sun, Z., Jiang, Q., & Li, G. (2025). Does generative artificial intelligence improve students' higher-order thinking? A meta-analysis based on 29 experiments and quasi-experiments. *Journal of Intelligence, 13*(12), 160. https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence13120160
   It finds a moderate positive effect on higher-order thinking, strongest for problem-solving and weakest for creativity. Students with stronger self-regulation skills benefited more. The full text is free on PubMed Central.

**Peer-reviewed experiment, university students**

6. **Peer-reviewed article (British Journal of Educational Technology).** Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   This randomized experiment had 117 university students do a writing task with help from ChatGPT, a human expert, a writing tool, or nothing. The ChatGPT group wrote better essays but didn't gain significantly more knowledge, and it showed fewer self-monitoring behaviors. *Abstract only.* The participants were college students, not high schoolers.

**Preprints, not yet peer reviewed**

7. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047v2) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   Two pre-registered lab experiments plus a field study. There was no overall effect on learning. Students who substituted AI for their own work covered more topics but understood each one less, while students who used it as a complement understood more. The abstract doesn't give the participants' ages. Treat it as provisional until it's peer reviewed.

8. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Dai, X., Wen, Z., Jiang, J., Liu, H., & Zhang, Y. (2025). *How students use AI feedback matters: Experimental evidence on physics achievement and autonomy* (arXiv:2505.08672) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.08672
   Two five-week randomized trials with 387 high school physics students. The effects of required versus on-demand AI hints differed by achievement level, and some groups lost self-regulation. Watch for its small subgroups and lack of peer review.

**Research organization reports (surveys)**

9. **Research organization report.** Schwartz, H. L., & Diliberti, M. K. (2026). *More students use AI for homework, and more believe it harms critical thinking: Selected findings from the American Youth Panel* (Report No. RR-A4742-1). RAND Corporation. https://doi.org/10.7249/RRA4742-1
   A December 2025 national survey of 1,214 people aged 12–29. Homework use rose from 48% to 62% in seven months, and the most common use was getting better explanations of assignments (38%). 45% of students called getting direct answers cheating. *This is a survey of what students do and believe, not a measure of learning.* It was funded by the Gates Foundation.

10. **Research organization report.** McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
    A fall 2025 national survey of 1,458 U.S. teens. 54% had used chatbots for schoolwork help, and 10% do all or most of their schoolwork with them. *It's useful for how common the habit is, not for whether it helps.*
