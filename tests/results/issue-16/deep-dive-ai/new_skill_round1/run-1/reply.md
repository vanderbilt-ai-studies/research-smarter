## Sources

**Peer-reviewed articles** (I checked each journal and DOI in the Crossref database. Publisher sites blocked me, so I worked from abstracts only.)

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey. Some got a standard ChatGPT-style tool and some got a version built to tutor. With access, practice grades rose 48% and 127%. When access was taken away, the standard-tool group scored 17% lower than students who never had it. The tutor version largely avoided that drop.

2. **Peer-reviewed article.** Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   A randomized lab experiment with 117 **university** students writing essays. The ChatGPT group improved its essays the most, but its knowledge gain and transfer weren't significantly different from the other groups. These students also showed fewer self-monitoring behaviors.

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), 6. https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006
   A survey and interviews with 666 people of mixed ages. Frequent AI use correlated with lower critical-thinking scores, linked through cognitive offloading, and younger participants relied on AI more. *Watch for:* it shows correlation, not cause, and the participants aren't high schoolers specifically.

4. **Peer-reviewed article (systematic review and meta-analysis).** Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   I confirmed it exists and where it was published, but I couldn't open even its abstract. I'm not summarizing its findings because I haven't read them.

**Preprints and working papers** (not peer reviewed)

5. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (Version 2) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   Two pre-registered lab experiments and a field study. Overall, LLM access had no effect on learning. Students who had the LLM generate solutions covered more topics but understood each less. Students who asked it for explanations understood more. *Watch for:* the abstract doesn't say how old the participants were, and the findings changed between versions.

6. **Working paper from an international agency (not peer reviewed).** De Simone, M. E., Tiberti, F. H., Barron Rodriguez, M. R., Manolio, F. A., Mosuro, W., & Dikoru, E. J. (2025). *From chalkboards to chatbots: Evaluating the impact of generative AI on learning outcomes in Nigeria* (Policy Research Working Paper No. 11125). World Bank. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:11125
   A randomized trial of a six-week after-school program for first-year senior secondary students, using GPT-4-powered Copilot as an English tutor. English scores rose by 0.23 standard deviations, and gains were largest for girls and for students who started out stronger. *Watch for:* this was a structured program with teachers, not students doing homework on their own.

**Research organization and agency reports**

7. **Research organization report.** Schwartz, H. L., & Diliberti, M. K. (2026). *More students use AI for homework, and more believe it harms critical thinking: Selected findings from the American Youth Panel* (Report No. RR-A4742-1). RAND Corporation. https://doi.org/10.7249/RRA4742-1
   A survey of 1,214 people aged 12–29. The share using AI for homework rose from 48% to 62% during 2025. 67% agreed that more AI use will harm critical thinking. Except for getting direct homework answers, most didn't see their school uses of AI as cheating. *Watch for:* this measures use and opinions, not learning. It was funded by the Gates Foundation.

8. **Research organization report.** McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI.* Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
   A survey of 1,458 U.S. teens in fall 2025. 54% have used chatbots for schoolwork, about 1 in 10 do all or most of their schoolwork with them, and 59% say AI cheating happens at their school at least somewhat often. *Watch for:* also a survey of use, not of learning.

9. **International agency report (read through a summary).** OECD. (2026). *OECD digital education outlook 2026.* OECD Publishing. The OECD's page blocked me, so I read the [EU Digital Skills and Jobs Platform's summary](https://digital-skills-jobs.europa.eu/en/latest/news/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026-how-generative-ai-can-support-learning-when-used) instead. According to that summary, general-purpose AI improves students' work, but the advantage "tend[s] to disappear—or even reverse" when access is removed, while tools designed for teaching show more lasting gains. Cite the OECD report itself, not the summary, once you've read it.

10. **News article (secondhand report of a working paper).** Hussein, M. (2026, September 2). Faster homework, poor exam results: What AI is doing to students' learning. *Al Jazeera.* https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/9/2/faster-homework-poor-exam-results-what-ai-is-doing-to-students-learning
