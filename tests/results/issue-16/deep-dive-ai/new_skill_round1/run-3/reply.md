## Sources

**Peer-reviewed research.** These five meet your requirement of at least three peer-reviewed sources.

1. **Peer-reviewed article (*PNAS*).** Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students. While they had GPT-4, students did better on practice problems: grades rose 48% with a ChatGPT-style tool and 127% with a "tutor" version built to protect learning. Once access was removed, students who had used the ChatGPT-style tool scored 17% lower than students who never had it. The tutor version largely avoided that drop. The journal issued a correction (https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2518204122), and I couldn't read what it changed. I read the abstract only, because the publisher blocked access.

2. **Peer-reviewed article (*British Journal of Educational Technology*).** Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   A randomized experiment with 117 university students on a writing task. The ChatGPT group improved their essay scores the most, but their knowledge gain and transfer were no different from the other groups. The authors warn that ChatGPT may encourage dependence on the tool and "metacognitive laziness." The participants are college students, not high schoolers. I read the abstract only.

3. **Peer-reviewed systematic review (*Smart Learning Environments*).** Zhai, C., Wibowo, S., & Li, L. D. (2024). The effects of over-reliance on AI dialogue systems on students' cognitive abilities: A systematic review. *Smart Learning Environments, 11*, Article 28. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40561-024-00316-7
   A review of 14 articles. It finds that over-relying on AI dialogue systems affects decision-making, critical thinking, and analytical reasoning, because students come to prefer fast shortcuts. It's a small review. I read the abstract only.

4. **Peer-reviewed meta-analysis (*Computers & Education*).** Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   Pools 69 experimental studies from 2022–2024. It reports that ChatGPT improves academic performance and higher-order thinking and reduces mental effort. I read the abstract only from a library-catalog copy (BibBase), because the publisher page didn't load.

5. **Peer-reviewed article (*Computers & Education*).** Lawson, A. P., Martella, A. M., Weidlich, J., Mulders, M., & Buchner, J. (2026). Color me confounded: A critical analysis of media comparisons on ChatGPT in education. *Computers & Education, 254*, 105701. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2026.105701
   A critique of the experiments inside Deng et al. (2025) and a second meta-analysis. It finds that many of those studies didn't keep conditions comparable (teaching method, practice, time), so the reported benefits can't be cleanly credited to ChatGPT. My summary comes from the abstract of the preprint version, not the journal page.

**Working papers and preprints (not peer reviewed)**

6. **Working paper, not peer reviewed (CEPR).** Strömberg, D., Lei, V., & Wu, Y. (2026). *The generative AI learning penalty: Evidence from Chinese secondary education* (CEPR Discussion Paper No. 21577). Centre for Economic Policy Research. https://ideas.repec.org/p/cpr/ceprdp/21577.html
   Follows 26,811 students in grades 7–12 for 30 months. After students started using AI, their homework scores rose 18% and their monthly exam scores fell 20% within six months. About 80% of AI users had very short homework times paired with high homework scores, which the authors read as "homework outsourcing," and that group had the largest losses. *What to watch for:* this is a large, recent observational study, not an experiment. The use patterns are inferred from homework time, not observed directly. I read the abstract only.

7. **Research organization working paper, not peer reviewed (World Bank).** De Simone, M. E., Tiberti, F. H., Barron Rodriguez, M. R., Manolio, F. A., Mosuro, W., & Dikoru, E. J. (2025). *From chalkboards to chatbots: Evaluating the impact of generative AI on learning outcomes in Nigeria* (Policy Research Working Paper No. 11125). World Bank. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:11125
   A randomized trial. First-year senior secondary students used Microsoft Copilot (GPT-4) in a six-week after-school English program with teacher guidance. The effect on English was 0.23 standard deviations, and the gains were largest for girls and for students who started out stronger. *What to watch for:* this was a structured program, not unsupervised homework. I read the abstract only.

8. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed (arXiv).** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.09047
   One field study and two lab experiments with university coding students. Using an LLM to generate solutions led students to cover more topics but understand each one less. Asking it for explanations increased understanding. Overall learning showed no net effect. *What to watch for:* the participants are university students, not high schoolers, and I found no journal version.

9. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed (arXiv).** Vanzo, A., Pal Chowdhury, S., & Sachan, M. (2024). *GPT-4 as a homework tutor can improve student engagement and learning outcomes*. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15981
   A small randomized trial with 75 students in an Italian high school, using a GPT-4 English homework tutor. Students found the homework more engaging. The overall learning-gain difference was not statistically significant (d = 0.251, p = 0.314), even though the abstract claims "significant improvements." *What to watch for:* the small sample, one teacher, and the gap between the abstract and the results.

**Survey data**

10. **Research organization report (Pew Research Center).** McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/

## Questions the results raise

- Whether "checking your work" is the same thing as "asking for explanations." Your position depends on that, and only the second has been studied.
- Whether the *tool's design* (the guardrails in Bastani, the teacher guidance in the World Bank study) matters as much as the *student's choice* of how to use it.
- Whether results from college students carry over to high schoolers.
