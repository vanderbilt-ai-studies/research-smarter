## Sources

**Peer-reviewed articles** (these seven count toward your three-peer-reviewed minimum)

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*). Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   A randomized field experiment with nearly 1,000 students in grades 9–11 at one high school in Turkey. Students practiced with nothing, with a standard GPT-4 chat ("GPT Base"), or with a version told to give hints instead of answers ("GPT Tutor"). Both AI groups scored higher during practice. On the exam without AI, the GPT Base group scored about 17% lower than students who never had it, and the GPT Tutor group scored about the same as them. The free version is on PubMed Central. It notes a correction from August 2025 that I didn't read.

2. **Peer-reviewed article, meta-analysis** (*Computers & Education*). Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   According to search results, it pools 69 experimental studies from 2022–2024 and finds better academic performance with ChatGPT than without, but no change in self-efficacy. **I couldn't open the article or its abstract** (the publisher blocked me). I confirmed the citation through Crossref, but the summary comes from search results.

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*). Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   A randomized experiment with 117 university students on a writing task. They had help from ChatGPT, a human expert, writing-analytics tools, or nothing. The ChatGPT group improved their essay scores most, but their knowledge gain and transfer weren't significantly different from the others. Their self-regulated learning steps also differed. I read the abstract only.

4. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers in Human Behavior*). Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, 108386. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386
   91 college students researched a question using either ChatGPT-3.5 or Google. The ChatGPT group reported less mental effort but gave weaker reasoning and justifications. My summary comes from the authors' university page, not the article itself.

5. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Societies*). Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), 6. https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006
   A survey-and-interview study of 666 people of mixed ages, not just students. Heavier AI use went with lower critical-thinking scores, linked through "cognitive offloading" (letting the tool do the thinking). Younger participants relied on AI more. This is correlational, so it can't show cause.

6. **Peer-reviewed article** (*LUMAT*, which states on its own site that it's peer reviewed). Bråskén, M., Berts, K.-E., Wallin, S., & Frilund, S. (2025). Students' use of ChatGPT in an algebra class: A case study of prompts and attitudes. *LUMAT: International Journal on Math, Science and Technology Education, 13*(1), 16. https://doi.org/10.31129/LUMAT.13.1.2877
   A case study of 14 upper-secondary students in a 15-week Finnish algebra course, based on the prompts they actually typed. Students found ChatGPT easy and helpful, but very few prompts showed goal-setting or reflection. The sample is very small, and the study describes what students did rather than measuring learning.

**Preprints, not yet peer reviewed**

7. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed** (arXiv). Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047v2). https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   University students, in two lab experiments plus a field study of programming courses. LLM access had no overall effect on learning in the lab. Asking the AI for solutions went with covering more topics but understanding each less. Asking it for explanations went with deeper understanding. The authors call that usage analysis exploratory. It's here because it measures usage patterns directly, but it hasn't been through peer review.

8. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed** (arXiv). Abdelghani, R., Kaiser, P., & Murayama, K. (2026). *Changes in help-seeking strategies predict unaided performance during AI-based mathematical learning* (arXiv:2606.28472). https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28472
   About 112 ninth graders (ages 14–15) at three German schools used an AI tutor for one math session. Their requests were coded as understanding-oriented or as answer- and verification-seeking. Students whose requests shifted toward understanding during the session did better on a test without AI afterward. It's very recent and covers only one session. Some of the sample figures came to me second-hand, so check them in the paper.

**Survey reports from research organizations**

9. **Research organization report** (Pew Research Center). McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
   A national survey of 1,458 U.S. teens aged 13–17, taken in fall 2025. 54% have used chatbots for schoolwork, and 1 in 10 say they do all or most of their schoolwork with chatbot help. This tells you how many teens use chatbots and what for. It doesn't measure whether they learn, and it doesn't separate answer-getting from checking.

10. **Research organization report** (RAND). Schwartz, H. L., & Diliberti, M. K. (2026). *More students use AI for homework, and more believe it harms critical thinking: Selected findings from the American Youth Panel* (Report No. RR-A4742-1). RAND Corporation. https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4742-1.html
   A survey of 1,214 people aged 12–29 in December 2025. Use of AI for homework rose from 48% to 62% over 2025, and 67% agreed that more AI use will harm critical thinking. These are students' opinions, not measured learning. The ages go well past high school, and the Gates Foundation funded it.

## Questions the results raise

- Several studies count "verification-seeking" (asking the AI whether an answer is right) together with answer-seeking, not with understanding. That bears on what "checking your work" means in your position.
- Bastani compares two *tools*, not two kinds of students. Whether the tool's design or the student's own habits is what matters is an open question in this research.
- Students' prior knowledge keeps coming up as a factor that changes the results.
