## Sources

**Peer-reviewed research** (6 sources, which covers your "at least 3 peer-reviewed" requirement)

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students. One group got a standard ChatGPT-style tutor and another got a tutor with built-in safeguards. With access, grades went up by 48% and 127%. When access was taken away, the standard-tutor group scored 17% lower than students who never had AI, and the safeguards "largely mitigated" that drop. *A correction was published in August 2025, so read the corrected version.*

2. **Peer-reviewed conference paper.** Vanzo, A., Pal Chowdhury, S., & Sachan, M. (2025). GPT-4 as a homework tutor can improve student engagement and learning outcomes. In *Proceedings of the 63rd Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (Volume 1: Long Papers)* (pp. 31119–31136). Association for Computational Linguistics. https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.acl-long.1502
   A randomized trial in four high school English-as-a-second-language classes. GPT-4 ran interactive homework sessions with follow-up questions in place of regular homework. The authors report greater grammar gains and higher engagement. *It's small (four classes) and tests a specially prompted tutor, not ordinary chatbot use.*

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Yang, T.-C., Hsu, Y.-C., & Wu, J.-Y. (2025). The effectiveness of ChatGPT in assisting high school students in programming learning: Evidence from a quasi-experimental research. *Interactive Learning Environments, 33*(6), 3726–3743. https://doi.org/10.1080/10494820.2025.2450659
   153 high school students learning programming. Those using ChatGPT reported lower flow experience, lower self-efficacy, and lower learning achievement than those taught with conventional methods. *Quasi-experimental means students weren't randomly assigned. I read only the abstract because the publisher page blocked me.*

4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Bulut, N., & Borromeo Ferri, R. (2026). AI-powered learning: Exploring secondary school students' use of ChatGPT for solving mathematical modelling problems. *ZDM – Mathematics Education, 58*(5), 993–1009. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11858-026-01803-6
   A qualitative study of ten 10th-graders working on math modelling tasks, using their chat logs and interviews. Students mostly typed simple one-shot prompts, and they rarely validated or interpreted what ChatGPT gave back. *It describes behaviour and doesn't measure learning. The sample is very small.*

5. **Peer-reviewed article.** Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), 6. https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006
   A survey and interview study of 666 people of all ages. More frequent AI use went with lower critical-thinking scores, linked through "cognitive offloading," and younger participants relied on AI more. *It's correlational, so it can't show cause. It covers adults, not high schoolers specifically, and a correction has been published.*

6. **Peer-reviewed article.** Weidlich, J., Gašević, D., Drachsler, H., & Kirschner, P. (2025). ChatGPT in education: An effect in search of a cause. *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning, 41*(5), e70105. https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.70105
   A conceptual critique of ChatGPT-and-learning experiments. The authors checked studies from a major meta-analysis and found that only a small minority clearly described the treatment, described what the control group did, and used valid learning measures. *It's not a study of students. It's about how to judge the other studies.*

**Reports from research organizations**

7. **Research report (World Bank working paper, not peer reviewed).** De Simone, M., Tiberti, F., Barron Rodriguez, M., Manolio, F., Mosuro, W., & Dikoru, E. J. (2025). *From chalkboards to chatbots: Evaluating the impact of generative AI on learning outcomes in Nigeria* (Policy Research Working Paper No. 11125). World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/1813-9450-11125
   A randomized trial of a six-week after-school program for senior secondary students in Nigeria, using Copilot (GPT-4) with teachers present. It found a 0.23 standard deviation gain in English. *It's included because it's a large randomized trial with secondary students. Watch for the setting: it was supervised and after school rather than homework, and the paper hasn't been through journal review.*

8. **Research organization report.** McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
   A national survey of 1,458 U.S. teens from fall 2025. One in ten said they do all or most of their schoolwork with chatbot help, and about half said chatbots have been at least somewhat helpful. *It's a survey of use and opinions, so it can tell you how common chatbot use is but not whether it helps learning.*

**Preprints**

9. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   Two pre-registered lab experiments found no overall effect on learning. In further analyses and a field study, students who replaced learning steps with the AI (for example, generating exercise solutions) understood each topic less well. Students who used it alongside their own work (for example, asking for explanations) understood more. *It's included because it's one of the few studies that splits students by how they used the AI. Watch for two things: the participants are university programming students, and the usage findings come from the "exploratory" analyses rather than the pre-registered main test.*

10. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Rismanchian, S., Uzun, H., Matayoshi, J., Cosyn, E., & Kurd-Misto, E. (2026). *Faster completion, less learning: Generative AI reduced study time on math problems and the knowledge they build* (arXiv:2605.21629) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21629
   Uses 3.2 million interactions on the ALEKS math platform. After ChatGPT came out, time spent on word problems that AI can solve easily fell 31.3% among high schoolers. Among college students, scores on supervised (proctored) retention questions dropped. *The data set is very large, but the learning-outcome results are for college students, and two of the authors work for the company behind ALEKS.*
