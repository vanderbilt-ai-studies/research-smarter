1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, Article 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211 (full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11984610/)
   The study covered 1,227 students aged 12–15 at 30 schools in England. Students at schools with restrictive phone policies used their phones about 40 minutes less during the school day. It compares schools at one point in time, so it can't show cause and effect.

2. **Peer-reviewed article.** King, D. L., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., & Wade, T. (2024). "Phones off while school's on": Evaluating problematic phone use and the social, wellbeing, and academic effects of banning phones in schools. *Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 13*(4), 913–922. DOI: 10.1556/2006.2024.00058
   This was a preregistered study of about 1,280 students in grades 7–12 at five South Australian schools, some with a ban and some without, surveyed at the start and one month later. School belonging didn't differ between the two groups. Bullying was slightly higher in the ban group, but it fell significantly in both groups. One month is a short follow-up. *The publisher's page blocked me, so I worked from the abstract in Crossref.*

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. DOI: 10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
   This study compared classroom-only bans with whole-school bans in 24 Dutch schools (1,398 students, average age 16). Wellbeing and bullying were no different under the two types of ban. Whole-school bans were linked to weaker student–teacher connection and, for girls, a weaker sense of belonging at school. It compares two kinds of ban, not a ban against no ban. *I read the abstract only, in Crossref.*

4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Beneito, P., & Vicente-Chirivella, Ó. (2022). Banning mobile phones in schools: Evidence from regional-level policies in Spain. *Applied Economic Analysis, 30*(90), 153–175. DOI: 10.1108/AEA-05-2021-0112
   Two Spanish regions banned phones in 2015, and the authors compared them with the rest of Spain before and after. They report "noticeable reductions of bullying incidence among teenagers" in those two regions. The data is at the regional level and comes from official bullying counts, so it doesn't separate in-school bullying from online bullying. *I read the abstract only; I couldn't open the full text.*

5. **Peer-reviewed article (scoping review).** Campbell, M., Edwards, E. J., Pennell, D., Poed, S., Lister, V., Gillett-Swan, J., Kelly, A., Zec, D., & Nguyen, T.-A. (2024). Evidence for and against banning mobile phones in schools: A scoping review. *Journal of Psychologists and Counsellors in Schools, 34*(3), 242–265. https://eprints.qut.edu.au/251231/
   This review looked at 22 studies on how bans affect academic outcomes, mental health and cyberbullying. None of them was a randomized trial. The studies defined bans and outcomes so differently that the authors found them hard to reconcile. Its conclusions about cyberbullying are in the full text, which I didn't read.

6. **Peer-reviewed article (argument paper).** Selwyn, N., & Aagaard, J. (2021). Banning mobile phones from classrooms—An opportunity to advance understandings of technology addiction, distraction and cyberbullying. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 52*(1), 8–19. DOI: 10.1111/bjet.12943
   This paper has no new data. It's a scholarly argument that bans are a chance to rethink five concerns, cyberbullying among them. It's useful for framing the debate, not as evidence of what bans do. *I read the abstract only, in Crossref.*

**Working papers (not yet peer reviewed)**

7. **Working paper, not yet peer reviewed.** Allcott, H., Baron, E. J., Dee, T., Duckworth, A. L., Gentzkow, M., & Jacob, B. (2026). *The effects of school phone bans: National evidence from lockable pouches* (NBER Working Paper No. 35132). National Bureau of Economic Research. https://www.nber.org/papers/w35132
   This is a large US study of schools that adopted lockable phone pouches. The pouches "substantially" cut phone use. Student wellbeing dipped in the first year, then recovered and turned positive in later years. There was "no clear evidence" of less perceived online bullying. It's a well-known research team, but it hasn't been peer reviewed yet. *I read the abstract only.*

8. **Working paper, not yet peer reviewed.** Abrahamsson, S. (2024). *Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health* (Discussion Paper No. 1/2024). Norwegian School of Economics. https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/hhsnhheco/2024_5f001.htm
   This study covers Norwegian middle schools, which are younger students than yours. The abstract says bullying went down for both boys and girls after bans, and that girls made fewer health-care visits for psychological symptoms. *Abstract only; the full-text server was down.* Figures quoted elsewhere online (for example, "46% less bullying") came from secondhand coverage that I didn't check.

## Questions the results raise

- There may be a gap between students *saying* they talk more and researchers *measuring* better connection: one Dutch study found weaker belonging for girls under whole-school bans.
- Most studies measure bullying overall. Whether bullying *moved* or simply *dropped* may depend on whether a study separates in-school from online bullying.
- Your counterargument about boredom may matter more for students who were already isolated, given the NL Times report.
