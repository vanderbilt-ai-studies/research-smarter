## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Does it hurt learning?** This is the biggest argument. In Oregon, Paul Thompson found math and reading scores dropped after districts switched, and the drop grew over time ([Education Next, 2021](https://www.educationnext.org/shrinking-school-week-effects-four-day-schedule-student-achievement/)). Other states, like Missouri, found no significant difference ([Education Week explainer, 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04)).
- **Does it help hire and keep teachers?** This is often the main reason districts switch. An Arkansas study found teachers in four-day districts were 28% more likely to stay. Oregon and Montana studies found no difference ([Education Week, 2025](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/4-day-school-weeks-may-have-diminishing-returns-for-teacher-recruitment/2025/07)).
- **Is it worth the money?** Savings are usually estimated at a few percent of a district's budget. Thompson estimates about 2% in Oregon.

**Major voices**
- Economists who study education, including Thompson (Oregon State) and Stefanie Fischer and Daniel Argyle (Colorado youth crime).
- RAND Corporation, which has studied how the schedule is put into practice.
- The HEDCO Institute at the University of Oregon, which did a 2025 systematic review of the 11 strongest studies ([UO news release](https://news.uoregon.edu/node/13412)).
- Education Week and Chalkbeat, which turn this research into coverage for teachers and policymakers.

**Patterns**
- **Mostly rural, and spreading fast.** More than 2,100 schools now use the schedule.
- **Results depend on context.** HEDCO found that effects differ between rural and non-rural districts and between elementary and high school. No study looked only at city schools.
- **Most studies use existing records.** They compare test scores, turnover data, and arrest records between districts that switched and districts that didn't. Few ask students or families directly.
- **One state at a time.** Many findings come from a single state, such as the Colorado study linking the schedule to a rise of nearly 20% in youth crime ([Chalkbeat, 2018](https://chalkbeat.org/2018/4/2/21104689/four-day-school-weeks-a-nationwide-symptom-of-tight-budgets-lead-to-more-youth-crime-study-finds)).

**Open questions**
- **What do students actually do on the fifth day?** HEDCO names this as unanswered.
- **How much instruction is really lost?** Some districts lengthen the other four days and some don't.
- **What changes when nearby districts switch too?** A teacher-hiring advantage may shrink once every district in the area has the schedule.
- **What happens to families and to meals?** There's little evidence on childcare, parents' jobs, and access to school meals on the off day.
