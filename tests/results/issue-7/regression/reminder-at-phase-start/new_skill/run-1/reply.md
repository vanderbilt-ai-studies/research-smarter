## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Achievement.** The largest recent study, by Morton, Thompson and Kuhfeld, followed students in grades 3–8 across six states. It found small negative effects, for example −0.07 standard deviations in spring reading. The effects were larger in non-rural schools than in rural ones and may grow over time ([EdWorkingPaper 22-630](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai22-630); the published version appeared in *Economics of Education Review*, 2024, per [Journalist's Resource](https://journalistsresource.org/education/four-day-school-week-research/)). An older Colorado study by Anderson and Walker (2015) found *positive* effects on reading and math.
- **Teachers.** District leaders widely believe the schedule helps them hire and keep teachers. A Missouri study of 178 switching districts found no meaningful retention or recruitment gain: 0.6 fewer exits per 100 teachers, which isn't statistically significant ([The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/k-12-leaders-love-the-four-day-school-week-but-a-new-study-shows-that-it-doesnt-do-what-they-hope/); [Show-Me Institute commentary by Cory Koedel](https://showmeinstitute.org/?p=588415)). An Oregon study found turnover about 4 percentage points *higher* five or more years after a switch ([Education Week, 2024](https://edweek.org/leadership/4-day-school-weeks-and-their-surprising-effect-on-teacher-turnover/2024/10)). A Texas study found turnover fell by about 2.7 points ([Education Week, 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04)).

**Major voices**
- **Economists of education** do most of the work: Paul Thompson (Oregon State), Emily Morton (NWEA), and Cory Koedel (Missouri).
- **RAND** published a large 2021 mixed-methods study. I couldn't read the report itself. Secondhand summaries say families were highly satisfied and that student growth fell behind after about three years.
- **Groups that support adoption:** superintendents, especially in rural districts, and parents. In the RAND survey, nearly three-quarters of parents were "very satisfied" ([Education Week, 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04)).

**Patterns**
- **How much time is cut seems to matter.** Thompson and Ward found score drops only in schools that cut total weekly hours the most ([Journalist's Resource](https://journalistsresource.org/education/four-day-school-week-research/)).
- **Rural and non-rural districts differ.** Effects keep varying by location.
- **Outcomes beyond test scores split both ways:**
  - Oklahoma high schools saw bullying fall about 39% and fights about 31% after switching.
  - Rural Colorado saw juvenile crime rise nearly 20%, mostly property crime ([Chalkbeat](https://chalkbeat.org/2018/4/2/21104689/four-day-school-weeks-a-nationwide-symptom-of-tight-budgets-lead-to-more-youth-crime-study-finds)).
  - An Oregon health study linked switching to more drug use and food insecurity, but it rests on only 8 switching schools, and the authors themselves urge caution ([Tomayko et al., *Journal of School Health*, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8595551)).
- **Savings are small:** roughly 0.4–2.5% of a district's budget.

**Open questions**
- Long-run effects. Most studies cover only a few years after the switch.
- Childcare and parents' work, which is discussed a lot but measured little.
- Why districts that report high satisfaction show flat or negative measured outcomes.
- Whether results from the rural West apply to the fast-growing suburban adopters, such as in Texas.

Two notes on these sources. The Show-Me Institute piece is commentary on the Missouri study, not the study itself. The EdWorkingPaper is a working-paper version of the study, so the published version may differ slightly.
