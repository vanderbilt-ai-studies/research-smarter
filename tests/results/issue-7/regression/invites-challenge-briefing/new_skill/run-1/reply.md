## Preliminary briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Does it hurt learning?** This is the biggest fight. Paul Thompson's Oregon study of about 690,000 students found math and reading scores fell, and the losses grew over time in schools that kept the schedule ([Education Next, 2021](https://www.educationnext.org/?p=49713460)). Studies in Colorado and Missouri found no significant difference ([Education Week, Apr. 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04)).
- **Does it help keep teachers?** A study of every Texas teacher since 2006, led by Georgia State economist Cade Lawson, found turnover fell about 2.7 percentage points after districts switched. Earlier studies in Oregon, Colorado and Missouri found no effect, or higher turnover ([Education Week, Jan. 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/do-4-day-school-weeks-attract-and-retain-better-teachers-what-the-largest-study-yet-says/2026/01)).
- **Is it good for kids outside school?** Education Week reports an Oklahoma study that found less bullying and fewer fights. It also reports an Oregon study that found more food insecurity and more marijuana use. I've only seen both studies described in that article, not in the originals.

**Major voices**
- Economists who study education: Thompson at Oregon State and Lawson at Georgia State.
- RAND Corporation, whose 2021 study covered Idaho, New Mexico and Oklahoma.
- NWEA, the testing organization.
- The Education Commission of the States and the National Conference of State Legislatures, which publish policy overviews ([NCSL](https://www.ncsl.org/education/four-day-school-week-overview)).

**Patterns**
- Most four-day districts are small and rural, in the West and Midwest. They grew from 257 schools in 1999 to more than 2,100 schools in 26 states by 2024-25.
- **Savings are small.** The Education Commission of the States estimates districts save about 0.4% to 2.5% of their budget. Thompson puts the average at about $350 per student, roughly 2% of spending.
- **The stated reasons seem to be changing.** Lawson told Education Week that the early reason was saving money, "and there wasn't much of that." He now describes teacher recruitment as the main reason districts give.
- Most studies look at one state at a time, and many recent ones are working papers that haven't been peer-reviewed yet.

**Open questions**
- What happens on the fifth day (closed buildings, optional programs, teacher training), and does that change the effects?
- How do larger suburban districts that switched after the pandemic compare with rural ones? Colorado's 27J, near Denver, is one example.
- How are local communities, businesses and working parents affected? I found little research on this.
