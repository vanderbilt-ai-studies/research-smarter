**Peer-reviewed articles.** All seven count toward your requirement of at least 3 peer-reviewed sources. I confirmed each journal in Crossref.

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*)
   Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. Free copy: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/
   This was a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey. While students had GPT-4 for practice, grades rose 48% with a standard ChatGPT-style tool and 127% with a tutor version built to protect learning. When access was taken away, the standard-tool group scored 17% lower than students who never had it. The tutor version largely avoided that drop.

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Interactive Learning Environments*)
   Yang, T.-C., Hsu, Y.-C., & Wu, J.-Y. (2025). The effectiveness of ChatGPT in assisting high school students in programming learning: Evidence from a quasi-experimental research. *Interactive Learning Environments, 33*(6), 3726–3743.
   This study had 153 high school programming students. The ChatGPT group reported lower flow, self-efficacy, and achievement than the group taught with conventional methods. *The publisher's page blocked me, so I read only the abstract, through OpenAlex.*

3. **Peer-reviewed article, systematic review and meta-analysis** (*Computers & Education*)
   Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224.
   It pools 69 experimental studies, mostly at the university level. Overall, ChatGPT improved academic performance and reduced mental effort. The authors warn that weak methods (small samples, unproctored tests) mean the results should be read with caution. *Page blocked; I read the abstract only.*

4. **Peer-reviewed article, meta-analysis** (*Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*)
   Wu, X., Zhu, P., Zhang, J., Yin, M., & Wang, Y. (2026). ChatGPT's impact on student learning outcomes: A meta-analysis of 35 experimental studies. *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 13*, 684.
   It pools 35 studies with 4,193 participants and finds a moderate positive effect (g = 0.670). Subject, length of the experiment, and instructional mode affected the size of the effect, but educational level did not. *Page didn't load for me; I read the abstract only.*

5. **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*)
   Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. Author's version: https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09315
   117 university students did a writing task, each with a different kind of support. The ChatGPT group improved their essays the most, but their knowledge gain and transfer were no different from the other groups. They also showed fewer self-monitoring behaviors.

6. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers in Human Behavior*)
   Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, 108386. Repository copy: https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/125262/
   91 university students were randomly assigned to research a science question with either ChatGPT or Google. The ChatGPT group felt less mental load but produced weaker reasoning in their final recommendations.

7. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Societies*)
   Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), 6.
   This was a survey and interview study of 666 people across age groups. More frequent AI use went with lower critical-thinking scores, linked through "cognitive offloading," and younger people relied on AI most. It shows correlation, not cause. *Page blocked; I read the abstract only.*

**Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** I'm including it because it's the closest study to your second sub-question.

8. Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (Version 2) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   Two pre-registered lab experiments found no overall effect of LLMs on learning. Students who used the LLM to *replace* learning activities (for example, generating solutions) covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used it to *complement* their learning (for example, asking for explanations) understood more. LLMs also widened the gap between students with low and high prior knowledge.

**Research organization reports**

9. McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
   Pew surveyed 1,458 U.S. teens (ages 13–17) in fall 2025. 54% have used chatbots for schoolwork help, and one in ten say they do all or most of their schoolwork with that help.

10. RAND Corporation. (2026, March 17). *Student use of AI for homework rises as concerns grow about critical thinking skills* [Press release]. https://www.rand.org/news/press/2026/03/student-use-of-ai-for-homework-rises-as-concerns-grow.html
   From May to December 2025, AI use for homework rose from 48% to 62% among students ages 12–29, driven mainly by middle and high schoolers. 67% said AI harms critical thinking. 45% count getting direct answers as cheating, while nearly 80% don't count using AI to understand an assignment as cheating. *This is the press release; the full report (RRA4742-1) blocked me.*

## What I found and didn't find

- **There's plenty on college students and short lab tasks.** There's much less on high schoolers. Only Bastani and Yang studied high school students, and neither studied homework done at home.
- **I found almost no studies that measure "checking work" as a behavior.** The closest are Lehmann's replace-versus-complement split and Bastani's two tool versions. Bastani varied the *tool's design*, not the student's own choice.
- **I found no long-term studies.** Most experiments last days or weeks.
- **Watch out for a retracted study.** You'll likely run into Wang & Fan's 2025 meta-analysis in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*. Crossref shows it was retracted in 2026, so don't cite it.

## Questions these results raise

- How well do findings about college students apply to high schoolers?
- Is "checking your work" something students choose, or something stronger students already do? Lehmann's finding about prior knowledge is relevant here.
- Do the meta-analyses' positive averages hide differences in *how* students used the tool?
