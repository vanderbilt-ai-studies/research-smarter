## Sources

**Peer-reviewed original studies**

- **Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*). Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. Free copy, opened: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/ (the publisher's page blocked me). This was a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students. They used either a standard ChatGPT-style tutor or a tutor with built-in safeguards. Both groups scored higher on practice while they had the tool. Once access was removed, the standard-tool group did 17% worse than students who never had it, and the safeguarded tutor largely prevented that drop.
- **Peer-reviewed article** (*Interactive Learning Environments*). Yang, T.-C., Hsu, Y.-C., & Wu, J.-Y. (2025). The effectiveness of ChatGPT in assisting high school students in programming learning: Evidence from a quasi-experimental research. *Interactive Learning Environments, 33*(6), 3726–3743. Found and confirmed in Crossref, but I couldn't open the publisher's page, so check it before relying on it. In this quasi-experiment with 153 high school students, the ChatGPT group reported lower flow, lower self-efficacy and lower learning achievement than students taught with conventional methods.
- **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*). Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. The journal page blocked me; the free preprint copy opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09315. This randomized experiment had 117 university students do a writing task. The ChatGPT group improved their essays most, but they gained no more knowledge or transfer than the other groups, and they showed fewer self-monitoring steps.
- **Peer-reviewed article** (*Societies*). Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), 6. Confirmed in Crossref; the publisher's page blocked me. This survey-and-interview study of 666 people of varied ages found that heavier AI use went with lower critical-thinking scores, linked to offloading thinking onto the tool. It's correlational, and the sample was mostly adults, not high schoolers.

**Peer-reviewed reviews, meta-analyses, and critiques**

- **Peer-reviewed meta-analysis** (*Computers & Education*). Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. Confirmed in Crossref; the publisher's page blocked me. It pooled 69 experimental studies, mostly at the university level, and found ChatGPT improved academic performance while reducing mental effort. The authors warn that the studies had weak designs and that the tests often measured the quality of the output rather than skills.
- **Peer-reviewed meta-analysis** (*Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*). Wu, X., Zhu, P., Zhang, J., Yin, M., & Wang, Y. (2026). ChatGPT's impact on student learning outcomes: A meta-analysis of 35 experimental studies. *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 13*, 684. https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-026-07019-z. Across 35 studies with 4,193 participants, it found a moderate positive effect of ChatGPT on learning. Subject, study length and teaching mode affected results; education level did not.
- **Peer-reviewed systematic review** (*Smart Learning Environments*). Zhai, C., Wibowo, S., & Li, L. (2024). The effects of over-reliance on AI dialogue systems on students' cognitive abilities: A systematic review. *Smart Learning Environments, 11*, 28. Confirmed in Crossref; the publisher's page blocked me. This review of 14 articles concludes that over-relying on AI chatbots weakens students' critical thinking and decision-making, because users favor fast answers.
- **Peer-reviewed critical analysis** (*Computers & Education*). Lawson, A. P., Martella, A. M., Weidlich, J., Mulders, M., & Buchner, J. (2026). Color me confounded: A critical analysis of media comparisons on ChatGPT in education. *Computers & Education, 254*, 105701. Author's copy, opened: https://learninglab.uni-due.de/sites/default/files/1-s2.0-S0360131526001405-main.pdf. The authors reexamined the studies inside the Deng and Wang & Fan meta-analyses. Many compared ChatGPT groups with control groups that differed in teaching methods, practice or time. They conclude that ChatGPT's reported benefits "must be interpreted with caution."

**Preprint**

- **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047. Based on studies in coding classes, it found no overall effect of chatbots on learning. Using them to *replace* learning activities, such as generating solutions, lowered understanding, while using them to *complement* learning, such as asking for explanations, raised it. The abstract doesn't say whether participants were high schoolers.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plentiful:** experiments and meta-analyses on whether ChatGPT raises scores overall, mostly with university students.
- **Thin:** studies that directly compare *how* students choose to use chatbots. Lehmann et al. comes closest, but it's a preprint. Bastani et al. compared two tool designs, not students' own habits.
- **Not found:** any study of high schoolers doing real homework at home, and long-term studies beyond one semester.
- **Contested:** the positive meta-analyses. Lawson et al. questions their study designs, and one of them was retracted.

## Questions the results raise

- Bastani et al. tested how the *tool* was designed, while your position is about how the *student* uses it. It's worth working out whether that difference matters for your argument.
- Several studies found better scores during use but no gain afterward, which raises the issue of what "learning" means in your paper.
- Lehmann et al. found the gap between students with more and less prior knowledge got wider. That could connect to which students tend to check their work and which tend to copy answers.
