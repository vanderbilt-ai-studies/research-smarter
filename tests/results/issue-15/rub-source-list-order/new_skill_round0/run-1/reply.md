## Sources

**Peer-reviewed articles**

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*). Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. *Publisher page blocked me; details and abstract are from Crossref. A correction was issued in August 2025, which I couldn't read.*
   A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey compared two tools: a plain ChatGPT-style tool and a tutor version with built-in safeguards. With the plain tool, practice grades went up 48%. When the tool was taken away, those students scored 17% lower than students who never had it. The abstract says the safeguards "largely mitigated" that drop.

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*). Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. *Publisher page blocked me; abstract from Crossref, which dates the online version to December 2024.*
   117 university students wrote essays with help from ChatGPT, a human expert, a writing-analytics tool, or nothing. The ChatGPT group improved their essays most, but they didn't gain more knowledge or transfer it better. The authors warn that ChatGPT may encourage "metacognitive laziness."

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers in Human Behavior*). Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, 108386. Open-access copy: https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/125262/
   91 university students researched a science issue using either ChatGPT-3.5 or Google. The ChatGPT users reported less mental effort but produced lower-quality reasoning in their final recommendations.

4. **Peer-reviewed article (meta-analysis)** (*Computers & Education*). Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. *Publisher page blocked me. Venue confirmed via Crossref; I saw the abstract only on a citation-database page, not the publisher's.*
   This review pooled 69 experimental studies from 2022–2024. It reports that ChatGPT improved academic performance and reduced mental effort, with no effect on self-efficacy. The authors themselves warn that methodological limits "warrant cautious interpretation."

5. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Societies*, MDPI). Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), Article 6. *Publisher page blocked me; abstract from Crossref. A correction was issued, which I couldn't read.*
   Surveys and interviews with 666 people of mixed ages (not specifically students) found that heavier AI use went with lower critical-thinking scores, partly through "cognitive offloading," meaning handing mental work to the tool. Younger participants relied on AI more. This is a correlational study.

6. **Peer-reviewed conference paper** (ACL 2025, the Association for Computational Linguistics' main conference). Vanzo, A., Pal Chowdhury, S., & Sachan, M. (2025). GPT-4 as a homework tutor can improve student engagement and learning outcomes. In *Proceedings of the 63rd Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (Volume 1: Long Papers)* (pp. 31119–31136). https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1502
   A randomized trial in four high school English-as-a-second-language classes replaced regular homework with sessions on a GPT-4 tutor. Students were more satisfied and learning outcomes weren't harmed, and "one group" showed significantly better gains. Note that the tutor was built by the researchers, not plain ChatGPT.

**Working papers (research organizations, not yet peer reviewed in a journal)**

7. **Working paper** (Centre for Economic Policy Research). Strömberg, D., Lei, V., & Wu, Y. (2026). *The generative AI learning penalty: Evidence from Chinese secondary education* (CEPR Discussion Paper No. 21577). https://ideas.repec.org/p/cpr/ceprdp/21577.html
   30 months of data on 26,811 Chinese students in grades 7–12. After students started using AI, homework scores rose 18% but monthly exam scores fell 20% within six months. The authors say the losses are concentrated among about 80% of AI users whose behavior "is consistent with homework outsourcing." They infer that from very fast completion times; nobody observed it directly.

8. **Working paper** (World Bank Policy Research Working Paper). De Simone, M. E., Tiberti, F. H., Barron Rodriguez, M. R., Manolio, F. A., Mosuro, W., & Dikoru, E. J. (2025). *From chalkboards to chatbots: Evaluating the impact of generative AI on learning outcomes in Nigeria* (Policy Research Working Paper No. 11125). World Bank. https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/wbkwbrwps/11125.htm
   A six-week randomized trial with first-year senior secondary students in Nigeria. Students used Microsoft Copilot in pairs during after-school sessions, with teacher guidance. English scores rose 0.23 standard deviations. The largest gains were for girls and for students who started out stronger.

**Preprints, not yet peer reviewed**

9. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed** (arXiv). Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* arXiv:2409.09047. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   Two lab experiments found no overall effect of LLMs on learning. Students who used them to substitute for their own work, for example by generating exercise solutions, covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used them to complement their work, for example by asking for explanations, understood more. The abstract doesn't give participants' ages.

10. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed** (arXiv). Kosmyna, N., Hauptmann, E., Yuan, Y. T., Situ, J., Liao, X.-H., Beresnitzky, A. V., Braunstein, I., & Maes, P. (2025). *Your brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of cognitive debt when using an AI assistant for essay writing task*. arXiv:2506.08872. https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872
    54 participants wrote essays using an LLM, a search engine, or no tools while researchers recorded their brain activity with EEG. The LLM group showed the weakest brain connectivity, struggled to quote their own essays, and felt the least ownership of them.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plentiful:** studies where AI raises performance *while the tool is available* but not real learning afterward (sources 1, 2, 3, 7). Several meta-analyses also exist.
- **Thin:** studies that actually observe *how* individual high schoolers use a chatbot for homework, such as checking work versus copying answers, and then link that to their learning. Source 7 infers the type of use from timing. Source 9 measures it in a lab, and its participants' ages aren't given in the abstract.
- **Thin:** U.S. high school outcome data. The high school studies come from Turkey, China, Nigeria and a European classroom. Pew covers U.S. teens but measures use and opinions, not learning.
- **Long-term studies:** only source 7 runs longer than a few months.
- **A retraction:** a widely cited 2025 meta-analysis by Wang & Fan in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* reported big learning gains. Crossref shows it was **retracted in April 2026**, so I left it out. It still appears in search results and in other papers' citations, so watch for it.

## Open issues the results raise

- Several of the "helpful" results (sources 1, 6, 8) involve tutors *designed* to hold back answers or used under teacher guidance. That's a different variable from what the *student* chooses to do.
- Sources 2, 3 and 10 study university students or adults. You'll need to decide how much they tell you about high schoolers.
