1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*). Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   Free full text: [PubMed Central copy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/). This opened. The publisher's DOI page blocked my tool.
   *Summary:* A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students. Some students had a standard ChatGPT-style tutor ("GPT Base") and some had a tutor with prompts designed to protect learning ("GPT Tutor"). The safeguards in GPT Tutor largely prevented that drop.

**Working papers (not peer reviewed)**

3. **Working paper, not peer reviewed** (CEPR Discussion Paper). Strömberg, D., Lei, V., & Wu, Y. (2026). *The generative AI learning penalty: Evidence from Chinese secondary education* (CEPR Discussion Paper No. 21577). Centre for Economic Policy Research. [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cpr/ceprdp/21577.html)
   *Summary:* 30 months of data on 26,811 Chinese students in grades 7–12. After students started using AI, homework scores rose 18% and completion time fell 30%. Monthly exam scores fell 20% within six months. The losses fell mostly on the roughly 80% of AI users whose homework was finished unusually fast with high scores, which the authors read as outsourcing. AI users who kept spending about as much time on homework as non-users had only small losses.

4. **Working paper, not peer reviewed** (World Bank Policy Research Working Paper). De Simone, M. E., Tiberti, F. H., Barron Rodriguez, M. R., Manolio, F. A., Mosuro, W., & Dikoru, E. J. [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:11125). The World Bank's own page blocked my tool.
   *Summary:* A six-week randomized trial in Nigeria with first-year senior secondary students. They used Microsoft Copilot (GPT-4) as an after-school English tutor. 5. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed** (arXiv). Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047v2) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.09047
   *Summary:* Two pre-registered lab experiments found no overall effect of LLM access on learning. Using the AI in place of your own work (for example, having it solve exercises) meant covering more topics but understanding each one less. Using it alongside your own work (for example, asking for explanations) improved understanding. The abstract doesn't say who the participants were, so check whether they were high schoolers.

- **Thin:** studies that directly watch *how* high schoolers use chatbots, such as checking work versus copying answers, and then measure learning. Strömberg et al. infer use from homework time, and Bastani et al. compare tool designs, not student habits. Most experimental work is with university students, as Deng et al. note.
- **Not found:** long-term studies beyond about two years, and studies of everyday homework use in U.S. high schools.

## Questions these results raise

- Several studies compare **tool design** (a guarded tutor vs. an open chatbot) rather than **student choice**. Your position is about what students choose to do, so it's worth working out whether that difference matters for it.
- Strömberg et al. found that boys and high achievers lost more, and Lehmann et al. found the gap between low and high prior knowledge got wider. That could bear on whether "checking vs. getting answers" is the whole story.
