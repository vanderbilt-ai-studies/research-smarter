### Peer-reviewed articles

- **Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*). Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. *The publisher page blocked me, so this has no link. I read only the abstract.*
  A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students. They got either a standard ChatGPT-style tutor or a tutor with built-in safeguards. With access, both groups did better on practice. Once access was removed, the standard-tutor group scored 17% lower than students who never had it, and the safeguarded tutor largely avoided that drop.

- **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers in Human Behavior*). Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, 108386. *The publisher page blocked me, so this has no link. I read only the abstract.*
  91 university students researched a science issue using either ChatGPT or Google. The ChatGPT group reported less mental effort but wrote weaker reasoning and justifications.

- **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*). Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. Free preprint version: https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09315
  117 university students were randomly assigned to write with ChatGPT, a human expert, a writing-analytics tool, or no help. The ChatGPT group improved its essay scores most, but didn't gain significantly more knowledge or transfer. The groups also differed in how they self-regulated their learning.

- **Peer-reviewed article** (*Smart Learning Environments*). Zhai, C., Wibowo, S., & Li, L. D. (2024). I read only the abstract.*
  A review of 14 studies. It finds that over-reliance (accepting AI output without questioning it) is linked to weaker critical thinking, decision-making and analytical reasoning, because people prefer quick shortcuts.

- **Peer-reviewed article** (*Smart Learning Environments*). Klar, M. (2025). Using ChatGPT is easy, using it effectively is tough? A mixed methods study on K-12 students' perceptions, interaction patterns, and support for learning with generative AI chatbots. *Smart Learning Environments, 12*, Article 32. *The publisher page blocked me, so this has no link. I read only the abstract.*
  An experiment with 106 secondary school students. Without guidance, students underused the chatbot's ability to adapt material to them. With guidance, they used it twice as much, but both groups gained about the same amount of knowledge.

- **Peer-reviewed article** (*LUMAT: International Journal on Math, Science and Technology Education*). Brasken, M., Berts, K.-E., Wallin, S., & Frilund, S. (2025). - **Peer-reviewed article** (*Scientific Reports*). Kestin, G., Miller, K., Klales, A., Milbourne, T., & Ponti, G. (2025). ### Preprint

- **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047v2). arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
  Two lab experiments plus a field study. They find no overall effect on learning. Students who used the AI to generate solutions covered more topics but understood each one less, and students who used it to ask for explanations understood more. ### Research organization reports

- **Research organization report.** McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
  A fall 2025 survey of 1,458 U.S. teens. 54% have used chatbots for schoolwork, and 10% say they do all or most of their schoolwork with chatbot help. 59% say students at their school use AI to cheat at least somewhat often.

- **Research organization report.** Schwartz, H. L., & Diliberti, M. K. (2026). *More students use AI for homework, and more believe it harms critical thinking: Selected findings from the American Youth Panel* (RR-A4742-1). RAND Corporation. https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4742-1.html
  A December 2025 survey of 1,214 people aged 12–29. AI use for homework rose from 48% to 62% between May and December 2025, and 67% said more AI use for schoolwork will harm critical thinking. It reports what students say, not tested learning.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plentiful:** studies with college students, and surveys of how many teens use chatbots.
- **Thin:** experiments with high schoolers. Bastani, Klar and Brasken are the only three I found.
- **Not found:** any study comparing students who used a chatbot to *check their own finished work* with students who used it to *get answers*. The closest are Lehmann (solutions vs. explanations) and Bastani (a safeguarded tool vs. an open one). There are almost no long-term studies.
- **A warning:** a widely cited 2025 meta-analysis by Wang and Fan, which found large learning gains from ChatGPT, was [retracted in April 2026](https://www.govtech.com/education/nature-retracts-oft-cited-paper-on-positive-impact-of-chatgpt) over concerns about its analysis. I left it out. You'll still see it cited in articles and other papers, so watch for it.

## Questions the results raise

- Who chooses how the chatbot gets used: the student, or the tool's design? Bastani tested the tool's design, not students' own choices.
- Most of the "help" findings come from university students. It's unclear whether they apply to high schoolers.
- In the surveys, students' self-reports and their tested learning could point in different directions.
