**Peer-reviewed articles.** I confirmed the journal for each in Crossref.

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*)
   Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122.
   *The publisher's page blocked me, so there's no link. Get it through your library.* A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey. Students had either a plain ChatGPT-style tutor ("GPT Base") or one with learning safeguards ("GPT Tutor"). Both groups did better while they had access. Once access was removed, the GPT Base group scored 17% lower than students who never had it, and the authors say those students had used it as a "crutch." The safeguarded version largely avoided that drop. I read the abstract, not the full text.

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Scientific Reports*)
   Kestin, G., Miller, K., Klales, A., Milbourne, T., & Ponti, G. (2025). AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: An RCT introducing a novel research-based design in an authentic educational setting. *Scientific Reports, 15*, 17458. [Open-access copy on PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)
   A randomized trial with about 194 Harvard undergraduates in an introductory physics course. Students learned more in less time from a custom AI tutor built on teaching best practices than from an active-learning class, and they reported feeling more engaged.

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*)
   Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2024). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. [Preprint version on arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09315)
   *The journal page blocked me. The link goes to the authors' preprint, so cite the journal version.* A randomized experiment with 117 university students on a writing task. Students using ChatGPT improved their essay scores more than the other groups, but they showed no greater gain in knowledge or transfer. The authors warn about "metacognitive laziness." Some sources give the year as 2025, so check it against the journal.

4. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers in Human Behavior*)
   Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, 108386.
   *Publisher page didn't open, so there's no link.* 91 university students researched a science issue using either ChatGPT or Google. The ChatGPT group felt less mental effort but produced weaker reasoning in their final recommendations.

5. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers & Education*)
   Darvishi, A., Khosravi, H., Sadiq, S., Gašević, D., & Siemens, G. (2024). Impact of AI assistance on student agency. *Computers & Education, 210*, 104967.
   *Publisher page didn't open, so there's no link. The authors' abstract says it's open access.* A randomized experiment with 1,625 university students across 10 courses, using an AI tool that helped them write peer feedback. When the AI help was removed, students' work suffered: they had relied on the help rather than learned from it.

6. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Societies*)
   Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), 6.
   *Publisher page blocked me, so there's no link.* A survey plus interviews with 666 people of mixed ages. People who used AI tools more often scored lower on critical thinking, and the link ran through "cognitive offloading." Younger participants relied on AI more and scored lower. This is correlational, so it doesn't show cause. The article was corrected in September 2025, and I didn't check what changed.

7. **Peer-reviewed systematic review** (*Smart Learning Environments*)
   Zhai, C., Wibowo, S., & Li, L. D. (2024). The effects of over-reliance on AI dialogue systems on students' cognitive abilities: A systematic review. *Smart Learning Environments, 11*, 28.
   *Publisher page didn't open, so there's no link.* A review of 14 studies. It concludes that over-relying on AI dialogue systems affects decision-making, critical thinking, and analytical reasoning, because users favor quick shortcuts.

**Preprints and working papers (not peer reviewed)**

8. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed** (arXiv)
   Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* arXiv. [arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047)
   Two pre-registered lab experiments found no overall effect of LLMs on learning. Students who used them in place of their own work covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used them alongside their own work, for example asking for explanations, understood more. LLMs also widened the gap between students with low and high prior knowledge. The abstract doesn't say whether participants were high school or college students.

9. **Research organization report** (World Bank working paper, not peer reviewed)
   De Simone, M. E., Tiberti, F. H., Barron Rodriguez, M. R., Manolio, F. A., Mosuro, W., & Dikoru, E. J. (2025). *From chalkboards to chatbots: Evaluating the impact of generative AI on learning outcomes in Nigeria* (Policy Research Working Paper No. 11125). World Bank. [EconPapers listing](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:11125)
   A randomized trial with first-year senior secondary students in Nigeria. They spent six weeks in after-school sessions using Copilot (GPT-4) under teacher supervision, with prompts designed to make them reason. English scores rose by 0.23 standard deviations. I read only the abstract. This was a supervised program, not homework.

## What I found and didn't find

- **There's plenty of evidence that AI use can weaken learning when it replaces effort.** Most of it comes from university students, though.
- **There's little research on high schoolers specifically.** Bastani is the main one, plus the Nigeria working paper. Neither is about homework done alone at home.
- **I found no study that directly compares high schoolers who check their work with high schoolers who copy answers.** Lehmann et al. comes closest with its "substitute vs. complement" split. Bastani compares two tool designs, not two student habits.
- **I found almost no long-term studies.** Most run for weeks, not a school year.

## Questions these results raise

- Several studies point to how the tool is designed, not only how the student uses it. That could sit beside your "how students use it" framing or compete with it.
- Students' own sense of how much the AI helped may not match what they actually learned. An earlier version of Lehmann et al. reported this. That matters for any study that sorts students by how they *say* they use chatbots.
