## Sources

**Peer-reviewed articles.** For each one I confirmed the journal through Crossref and/or OpenAlex. Five of them count toward your requirement of 3 peer-reviewed sources.

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122.
   *The publisher's page blocked me, so there's no link. I confirmed the record and read the abstract through Crossref and OpenAlex. A correction was published in August 2025, and I couldn't see what it changed.*
   - This was a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students, comparing a standard ChatGPT-style tutor to one designed to protect learning.
   - While students had access, grades on practice problems went up. Once access was removed, students who had used the standard version scored 17% lower than students who never had it. The protected tutor largely prevented that drop.

2. **Peer-reviewed article.** Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530.
   *The publisher's page blocked me. The free preprint version is here: https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09315*
   - This was a randomized experiment with 117 **university** students writing essays with help from ChatGPT, a human expert, writing-analytics tools, or no tool.
   - The ChatGPT group improved their essay scores the most. Their knowledge gain and transfer were not significantly different from the other groups, and they showed fewer self-monitoring behaviors.

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), 6.
   *The publisher's page blocked me. I confirmed the record and read the abstract through Crossref and OpenAlex. A correction exists, and I couldn't see what it changed.*
   - Surveys and interviews with 666 people of mixed ages, not specifically students.
   - Frequent AI use was correlated with lower critical-thinking scores, and "cognitive offloading" (handing thinking off to the tool) accounted for part of that link. Younger participants relied on AI more.

4. **Peer-reviewed article (systematic review and meta-analysis).** Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   - Combines 69 experimental studies from 2022–2024, mostly at the **university** level.
   - Overall, ChatGPT improved academic performance and reduced mental effort. The authors caution that many of the studies were too small to give reliable estimates, and that many measured performance in ways ChatGPT's own output could inflate.

5. **Peer-reviewed article.** Klar, M. (2025). Using ChatGPT is easy, using it effectively is tough? A mixed methods study on K-12 students' perceptions, interaction patterns, and support for learning with generative AI chatbots. *Smart Learning Environments, 12*, 32. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40561-025-00385-2
   - An experiment with 106 German secondary students in grades 9–10. One group got brief guidance on how to use the chatbot, and the other got none.
   - Without guidance, students made little use of the chatbot's ability to adapt explanations to them. The guided group used it twice as much, but both groups learned about the same amount.

**Preprints, not yet peer reviewed**

6. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (Version 2) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   - Two pre-registered lab experiments plus a field study. Overall, the authors found no effect on learning. Students who used the AI *instead of* doing the work covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used it *alongside* their own work, for example to ask for explanations, understood more.
   - *Why it's here and what to watch for:* it's unusually close to your topic, but it hasn't been peer reviewed, and the abstract doesn't say who the participants were (the earlier version described coding students). Check the full paper for that.

7. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Kosmyna, N., Hauptmann, E., Yuan, Y. T., Situ, J., Liao, X.-H., Beresnitzky, A. V., Braunstein, I., & Maes, P. (2025). *Your brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of cognitive debt when using an AI assistant for essay writing task* (Version 2) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872
   - 54 participants wrote essays using ChatGPT, a search engine, or nothing, while EEG recorded their brain activity. The ChatGPT group showed the weakest brain connectivity and had trouble quoting their own essays.
   - *Why it's here and what to watch for:* it has had a lot of media attention, but the sample is small, the participants weren't high schoolers, and it hasn't been peer reviewed. It's useful for what happens in the brain during the task, not as proof of lasting harm.

**Research organization reports**

8. **Research organization report (working paper, not peer reviewed).** De Simone, M., Tiberti, F., Barron Rodriguez, M., Manolio, F., Mosuro, W., & Dikoru, E. J. (2025). *From chalkboards to chatbots: Evaluating the impact of generative AI on learning outcomes in Nigeria* (Policy Research Working Paper No. 11125). World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/1813-9450-11125
   - A randomized trial with senior secondary students who used Copilot (GPT-4) as an English tutor in a guided after-school program for six weeks. English scores rose by 0.23 standard deviations, with the largest gains for girls and for students who started out stronger.
   - *Why it's here and what to watch for:* it's a well-designed trial with high schoolers, but it's a working paper, not peer reviewed. Students also used the chatbot with teachers guiding them, which isn't the same as doing homework alone.

9. **Research organization report (survey).** McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
   - A survey of 1,458 U.S. teens aged 13–17, run September–October 2025. 54% have used chatbots for schoolwork, about 1 in 10 do all or most of their schoolwork with them, and 59% say cheating with AI happens at least somewhat often at their school.
   - *Why it's here and what to watch for:* it's a survey, not a study of learning. It's good for how teens use chatbots, but it can't tell you whether that use helps or hurts.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plentiful:** studies of college students, and short experiments that measure performance *while* students have the AI.
- **Thin:** studies of **high schoolers doing homework on their own**. Bastani is the strongest one I found. The Nigeria trial and Klar also involve secondary students, but in guided settings.
- **Not found:** any study that sorted students by whether they *checked their own work* versus *copied answers* and then compared what they learned. Lehmann's "instead of vs. alongside" comparison is the closest, and Bastani's two tutor designs come at it from another angle. I also found almost no studies longer than a single semester.
- **Warning:** a widely cited 2025 meta-analysis by Wang and Fan (*Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*) came up in my search, and coverage I found (GovTech) reports it was **retracted in April 2026**. I didn't open the retraction notice. If you see it cited elsewhere, check its status before using it.

## Questions these results raise

- Several of these studies compare the *design of the tool*, not the *student's choice*. That may or may not match your position's focus on what kids do with the tool.
- The gap between performance *with* the AI and *after* it's taken away is a recurring theme. It's worth thinking about which one your paper means by "learning."
- Two sources found bigger benefits for students who already knew more (Lehmann; De Simone et al.). That raises the issue of who tends to "check their work" in the first place.
