### Peer-reviewed articles (7, so these cover your "at least 3")

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*). Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey. One group had a standard ChatGPT-style tutor and another had a tutor built with learning safeguards. With the tools available, both groups did better on practice problems. When access was removed, the standard-tutor group scored 17% lower than students who never had AI. The safeguarded tutor largely avoided that drop. *(Abstract only.)*

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers & Education*). Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   A review of 69 experimental studies from 2022–2024, mostly at the university level. It found ChatGPT improved academic performance and reduced mental effort. The authors caution that many studies were small and that the tests used to measure learning may reflect the quality of ChatGPT's output rather than what students learned. *(Abstract only.)*

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*International Review of Research in Open and Distributed Learning*). Doo, M. Y., & Park, Y. (2026). A meta-analysis of ChatGPT's influence on learning achievement. *The International Review of Research in Open and Distributed Learning, 27*(1), 265–289. https://doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v27i1.8775
   A meta-analysis of 22 studies that found a moderate positive effect on achievement. The effect was larger for middle and high school students than for undergraduates, but that difference wasn't statistically significant.

4. **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*). Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   A randomized lab study of 117 **university** students on a writing task. The ChatGPT group improved their essays more than the other groups. However, they didn't gain more knowledge or transfer it better, and they showed fewer self-monitoring behaviors such as evaluating their own work. *(Abstract only.)*

5. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Societies*). Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), 6. https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006
   Surveys and interviews with 666 people of mixed ages, not only students. Frequent AI use was linked to weaker critical thinking, partly through "cognitive offloading" (letting the tool do the thinking). Younger participants relied on AI more. The design is correlational. *(Abstract only.)*

6. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Scientific Reports*). Kestin, G., Miller, K., Klales, A., Milbourne, T., & Ponti, G. (2025). AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: An RCT introducing a novel research-based design in an authentic educational setting. *Scientific Reports, 15*, 17458. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6
   A randomized trial with **college** physics students. Students learned more, in less time, from a custom AI tutor built on teaching best practices than from an active-learning class. *(Abstract only.)*

7. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence*). Valeri, F., Nilsson, P., & Cederqvist, A.-M. (2024). Exploring students' experience of ChatGPT in STEM education. *Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence, 8*, 100360. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.caeai.2024.100360
   A survey of 450 upper-secondary STEM students in Sweden, plus interviews with 12 of them. Most used ChatGPT mainly to help them understand concepts, and some had strategies for spotting made-up answers. This study describes how students use it; it doesn't measure learning. *(Abstract plus the university's news release.)*

### Working paper and preprint (not peer reviewed)

8. **Working paper, not peer reviewed** (World Bank). De Simone, M. E., Tiberti, F. H., Barron Rodriguez, M. R., Manolio, F. A., Mosuro, W., & Dikoru, E. J. (2025). *From chalkboards to chatbots: Evaluating the impact of generative AI on learning outcomes in Nigeria* (Policy Research Working Paper No. 11125). World Bank. https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/wbkwbrwps/11125.htm
   A randomized trial with first-year senior secondary students in Nigeria. They used Microsoft Copilot for English over six weeks, with teacher support, and improved by 0.23 standard deviations in English. *Why it's here:* it's one of very few high school trials. *Watch for:* it hasn't been peer reviewed, and the World Bank's own page blocked me, so the link goes to an economics index record.

9. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed** (arXiv). Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (Version 2) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   Two lab experiments found no overall effect on learning. Students who used chatbots to *replace* learning activities covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used them to *complement* their studying understood more. *Why it's here:* it's the closest match I found to your "how they use it" sub-question. *Watch for:* it isn't peer reviewed, and the abstract doesn't say how old the participants were.

### Research organization reports

10. **Research organization report** (Pew Research Center). Sidoti, O., Park, E., & Gottfried, J. (2025, January 15). *About a quarter of U.S. teens have used ChatGPT for schoolwork – double the share in 2023.* Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork-double-the-share-in-2023/

11. **Research organization report** (Pew Research Center). McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI.* Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
    A survey of 1,458 U.S. teens in fall 2025: 54% had used chatbots for schoolwork, and about 1 in 10 did all or most of their schoolwork with them. 59% said students at their school use AI to cheat at least somewhat often. *Watch for:* the same as above, and I only read the report's first page.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plentiful:** meta-analyses and lab studies, mostly with **college students**.
- **Thin:** randomized studies of **high schoolers**. Only Bastani (math, Turkey) and the World Bank paper (English, Nigeria) fit. There are none from U.S. high schools.
- **Not found:** a study that directly compares high schoolers who check their work with those who just copy answers. Lehmann compares students who replaced learning with students who complemented it, but in a lab with participants of unstated age. Bastani compares two *tool designs*, not student habits.
- **Missing:** long-term studies. Most studies last from one session to a few weeks.

## Questions these results raise

- Whether "checking your work" is a habit students choose, or something a tool's design pushes them into. Bastani changed the tool, not the students.
- How far findings about college students apply to high schoolers.
- Lehmann and the World Bank paper both report that results differed by students' prior ability. That's a factor your position doesn't mention yet.
