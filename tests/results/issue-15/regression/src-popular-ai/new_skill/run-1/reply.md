## Sources

**Peer-reviewed articles.** All five of these count toward your 3 peer-reviewed sources. I confirmed each journal through Crossref.

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*)
   Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), Article e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   Read via: [OpenAlex record (abstract)](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1073/pnas.2422633122)
   This was a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey. Some got a standard ChatGPT-style tool ("GPT Base") and some got a tutor version built to protect learning ("GPT Tutor"). Both raised practice grades. Once access was removed, the GPT Base students scored 17% lower than students who never had access, and the tutor version largely avoided that drop. I read only the abstract.

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers & Education*, a meta-analysis)
   Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, Article 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   Read via: [OpenAlex record (abstract)](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224)
   This combines 69 experimental studies from 2022 to 2024. Overall, ChatGPT improved academic performance and lowered mental effort. The authors warn that weak methods in many of the studies call for caution, and they recommend separating the quality of students' output from actual learning gains. I read only the abstract.

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*)
   Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   Read via: [arXiv preprint version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09315)
   This was a randomized experiment with 117 university students on a writing task. Students with ChatGPT improved their essay scores more than the other groups, but their knowledge gain and transfer weren't significantly different. They also showed fewer self-monitoring processes, such as evaluating their own work.

4. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers in Human Behavior*)
   Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, Article 108386. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386
   Read via: [TUM research portal](https://portal.fis.tum.de/en/publications/cognitive-ease-at-a-cost-llms-reduce-mental-effort-but-compromise/)
   This study randomly assigned 91 university students to research a science question with either ChatGPT or Google. The ChatGPT users reported less mental effort, and the quality of their reasoning and justifications was lower.

5. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Scientific Reports*)
   Kestin, G., Miller, K., Klales, A., Milbourne, T., & Ponti, G. (2025). AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: An RCT introducing a novel research-based design in an authentic educational setting. *Scientific Reports, 15*, Article 17458. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6
   Read via: [PubMed Central full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)
   This was a randomized crossover study with 194 analyzed students in a Harvard physics course. Students using a purpose-built AI tutor at home learned more, in less time, than they did in an active-learning class session. The authors say the result depended on the tutor's design and may not hold for higher-order tasks.

**Preprints, not yet peer reviewed.** These are included because they get closest to your "how they use it" sub-question. Treat their findings as provisional.

6. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed**
   Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047v2) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   This covers university students learning Python, in two lab experiments and a field study at a Dutch university. Overall there was no effect on learning. Students who used the chatbot to generate solutions covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who asked it for explanations understood more. Watch for: it isn't peer reviewed, and the participants weren't high schoolers.

7. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed**
   Vanzo, A., Pal Chowdhury, S., & Sachan, M. (2024). *GPT-4 as a homework tutor can improve student engagement and learning outcomes* (arXiv:2409.15981) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15981
   This was a randomized trial in four high school English-as-a-second-language classes, where GPT-4 tutoring sessions replaced some homework. The authors report bigger grammar gains and higher engagement. Watch for: it's not peer reviewed (it was submitted to a conference), it's small, and the page doesn't give a sample size.

**Research organization reports.** These are surveys, not studies of learning. They're useful for how teens use chatbots, not for whether it helps them.

8. **Research organization report**
   Sidoti, O., Park, E., & Gottfried, J. (2025, January 15). *About a quarter of U.S. teens have used ChatGPT for schoolwork – double the share in 2023*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork/
   This is a national survey of 1,391 teens from fall 2024. 26% had used ChatGPT for schoolwork, up from 13% in 2023. 54% said using it to research topics is acceptable, compared with 29% for solving math problems and 18% for writing essays.

9. **Research organization report**
   McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
   This survey of 1,458 teens from fall 2025 found that 54% had used chatbots for schoolwork help and 1 in 10 do all or most of their schoolwork with one. 59% said students at their school use AI to cheat at least somewhat often.

## What I found and didn't find

- **There's plenty of evidence on college students and very little on high schoolers.** Only Bastani is a peer-reviewed study of high school students. Vanzo is a high school study but a preprint, and everything else involves university students.
- **No study I found compares "checking work" with "getting answers" directly.** The closest are Lehmann (explanations versus solutions) and Bastani (a guarded tutor versus open access). Both draw their lines a bit differently from yours.
- **Long-term evidence is thin.** Most studies measure learning within days or weeks.
- **I found no study of U.S. high schoolers' learning outcomes,** only surveys about their use.

## Questions these results raise

- Several studies divide students by the *tool's design* rather than by the *student's choice*. That bears on whether "how kids use it" is something students decide or something the tool decides.
- Bastani, Deng, and Fan all separate how well students do on a task from what they actually learn. That distinction matters for how you'd measure "help" and "hurt."
