**Peer-reviewed articles**

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Frontiers in Developmental Psychology*)
   Vereecke, J., Vanherle, R., Nassen, L.-M., Boen, H., Eggermont, S., Beullens, K., Vandenbosch, L., & Schreurs, L. (2026). More than disconnecting? School smartphone bans and adolescents' social wellbeing. *Frontiers in Developmental Psychology, 4*, Article 1796471. https://doi.org/10.3389/fdpys.2026.1796471
   This study followed 960 students at one Belgian secondary school across eight surveys in the year after the school switched to a total ban, breaks included. Loneliness, friend support and cyberbullying victimization didn't change in the short term. Over the year, loneliness rose modestly. There was no comparison school, so the authors say the change can't be blamed on the ban.

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Journal of Youth and Adolescence*)
   Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12966246/ (DOI 10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6, taken from this page; I didn't open the DOI link itself)
   This study surveyed 1,398 students aged about 16 at 24 Dutch schools, comparing partial bans with full bans. Bullying outcomes, in school and online, didn't differ. Full bans were linked to weaker student–teacher connectedness and, for girls, a weaker sense of school belonging.

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Journal of Behavioral Addictions*)
   King, D. L., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., & Wade, T. (2024). "Phones off while school's on": Evaluating problematic phone use and the social, wellbeing, and academic effects of banning phones in schools. *Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 13*(4), 913–922. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11737419/ (DOI 10.1556/2006.2024.00058, taken from this page)
   This was a preregistered comparison of ban and no-ban schools in South Australia, with 1,282 students in Grades 7–12 followed for one month. School belonging didn't differ between the groups. Bullying fell in both groups, and it was slightly higher in the ban group.

4. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Applied Economic Analysis*)
   Beneito, P., & Vicente-Chirivella, Ó. (2022). Banning mobile phones in schools: Evidence from regional-level policies in Spain. *Applied Economic Analysis, 30*(90), 153–175. https://doi.org/10.1108/AEA-05-2021-0112
   This study compared two Spanish regions that banned phones in 2015 with regions that didn't, using officially reported bullying cases. The authors report that bullying incidence fell by around 9.5% to 18% in the regions with bans. Cyberbullying wasn't analysed separately.

5. **Peer-reviewed article** (*The Lancet Regional Health – Europe*)
   Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, Article 101211. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11984610/ (DOI 10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211; that DOI link didn't load for me, but this PubMed Central copy of the paper did)
   This study covered 1,227 students aged 12–15 at 30 English schools. In restrictive schools, students used their phones less during the school day, but total phone and social media use and wellbeing were no different. The paper didn't measure face-to-face interaction or bullying. It names both as topics for future research.

6. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Journal of Human Resources*, published online ahead of print)
   Abrahamsson, S. (2026). Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health. *Journal of Human Resources*. Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.0224-13403R2
   This study looked at middle schools that adopted bans at different times. According to the abstract, bans "lower bullying for both genders" and reduce girls' use of psychological health care. **The full text is paywalled, so I've only read the abstract.** Search results describe an earlier version of the paper as being about Norway, but the abstract I read doesn't name the country.

**Other credible sources (not peer-reviewed)**

7. **Working paper, not peer reviewed** (National Bureau of Economic Research)
   Allcott, H., Baron, E. J., Dee, T., Duckworth, A. L., Gentzkow, M., & Jacob, B. (2026). *The effects of school phone bans: National evidence from lockable pouches* (NBER Working Paper No. 35132). National Bureau of Economic Research. https://www.nber.org/papers/w35132
   This is a national U.S. study of schools using locked phone pouches. Disciplinary incidents rose in the first year and then faded, and there was "no clear evidence" of change in perceived online bullying.
   *Why it's here:* it's the largest U.S. study I found, and it's one of the few that includes high schools. Keep in mind that it hasn't been peer reviewed yet, and part of its data came from the company that sells the pouches. I read only the abstract.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plenty:** studies of bullying, belonging and wellbeing, especially from Europe and Australia.
- **Thin:** studies that directly measure face-to-face talk. None of the sources I opened observed or counted conversations. The closest measures are related ones like belonging, loneliness and friend support, plus student self-reports in the qualitative study I couldn't open.
- **Not found:** any study that tests whether conflict moves to after school. Several papers say cyberbullying often happens outside school hours, but none tracked whether it increased there after a ban.
- **Skewed toward younger students:** many samples are aged 12–15. Few studies focus on U.S. high schoolers.

## Questions these results raise

- Some studies measure bullying in general and others measure cyberbullying. "Conflict" in your position could mean either one.
- Belonging, loneliness and friend support aren't the same thing as face-to-face talk. You'll need to decide whether they count as evidence for your first sub-question.
- Some effects change over time, such as the first-year rise in discipline in the NBER study. How long a ban has been in place may matter to your argument.
