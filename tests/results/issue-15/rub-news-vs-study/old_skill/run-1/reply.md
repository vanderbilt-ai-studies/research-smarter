Welcome back. You've finished Phases 0–4 and you're testing sources you found yourself. You're asking which physical characteristics let tardigrades survive extreme temperatures, and your position is that drying out into a tun state is the main thing protecting their cells. Thanks for confirming those haven't changed.

You've already given your own assessment, and you've noticed two real strengths. A well-known science news site has editors and some fact-checking, which puts it well above a blog. Quoting the scientists shows the journalist actually talked to the people who did the work.

The weakness is that the article is still **secondhand**. Measured against the checklist:

- **Methods:** The article tells you what the scientists found. It can't show you how they found it: which species, what radiation doses, which experiments, what controls. A lab report is judged on exactly that detail.
- **Venue:** The news site isn't peer-reviewed. The scientists' quotes are short excerpts the journalist chose, and they aren't the scientists' published, reviewed claims. So the article doesn't count toward your 4 peer-reviewed sources.
- **Corroboration:** A news story about a new discovery almost always reports on a specific published study. When the study itself is available, that's what you should cite, especially in CSE style for a biology lab report.

So it doesn't meet the bar as evidence for a research finding. It's still useful as a lead to the real source. Look in the article for a link, a journal name or the lead scientist's name, then find the original paper. Once you have the paper, compare what it says with the article's summary. News write-ups sometimes make a finding sound bigger than it is.

There's also a fit question. Your research question is about extreme **temperatures**, and this protein is about **radiation**. How do you see it connecting to your question, or to your claim that the tun state is the main mechanism?
