> **Phase 1 of 5 · Explore and choose a direction**

Welcome back. When you left off, you'd finished intake for your 8-page argumentative sociology paper (APA, at least 3 peer-reviewed sources). You'd also chosen the downsides of school phone bans as your angle, mostly because you can't stand your school's ban. The last step in Phase 1 is writing a draft question, and now you have one.

Here's what's good about it: it's arguable, which this assignment needs. Thoughtful people really do disagree about phone bans, and you clearly care about the answer. That kind of energy carries you through 8 pages.

The biggest problem is that **the question already contains its answer.** "Why are school phone bans a terrible idea?" has decided the verdict before you've read anything. That causes trouble in two ways:

- **Your research will be lopsided.** A question built this way only sends you looking for evidence that agrees with you. You can still end up arguing that bans are bad. But the argument gets stronger if it comes from an open question, because then you've actually weighed the other side.
- **"Terrible" is vague.** Terrible for whom, and judged by what: grades, mental health, safety in emergencies, students' rights? A reader can't tell what you're trying to find out.

You still get to take a side. That's what the thesis is for, and it comes later, once you've seen the evidence. The question is where you're still genuinely unsure.

So try this: **if you didn't already know how you felt, what would you actually want to find out about phone bans?** Write a new version of your question in your own words, and tell me what you're judging bans *by*.
