## Preliminary briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Do they hurt learning?** RAND (2023) found less achievement growth in four-day districts. A Missouri state analysis (2024) and an Oklahoma study of 411 districts found no significant difference. An Oregon State study found that the gap mostly disappeared when districts kept instructional hours high.
- **Do they help with staffing?** In one EdWeek Research Center survey, two-thirds of educators said a four-day week would make them more willing to take a job. Turnover data is less clear. In Texas, turnover fell by about 2.7 percentage points. A Colorado study (Bowser, 2025) found no significant effect on turnover for teachers, principals, or support staff. In Brighton, Colorado, teachers became *less* likely to return after the switch.
- **Are the savings worth it?** The Education Commission of the States puts typical savings at 0.4%–2.5% of a district's budget. It also notes that programs for the off day can cancel out much of that.

**Major voices**
- **RAND Corporation:** several multi-state studies covering achievement, parents, and staffing.
- **University economists:** for example, Fischer and Argyle's peer-reviewed 2018 study of juvenile crime in Colorado.
- **Education Commission of the States:** cost analyses.
- **State education departments:** Missouri, for example.
- **Education Week:** tracks the whole debate.

**Patterns**
- Most studies compare four-day districts with similar five-day districts before and after the switch, using state data.
- Most four-day districts are rural, so findings may not carry over to suburbs or cities.
- Where savings show up, they come from transportation, food service, and building operations, not teaching.
- Why districts switch has changed. Many cut costs during the Great Recession. More recently, districts cite teacher recruitment and burnout.

**Open questions**
- What the off day means for families: child care, food, and supervision. Some studies use student self-reports and conflict with each other.
- Long-term effects, as the schedule spreads to more than 2,100 schools in 26 states.
- How districts actually make the decision, and why some reverse it.
