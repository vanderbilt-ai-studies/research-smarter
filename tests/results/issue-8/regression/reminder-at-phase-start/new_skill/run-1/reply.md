## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Achievement.** A 2023 RAND study found slower achievement growth in four-day districts. An Oregon State study found no significant gap when districts kept enough total instructional time. A 2024 Missouri state study found no significant difference. ([EdWeek explainer, Apr. 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04)) The disagreement is less about whether there's a cost and more about **when** there is one.
- **Teachers.** Districts often adopt four-day weeks to recruit and keep teachers, and the evidence on that is mixed:
  - **Texas:** a study covering 17 years found turnover fell about 2.7 percentage points, but more than 1 in 5 teachers still left each year. ([EdWeek, Jan. 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/do-4-day-school-weeks-attract-and-retain-better-teachers-what-the-largest-study-yet-says/2026/01))
  - **Oregon:** turnover *rose* 2.3 points right after adoption and rose again five to nine years later, while pay in four-day districts fell further behind. ([FutureEd, Oct. 2024](https://www.future-ed.org/research-notes-does-a-four-day-school-week-improve-teacher-retention/))
- **Life outside school.** A peer-reviewed Colorado study found youth crime rose nearly 20%, mostly property crime ([Chalkbeat, 2018](https://chalkbeat.org/2018/4/2/21104689/four-day-school-weeks-a-nationwide-symptom-of-tight-budgets-lead-to-more-youth-crime-study-finds)). An Oklahoma study found bullying fell about 39% and fights about 31%, with no change in attendance (EdWeek explainer, above).

**Major voices**
- **RAND Corporation:** surveys and achievement studies, mainly in Idaho, New Mexico and Oklahoma.
- **Economists using state data:** Ainsworth, Penner and Liu in Oregon; Lawson in Texas; Fischer and Argyle in Colorado.
- **Policy trackers:** the [National Conference of State Legislatures](https://www.ncsl.org/education/four-day-school-week-overview/ftag/yhf4eb9d17) and the Education Commission of the States.
- **District leaders and parents:** in RAND's 2021 survey, nearly three-quarters of parents of elementary students were very satisfied.

**Patterns**
- **Small savings.** Districts typically save about 0.4% to 2.5% of their budget (NCSL; [K-12 Dive, 2022](https://www.k12dive.com/news/4-ways-transitioning-to-a-4-day-school-week-impacts-districts/628601)).
- **Results depend on setting.** Effects look smaller in rural districts and larger in suburban ones, and the amount of total instructional time a district keeps seems to matter a great deal.
- **Popular but costly?** People who live with the schedule tend to like it, while the measured outcomes are often neutral or negative.
- **A lot of state-specific research.** Many studies use one state's administrative data, so findings may not transfer from one state to another.

**Open questions**
- **Long-term effects.** Do effects grow, fade or reverse over many years? The Oregon turnover pattern suggests they can change over time.
- **The fifth day.** What do students actually do on it? Child care, extra programs and some districts requiring teachers to work that day all complicate the picture.
- **Families.** Effects on parents' jobs are understudied. One 2019 study found an 11% drop in employment for married mothers (K-12 Dive).
- **The newest wave.** Texas went from 8 four-day districts to at least 195 in six years, and the research hasn't caught up yet.
