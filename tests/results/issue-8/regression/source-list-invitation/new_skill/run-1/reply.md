  This study compares two Spanish regions that banned phones in schools in 2014–15 with regions that didn't, using officially reported bullying cases among ages 6–17.   This is a Dutch study of 195 adolescents (average age 16) surveyed six times a day for 17 days during the national school phone ban. - Bravo, L., & Elupula, K. (2025). The effect of a mobile phone ban on perceived academic and social life: A qualitative analysis. *Open Journal of Social Sciences, 13*(10), 83–96. https://doi.org/10.4236/jss.2025.1310005

## What I found and didn't find

- **Bullying during school:** There's a fair amount of evidence here, mostly from large-scale data in Spain and Norway, and it's mostly for middle-school ages.
- **Face-to-face interaction:** The evidence is thin. It's almost all students' self-reports from single schools, with nothing observational.
- **Conflict moving to after school:** I found no study that directly measures whether conflict shifts to after school. The closest are a claim that cyberbullying mostly happens outside school hours (from snippets of the Campbell review, not verified), the SMART Schools finding that phone use moves outside school, and the homework-time figure in Bravo & Elupula.
- **High school specifically:** Few studies look only at high schoolers. Several of the strongest ones are about ages 12–15.
- **Long-term effects:** Almost nothing follows students for more than a year or two.

## Questions these results raise

- Several sources measure where *phone use* goes, not where *conflict* goes. Whether those are the same thing matters for your position.
- The Florida suspension spike is a kind of school-day conflict caused by enforcing the ban itself, which your current framing doesn't cover.
- Officially reported bullying and students' own reports may tell different stories.
