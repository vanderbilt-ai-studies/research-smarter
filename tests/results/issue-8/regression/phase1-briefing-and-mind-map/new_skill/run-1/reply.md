### Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Does it hurt learning?** A multi-state study by Morton, Thompson and Kuhfeld (2024, *Economics of Education Review*) found slightly slower test-score growth during the school year: about −0.05 standard deviations in math and −0.06 in reading. The effect was larger in non-rural schools than rural ones ([K-12 Dive](https://www.k12dive.com/news/4-day-school-week-impact-reading-math/712528/)). Other studies find no detectable effect, so researchers disagree about how much this matters.
- **Does it help keep teachers?** The largest study so far, Cade Lawson's study of Texas, found teacher turnover dropped about 2.7 percentage points, but more than 1 in 5 teachers still left each year. Districts that switched did not attract more experienced or better-educated teachers ([Education Week, 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/do-4-day-school-weeks-attract-and-retain-better-teachers-what-the-largest-study-yet-says/2026/01)). Search results also mention an Oregon study that found turnover *rose*. I couldn't open that study, so treat it as unconfirmed.
- **Is it worth the money?** The National Conference of State Legislatures (NCSL) says the most a district could save is about 5.4%, but real savings average 0.4–2.5% ([NCSL](https://www.ncsl.org/education/four-day-school-week-overview)).

**Major voices**
- Economists working with large test-score and administrative datasets. Emily Morton's name comes up again and again.
- Research organizations like RAND, whose 2021 brief *Can Four Equal Five?* combined surveys with outcome data. I found the brief but couldn't read its text, so I'm not reporting what it says.
- Policy groups like NCSL that track adoption: about 850 districts in 24 states, mostly rural.

**Patterns**
- Most strong studies compare test scores before and after a district switches. Outcomes that don't show up in test scores, like family strain, sleep, and what students think, rely much more on surveys.
- Results vary a lot by state and by how districts handle lost hours: some make the other four days longer, and some use the fifth day for activities.
- Some effects show up outside school. A Colorado study by Fischer and Argyle linked the four-day week to a rise in youth crime of nearly 20%, mostly property crime ([Chalkbeat, 2018](https://chalkbeat.org/2018/4/2/21104689/four-day-school-weeks-a-nationwide-symptom-of-tight-budgets-lead-to-more-youth-crime-study-finds)).

**Open questions**
- Long-term outcomes, like graduation, college, and jobs, are barely studied.
- It's unclear whether the perk of attracting teachers fades once neighboring districts switch too.
- Few studies ask students and teachers what the off day is actually like.
- Little research looks at why some districts have gone back to five days.
