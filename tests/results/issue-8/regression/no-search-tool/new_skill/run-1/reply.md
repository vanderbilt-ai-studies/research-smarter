## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Money vs. learning.** Districts usually switch to save money. The main argument is whether the savings are worth it. One Oklahoma study found no detectable effect on test scores and savings of about 2% of a district's budget, mostly from transportation, food service and operations. I only read that study's abstract because the page blocked me.
- **Does achievement suffer?** Early work in Colorado found little harm and even a slight positive relationship with reading and math scores ([Anderson & Walker, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1162/edfp_a_00165)). Newer and larger studies find small negative effects. A multi-state, student-level study found lower reading scores and slower growth in math and reading. The effects were larger in non-rural schools and for female students ([Morton, Thompson & Kuhfeld, 2024](https://doi.org/10.26300/p96h-8a41)).
- **A perk for recruiting teachers?** Some districts now use the schedule to attract teachers. A case study of one rural district reports that it filled all its vacancies after switching ([Hollingworth & An, 2025](https://doi.org/10.55533/2643-9662.1504)). A study of an urban district (Nowak, Perrone & Smith, *Real Estate Economics*; abstract only) found lower retention among moderately experienced teachers.

**Major voices**
- Economists and education-policy researchers, especially Paul Thompson, who appears on many of the studies, and Emily Morton
- The RAND Corporation's 2021 report *Does Four Equal Five?* (Kilburn et al.). It's the largest qualitative study, but the page blocked me, so I've only seen its description.

**Patterns**
- **Mostly rural, mostly about money.** The schedule is mostly found in small rural districts, and districts usually adopt it for financial reasons. On average these schools have about 148 school days, compared with about 180 in five-day schools, even though their days are longer ([Thompson et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1162/edfp_a_00316)).
- **Test scores dominate.** Most effect studies compare test scores before and after a district switches.
- **Much of the research isn't peer-reviewed.** A 2025 scoping review counted 123 studies and found 75% were "gray literature," meaning reports rather than peer-reviewed journal articles. That matters for your peer-reviewed source requirement. I read only that review's abstract (Grant et al., *Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis*).

**Open questions**
- **The off day.** Little research looks at what happens to kids and families on the fifth day. One Colorado study found that juvenile arrests for property crime rose about 73% ([Fischer & Argyle, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econedurev.2018.03.010)).
- **Health and engagement.** Effects on student health and engagement have barely been studied. One Oregon study found more PE time in four-day schools and no difference in obesity rates ([Tomayko et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1123/jpah.2019-0648)).
- **Teacher claims.** Whether the schedule actually helps with teacher shortages is still mostly backed by anecdotes and case studies.
