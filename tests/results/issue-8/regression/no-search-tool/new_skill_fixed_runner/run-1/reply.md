## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Learning vs. everything else.** Test-score studies mostly find small negative effects. The RAND report *Does Four Equal Five?* (2021) found that achievement grew more slowly after districts switched, yet families and staff reported high satisfaction and valued the extra time together.
- **Does it actually solve the problems districts adopt it for?** Districts started switching mainly to save money, and more recently to attract teachers. Emily Morton's Oklahoma study put the savings at about 2% of a district's budget. Several 2026 studies, including Morton and DeArmond's and Ainsworth, Liu and Penner's work on Oregon, find no consistent gain in keeping teachers. The Oregon study found turnover went *up* by about 2 percentage points.
- **What happens on the day off.** Studies link the schedule to more food insecurity and changes in juvenile crime. These effects differ between rural and non-rural areas.

**Major voices**
A small group of education economists does most of the work: Paul Thompson (Oregon State), Emily Morton, and Mary Beth Walker with D. Mark Anderson, who wrote an early, widely cited Colorado study. RAND and Brown University's Annenberg Institute (EdWorkingPapers) publish a lot of it. Education Finance and Policy and Economics of Education Review are the main journals.

**Patterns**
- Most studies compare districts before and after they switch to districts that didn't switch (a method called "difference-in-differences").
- A 2025 scoping review counted 123 studies. About 74% looked at rural communities and about 75% were "gray literature" (reports and dissertations rather than journal articles). I could only read its abstract, not the full text.
- Results differ by state. Thompson and colleagues (2021) found that four-day schedules average about 148 days a year, compared with 180 in typical five-day schools, and that the schedules vary a lot in how they're built.

**Open questions**
- How families arrange child care, and what happens to parents' work.
- Teacher well-being, beyond whether teachers stay.
- Indigenous communities. A 2026 University of Kansas dissertation on Lakota families describes itself as the first study of a reservation-based district.
- Whether the effects grow larger over time.
