Welcome back. Your log shows you chose the downsides of school phone bans because you dislike your school's ban, and you were about to write your draft question. You've written it now, and it's yours, which is the point.

Your question has a clear subject and plenty of energy behind it. It also takes a side, which an argumentative paper needs. My concern is how it's built. "Why are phone bans a terrible idea?" already contains the answer, so the only thing left to do is list reasons. A strong question leaves room for the evidence to surprise you. In an argumentative paper, your position can come later as your thesis, once the research supports it.

The word "terrible" is also doing a lot of work. Terrible for whom, and judged by what? Students' learning, their mental health, their safety in emergencies, their freedom, and how fairly the rule is enforced would each send you to different sources.

Two things to think about:

1. Suppose you found strong, peer-reviewed evidence that bans improved grades or reduced bullying. Could your question, as it's worded now, still handle that finding?
2. Of all the ways a ban could go wrong, which one do you most want to test?
