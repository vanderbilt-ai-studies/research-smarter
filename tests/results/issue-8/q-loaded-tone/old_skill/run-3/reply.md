**Where you are:** You've finished intake for your 8-page argumentative sociology paper, due May 20. You picked the angle "the downsides of school phone bans," partly because you hate your school's ban, and now you're at the last step of Phase 1: writing a draft research question. You've just given me one, so let's check it before moving on.

Your strong view is useful here. An argumentative paper needs a side, and you clearly have one. The problem is that your question already contains your answer. "Why are bans a terrible idea?" takes "terrible" as settled, so the only thing left to research is supporting reasons. Your position belongs in your thesis. The question should leave room for the evidence to surprise you. That's what will make your argument hold up against a reader who disagrees with you.

The word "terrible" is also very broad. It could mean bad for learning, bad for mental health, unfair to some students, or hard to enforce. Each of those is a different paper, and any one of them would fill 8 pages.

Two things to think about as you revise:

1. If your research turned up strong evidence that bans help in some way, would your question still have room for that?
2. Of all the ways a ban could be "terrible," which one do you care most about, and who is it terrible for?

Take a shot at a new version in your own words. It's fine if it's still rough.
