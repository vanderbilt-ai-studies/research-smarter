**Phase 1 of 5 · Explore and choose a direction**

Welcome back. Last time you finished intake for your 8-page psychology literature review (APA, due May 20, 2027) and picked the angle "sleep and how well people think." Your reason was that you're always tired during exams and want to know if it matters. You were about to write your draft research question, and now you have.

**What's working:** You've named the specific things you want to connect (sleep duration, sleep quality, and memory) instead of a vague "sleep affects thinking." Keeping duration and quality separate is a good instinct, since they're not the same thing. It also suits a literature review, because it gives you a way to sort studies.

**Where it's still loose:** "Memory" and "adults" both cover a lot of ground. Memory could mean remembering what you studied last week, holding several numbers in your head while you solve a problem, or recalling a conversation. "Adults" covers everyone from 19-year-olds to 85-year-olds. An 8-page review that tries to cover all of that will end up skimming. A broad question also tends to sit right in the most crowded part of the field, which makes it harder for your review to add anything.

Two questions to help you tighten it:

1. When you picture the "memory" in your question, what kind do you actually mean?
2. Your interest began with being tired during exams, but this version doesn't mention students or exams. Did you widen it on purpose?

Answer in your own words, and then you can decide whether to revise the question or keep it as is.
