## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Do students learn less?** RAND compared districts in Idaho, New Mexico and Oklahoma and found achievement grew more slowly in four-day districts ([Kilburn et al., RAND, 2021](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA373-1.html)). A six-state study using NWEA test data found small negative effects on reading and math growth, larger in non-rural schools ([Morton, Thompson & Kuhfeld, EdWorkingPaper 22-630](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai22-630)). That's the 2023 working-paper version. Search results say a version later appeared in *Economics of Education Review*, but I haven't confirmed that.
- **Does it help with teachers?** In RAND's interviews, leaders and teachers believed it helps with recruiting. A December 2025 working paper on 17 years of Texas data found teacher turnover fell 2.7 percentage points, but incoming teachers were no more experienced or educated ([Lawson, EdWorkingPaper 25-1372](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai25-1372)).
- **Does it save money?** The savings look modest. NCSL's policy brief puts the range at 0.4% to 2.5% ([NCSL, 2023](https://www.ncsl.org/education/four-day-school-week-overview)), and RAND called the savings "relatively small." A *National Tax Journal* study by Paul Thompson (2021) reportedly found 1–2%. It showed up in search results but I couldn't open it, so check it before using it.

**Major voices**
- Economist Paul Thompson (Oregon State) appears on many of the studies.
- NWEA researchers Emily Morton and Megan Kuhfeld work from a large test-score database.
- RAND did the biggest mixed-methods study.
- The University of Oregon's HEDCO Institute did a 2025 systematic review of 11 studies and found little evidence of benefits for achievement, attendance, behavior or graduation ([UO news release](https://news.uoregon.edu/node/13412)).

**Patterns**
- Most studies compare four-day and five-day districts with similar statistical designs, using test scores, budgets or employment records.
- The research is concentrated in a few states, mostly in the West and Midwest.
- Results often depend on how much instructional time the shorter week keeps.
- Parent support tends to be high even where test results are mixed.

**Open questions**
- **The day off.** One peer-reviewed study linked Colorado four-day weeks to about a 20% rise in juvenile offenses, mostly property crime ([Fischer & Argyle, *Economics of Education Review*, 2018](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecoedu/v64y2018icp31-39.html)). An Oregon study found higher food insecurity and drug use among four-day students ([Tomayko et al., *Journal of School Health*, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8595551)). Both cover a single state.
- **Gaps.** Childcare costs for working families, what students themselves think, how the schedule is designed, and why some districts go back to five days have all had little study.
- **Growth.** The schedule keeps spreading: more than 2,100 schools in 26 states used it in 2024–25 ([Education Week, 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04)).
