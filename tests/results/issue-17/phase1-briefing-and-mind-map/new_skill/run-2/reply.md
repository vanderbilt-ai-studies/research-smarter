## First look at the research

**Key debates**
- **Test scores.** A University of Oregon [review of 11 studies (2025)](https://news.uoregon.edu/node/13412) found little evidence that the schedule improves achievement, attendance, behavior or graduation, and some results were negative. The debate is about why: the four-day week itself, or the hours that get cut. [Journalist's Resource](https://journalistsresource.org/education/four-day-school-week-research/) (Harvard's Shorenstein Center) reports that drops show up mainly where total instructional time falls.
- **Teachers.** Districts often say the schedule helps them hire and keep teachers, but the data disagree. An Oregon working paper found turnover *rose*, by about 4 percentage points five or more years after the switch ([Education Week, 2024](https://edweek.org/leadership/4-day-school-weeks-and-their-surprising-effect-on-teacher-turnover/2024/10)). A Texas study found it fell by about 2.7 points ([Education Week, 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/do-4-day-school-weeks-attract-and-retain-better-teachers-what-the-largest-study-yet-says/2026/01)). Both are news reports on working papers that haven't been peer reviewed yet.
- **Money.** Saving money is a common reason to switch. RAND's study of Idaho, New Mexico and Oklahoma, [*Does Four Equal Five?*](https://doi.org/10.7249/RR-A373-1) (2021), found the savings were relatively small.

**Major voices**
- **Paul Thompson's group at Oregon State.** Its work includes a peer-reviewed study of [early elementary achievement](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecresq.2022.12.009) in *Early Childhood Research Quarterly* (2022).
- **RAND**, including the Kilburn et al. report and economist Jason Ward.
- **The HEDCO Institute** at the University of Oregon, which ran the 2025 review.
- **Economists** publishing in *Economics of Education Review*.

**Patterns**
- Most research is about rural districts and test scores.
- Many studies compare districts before and after they switch, using state data.
- Results often depend on how many total hours students keep, their grade level, and where they live.
- Results also differ by student group. The Oregon State elementary study found larger negative effects for students who were already high achievers, and no significant harm for low-income students.

**Open questions**
- **Life outside school.** Little is known about what happens on the fifth day. A few peer-reviewed studies exist:
  - [Fischer and Argyle (2018)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:ecoedu:v:64:y:2018:i:c:p:31-39) linked the schedule to about a 20% rise in juvenile offenses in rural Colorado.
  - [Tomayko et al. (2021)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8595551), in the *Journal of School Health*, linked it to higher food insecurity among teens.
- **Families.** The effects on childcare and families are barely studied.
- **Teachers over time.** It's still unsettled how teacher effects change over the long run.
