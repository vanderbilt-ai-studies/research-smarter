## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Learning vs. logistics.** Supporters point to lower costs, easier teacher hiring and happy families. Critics point to lost learning time. RAND's 2021 report *Does Four Equal Five?* covers both sides. It found small cost savings, no gain in attendance, and test scores that grew more slowly than in similar five-day districts. It also found the schedule helped with teacher recruitment, and families and staff were generally satisfied with it ([RAND](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA373-1.html)).
- **Inside vs. outside school.** Fischer and Argyle studied rural Colorado and found juvenile offenses rose about 20% after districts switched, mostly property crime (*Economics of Education Review*, 2018; [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecoedu/v64y2018icp31-39.html)). An Oklahoma study by Morton (2022) reportedly found less bullying and fighting at school. I only saw that study described secondhand, in a [Journalist's Resource overview](https://journalistsresource.org/education/four-day-school-week-research/).

**Major voices**
- A small group of education economists writes much of this research, especially Paul Thompson (Oregon State) and Emily Morton (formerly NWEA, also a RAND co-author).
- RAND and NWEA, the testing organization whose student data many of these studies use.
- Rural district leaders and state legislatures, who make the actual adoption decisions.

**Patterns**
- Most studies compare districts before and after they switch, using test scores. Few measure what students or families actually experience.
- Effects tend to be small and vary by setting. Morton, Thompson and Kuhfeld found lower spring reading scores and slower within-year growth, with larger effects in non-rural schools ([working paper version](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai22-630)).
- Thompson and colleagues looked at early elementary students in Oregon. They found minimal average differences in 3rd-grade scores, but higher-achieving students were more negatively affected (*Early Childhood Research Quarterly*, 2022; [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9979633)).
- The schedule is spreading fast. One estimate puts it at more than 2,100 schools in 26 states. In Texas, the number of schools using it rose from 30 in 2020–21 to 506 in 2024–25 ([Journalist's Resource](https://journalistsresource.org/education/four-day-school-week-research/)).

**Open questions**
- What the fifth day means for working parents, childcare and kids who rely on school meals.
- Whether hiring gains last once neighboring districts adopt the schedule too.
- How much schedule design (total hours, what happens on the off day) explains the mixed results.
- Most evidence comes from a few states, so it's unclear how far it applies elsewhere.
