## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Does it hurt learning?** Most recent studies find small average losses. A 2021 Oregon study (Thompson, *Journal of Public Economics*) found drops in math and reading. A multi-state working paper (Morton, Thompson & Kuhfeld) found weaker reading gains across six states. An older Colorado study (Anderson & Walker, 2015) found no harm and even some math gains.
- **Does it help hire and keep teachers?** This is often why districts switch, but results conflict. An Oregon working paper found turnover rose after the switch. A Texas working paper (2026) found it fell slightly. A Missouri analysis found no lasting effect.
- **Is it worth the money?** Districts often adopt the schedule to save money, but estimates put the savings at only about 1–2%.

**Major voices**
- **Economists who study education policy:** Paul Thompson (Oregon State) and Emily Morton (NWEA) wrote much of the achievement research.
- **The RAND Corporation:** its 2021 report *Does Four Equal Five?* (Kilburn et al.) combined test scores with surveys and site visits.
- **Rural education researchers:** they published parent and staff surveys, mostly in *The Rural Educator*.

**Patterns**
- **Instructional time:** studies keep finding that losses are concentrated in districts that cut total hours the most. Four-day schedules average about 148 days a year, compared with 180 on a five-day week (Thompson et al., *Education Finance and Policy*, 2021).
- **Rural difference:** rural students sometimes fare better than small-town or suburban students on the same schedule.
- **Popular despite the research:** parents and students report high satisfaction even where test-score research is negative.
- **Mostly quasi-experiments:** most studies compare districts before and after they switch. Districts aren't randomly assigned to a schedule.

**Open questions**
- **The fifth day:** what students actually do on the day off, and who looks after them.
- **Families:** effects on child care and parents' jobs. Researchers raise these concerns, but I found almost no direct evidence.
- **Behavior and safety:** results point in different directions. A Colorado study (Fischer & Argyle, 2018) found juvenile offenses rose about 20%, mostly property crime. An Oklahoma study found fewer fights and less bullying at school.
- **Spread:** whether any benefits fade as more districts adopt the schedule. About 2,100 schools in 26 states used it as of 2024–25, according to Education Week.
