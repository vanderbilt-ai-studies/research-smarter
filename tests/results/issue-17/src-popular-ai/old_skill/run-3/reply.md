**Peer-reviewed journal articles** (I confirmed each journal in the Crossref database. Together they meet your requirement of at least 3 peer-reviewed sources.)

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), Article e2422633122. [EconPapers record with abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nas:journl:v:122:y:2025:p:e2422633122). The *PNAS* page blocked me, so I worked from this abstract.
   This was a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students. Some had a standard ChatGPT-style tool, some had a "tutor" version built to protect learning, and some had no AI. Both AI groups scored higher during practice. Once the AI was taken away, though, the standard-tool group scored 17% lower than students who never had it, and the tutor version largely avoided that drop.

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. The journal page blocked me. Here is the [arXiv preprint version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09315), which lists this journal DOI as related. Cite the journal version, not the preprint.

The ChatGPT group improved their essay scores more, but knowledge gain and transfer didn't differ between groups. How students regulated their own learning did differ.

4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). The publisher's page wouldn't open for me, so I have no link. I confirmed the citation in Crossref.
   This study compared college students researching a question with ChatGPT and with Google Search. I couldn't read the abstract. Secondhand descriptions say the ChatGPT group reported less mental effort but gave weaker reasoning, so check that against the paper.

5. **Peer-reviewed article.** Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), Article 6. The publisher's page blocked me, so I have no link. I confirmed the citation and abstract in Crossref.
   This combines surveys and interviews with 666 participants, not only students. It looks at how AI tool use relates to critical thinking, with "cognitive offloading" (handing mental work to the tool) as the link between them. It measures associations, not cause and effect.

**Preprints, not yet peer reviewed**

6. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047). arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   *Why it's here:* it's the closest match I found to the "how students use it" sub-question. It hasn't been peer reviewed, and it doesn't study high schoolers specifically.
   In two lab experiments, the AI had no overall effect on learning. Students who used it to generate solutions covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used it for explanations understood more. Students tended to prefer the first way.

7. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Huntington-Klein, N. (2026). *Little impact of ChatGPT on high school test scores* (arXiv:2605.08812). arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08812
   *Why it's here:* it looks at U.S. high school test scores at scale. It measures AI use for whole areas, not for individual students, so it can't tell how any one student used the tool.
   It compared areas with heavier and lighter educational ChatGPT use and found an effect on average test scores close to zero.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plentiful:** recent studies, from 2024 on, that link chatbot use to less mental effort or shallower processing. Most of them study college students or adults.
- **Thin:** experiments with high schoolers. Bastani et al. is the only one I found.
- **Missing:** any study I could find that directly compares high schoolers who use chatbots to check their work with those who use them to get answers. Lehmann et al. comes closest, but it's a preprint and it doesn't study high schoolers.
- **Also missing:** long-term studies that follow students for more than a few weeks, and studies outside math and writing.

## Questions these results raise

- Bastani's "tutor" version changed the tool itself. That's different from students choosing to use a plain chatbot carefully, and that difference matters for where your position puts responsibility.
- Several studies found that students *prefer* the answer-getting kind of use. That bears on how often the "helpful" use actually happens.
- The averages-only results (Deng, Huntington-Klein) could hide a split like the one you're predicting, or they could cut against it.
