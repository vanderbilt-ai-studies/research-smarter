## Sources

**Peer-reviewed journal articles** (I confirmed each journal in the Crossref database. The publisher pages blocked me, so I worked from the abstracts. I've left out links I couldn't open; search the title in Google Scholar or your library to get the article and its DOI.)

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. *(Publisher page blocked me; confirmed in Crossref.)*
   A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students compared a standard ChatGPT-style tutor with one built to protect learning. With access, grades on practice problems went up (48% with the standard tutor, 127% with the safeguarded one). When access was taken away, students who had used the standard tutor did 17% worse than students who never had it. The safeguarded tutor mostly avoided that drop.

2. **Peer-reviewed article.** Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. *(Publisher page blocked me; confirmed in Crossref; abstract read via OpenAlex.)*
   This review combines 69 experimental studies from 2022–2024, mostly at the university level. It finds that ChatGPT improves academic performance and reduces mental effort, and it warns that methodological limits call for caution.

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Weidlich, J., Gašević, D., Drachsler, H., & Kirschner, P. (2025). ChatGPT in education: An effect in search of a cause. *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning, 41*(5), e70105. https://research.ou.nl/en/publications/chatgpt-in-education-an-effect-in-search-of-a-cause/
   A critique that uses Deng et al. as its example. The authors audited some of the studies in that review and found that only a small minority clearly described what the ChatGPT group did and what the comparison group did, and used valid learning measures. They argue the gains can't yet be confidently credited to ChatGPT.

4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Wu, X., Zhu, P., Zhang, J., Yin, M., & Wang, Y. (2026). ChatGPT's impact on student learning outcomes: A meta-analysis of 35 experimental studies. *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 13*, 684. *(Publisher page asked for a login; confirmed in Crossref; abstract read via OpenAlex.)*
   This review combines 35 studies with 4,193 participants and finds a moderate positive effect on learning. Subject, how long the experiment ran, and how ChatGPT was used in instruction changed the effect. Educational level did not.

5. **Peer-reviewed article.** Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), 6. *(Publisher page blocked me; confirmed in Crossref.)*
   A survey-and-interview study of 666 people of all ages. Heavier AI use went with lower critical-thinking scores, and the link ran partly through "cognitive offloading," meaning handing thinking off to the tool. Younger participants relied on AI more. The study shows a correlation, not cause. One search result mentioned a correction from September 2025 that I couldn't check.

**Preprints, not yet peer reviewed**

6. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047). arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   Two pre-registered lab experiments found no overall effect on learning. Students who used the chatbot to replace learning steps (for example, having it write solutions) covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used it alongside their own work (for example, asking for explanations) understood more. *Why it's here:* it's the closest match I found to a "how students use it" comparison. *Watch for:* no peer review yet, and the abstract doesn't say whether the participants were high schoolers.

7. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Vanzo, A., Pal Chowdhury, S., & Sachan, M. (2024). *GPT-4 as a homework tutor can improve student engagement and learning outcomes* (arXiv:2409.15981). arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15981
   A randomized trial in four high school classes. GPT-4 tutoring sessions replaced regular homework for the treatment group, and that group gained more in grammar and engagement. *Why it's here:* it's one of the few high school homework experiments I found. *Watch for:* it's small and unreviewed, and the abstract gives no effect sizes.

8. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Kosmyna, N., Hauptmann, E., Yuan, Y. T., Situ, J., Liao, X.-H., Beresnitzky, A. V., Braunstein, I., & Maes, P. (2025). *Your brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of cognitive debt when using an AI assistant for essay writing task* (arXiv:2506.08872). arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872
   An EEG study of essay writing with 54 participants in three groups: chatbot, search engine, and no tools. The chatbot group showed the weakest brain connectivity and performed worse over four months. *Why it's here:* it's widely cited in this debate. *Watch for:* it's unreviewed, only 18 people did the final session, and the abstract I read doesn't say the participants were high schoolers.

**Survey reports**

9. **Research organization report.** Sidoti, O., Park, E., & Gottfried, J. (2025, January 15). *About a quarter of U.S. teens have used ChatGPT for schoolwork – double the share in 2023.* Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork-double-the-share-in-2023/
   In a fall 2024 survey of 1,391 U.S. teens, 26% had used ChatGPT for schoolwork, double the 2023 share. 54% said using it to research topics is acceptable, but only 18% said the same for writing essays. *Why it's here and what to watch for:* it shows how teens use chatbots and how they view that use. It can't show whether the use helps them learn.

10. **Research organization report.** Schwartz, H. L., & Diliberti, M. K. (2026). *More students use AI for homework, and more believe it harms critical thinking: Selected findings from the American Youth Panel* (RR-A4742-1). RAND Corporation. https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4742-1.html
   Among students from middle school through college, AI use for homework rose from 48% to 62% during 2025, driven mostly by middle and high schoolers. 67% said it harms critical thinking. 45% saw getting direct answers as cheating, but nearly 80% said using it to understand an assignment isn't. *Why it's here and what to watch for:* it's a perception survey, not a measure of learning. The numbers I found mix age groups, and I couldn't read the PDF for high school figures.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plentiful:** experiments and meta-analyses on ChatGPT and learning, but mostly with college students.
- **Thin:** studies of high schoolers doing actual homework. Bastani and Vanzo are the main ones.
- **Not found:** a study that sorts high schoolers by whether they check their work or just copy answers and then compares how much they learn. Bastani compares two tool designs, and Lehmann compares ways of using the tool in a lab. Neither matches your split exactly.
- **Long-term evidence is scarce.** Most studies run weeks, not years.
- **Left out on purpose:** a widely cited 2025 meta-analysis by Wang and Fan (*Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*) has been **retracted**, according to Crossref. If you see it cited elsewhere, that's why it's missing here.

## Questions the results raise

- Bastani's "guardrails" were built into the tool, not chosen by the students. That's a different variable from your "kids who check their work."
- Weidlich et al. argue that many ChatGPT studies can't separate the tool from the teaching method. That critique could apply to studies on either side of your position.
- In RAND's survey, students draw a line between getting answers and getting help understanding. That line is a lot like yours, but it's opinion data, not evidence about learning.
