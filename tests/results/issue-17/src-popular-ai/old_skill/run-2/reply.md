## Sources

**Peer-reviewed articles** (I confirmed each journal through Crossref or a database. Five of these count toward your 3 peer-reviewed minimum.)

1. **Peer-reviewed article** · Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. Page I opened: [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html). The PNAS page blocked me, so get the DOI from that record or your library.
   A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students who used either a standard ChatGPT-style tutor or one with learning safeguards. With access, practice grades rose 48% (standard) and 127% (safeguarded). Once access was removed, the standard-tutor group scored 17% lower than students who never had access. The abstract says the safeguards "largely mitigated" that drop.

2. **Peer-reviewed article** · Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. The journal page blocked me. I read the abstract on the [arXiv preprint page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09315).
   A randomized experiment with 117 **university** students on a writing task. Students had help from ChatGPT, a human expert, writing-analytics tools, or nothing extra. The ChatGPT group improved their essay scores more than the others but didn't gain more knowledge or transfer it better.

3. **Peer-reviewed article** · Doo, M. Y., & Park, Y. (2026). A meta-analysis of ChatGPT's influence on learning achievement. *International Review of Research in Open and Distributed Learning, 27*(1), 265–289. [Article page](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/view/8775)
   Combines 22 studies from 2022 to 2024 and finds a moderate positive effect on achievement (g = 0.573). Most participants were undergraduates. The effect for middle and high schoolers was larger (g = 0.928), but the difference from undergraduates wasn't statistically significant.

4. **Peer-reviewed article** · Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, Article 105224. Crossref confirms the details, but the publisher page wouldn't load, so I couldn't read the abstract. Check it before relying on it, and I won't describe its findings since I haven't seen them.

5. **Peer-reviewed article** · Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), 6. Page I opened: [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/gam/jsoctx/v15y2025i1p6-d1559787.html).
   Surveys and interviews with 666 people of "diverse age groups." It found that heavier AI use went with weaker critical thinking, linked through cognitive offloading. Younger participants relied on AI more. Two cautions: it's correlational, and it isn't a study of high schoolers or of homework.

6. **Peer-reviewed article** · Torres-Peralta, R., Cirett-Galán, F., Heras-Sanchez, M. del C., Lerma-Molina, K., & Espinoza-Flores, I. (2026). Perceived learning vs. engagement in AI-assisted homework: A comparative study of ChatGPT use across high school, university, and teachers in Sonora, Mexico (2024–2025). *Future Internet, 18*(3), 122. No link: the publisher blocked me, and I read the abstract through Crossref.
   A survey of 1,477 high school students, university students and teachers. It scored engagement by whether students read, changed and added their own ideas to ChatGPT's output instead of copying it. How much students felt they learned matched those engagement behaviors only weakly. It's self-report, not measured learning.

**Preprint, not yet peer reviewed**

7. Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047v2). [arXiv page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047)
   Two pre-registered lab experiments and a field study found no overall effect. Students who used the AI to *substitute* for their own work (e.g., generating solutions) covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used it to *complement* their work (e.g., asking for explanations) understood more. *Why it's here:* it's the closest match I found to "how students use it." *What to watch for:* it hasn't been peer reviewed, and the abstract doesn't say whether the students were in high school or university.

**Working paper from an international agency (World Bank), not peer reviewed**

8. De Simone, M. E., Tiberti, F. H., Barron Rodriguez, M. R., Manolio, F. A., Mosuro, W., & Dikoru, E. J. (2025). *From chalkboards to chatbots: Evaluating the impact of generative AI on learning outcomes in Nigeria* (Policy Research Working Paper No. 11125). World Bank. [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:11125)
   A randomized trial of six weeks of teacher-supported GPT-4 tutoring for first-year senior secondary students. English scores rose 0.23 standard deviations, which the authors equate to 1.5–2 years of usual schooling. *Why it's here:* it's a rigorous trial with secondary students. *What to watch for:* this was structured after-school tutoring with teachers present, not unsupervised homework.

**Research organization report (survey)**

9. McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. [Report page](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/)
   A fall 2025 survey of 1,458 U.S. teens. 54% have used chatbots for schoolwork help, and 10% do all or most of their schoolwork with them. *Why it's here:* it shows how common the behavior is. *What to watch for:* it measures use and opinions, not whether anyone learned.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plentiful:** short-term studies, meta-analyses, and studies of university students.
- **Thin:** studies of high schoolers doing *homework* specifically. Bastani is the strongest high school study, and it's about practice sessions at one school in Turkey. I found almost nothing long-term.
- **Not found:** any study that separates "checking your own work" from other uses. The closest categories in the research are "substitute vs. complement" (Lehmann) and "reading and adapting vs. copying" (Torres-Peralta), and neither one is quite "checking."
- **Excluded:** a widely cited 2025 meta-analysis by Wang & Fan in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* has been **retracted**. You'll probably see it cited elsewhere, so watch for it.

## Questions these results raise

- The "safeguarded tutor" in Bastani changes the tool, not the student's habits. That bears on whether your position is about student behavior or tool design.
- Several studies find that scores go up *while students have the AI* but don't hold up without it, so which outcome counts as "learning" in your paper matters.
- Lehmann reports that students tend to *prefer* substitution, so the "kids who check their work" group may be small.
