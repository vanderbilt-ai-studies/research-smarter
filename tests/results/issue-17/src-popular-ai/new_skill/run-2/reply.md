## Sources

**Peer-reviewed articles** (all six meet your peer-review requirement)

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), Article e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   - A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students. They used either a standard GPT-4 tutor or a version built with learning safeguards.
   - Access to GPT-4 raised grades on practice work. Once access was taken away, students who'd had the standard version scored 17% lower than students who never had access. The safeguards largely prevented that drop.
   - *Notes:* The publisher's page blocked my tool, so I confirmed this through Crossref and OpenAlex. Crossref also lists a published correction to this paper (https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2518204122), which I didn't open. News coverage I saw only in search results says the study took place in Turkey; the abstract doesn't say, so confirm that before you use it.

2. **Peer-reviewed article.** Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, Article 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   - Pools 69 experimental studies from 2022–2024, mostly with university students.
   - Reports improvements in academic performance, motivation and higher-order thinking, along with less mental effort. It found no effect on self-efficacy.
   - The authors flag weaknesses in the studies they reviewed, such as missing power analyses.

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Doo, M. Y., & Park, Y. (2026). A meta-analysis of ChatGPT's influence on learning achievement. *The International Review of Research in Open and Distributed Learning, 27*(1), 265–289. https://doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v27i1.8775
   - Pools 22 publications and finds a moderate positive effect on achievement (g = 0.573).
   - Middle and high school studies showed a larger effect than undergraduate studies (0.928 vs. 0.538), but the difference wasn't statistically significant. About 71% of participants were undergraduates.
   - Studies built around self-regulated learning had the largest effects (g = 1.115).

4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   - A randomized lab experiment with 117 university students doing a writing task. Each student got one of four kinds of support: ChatGPT, a human expert, writing analytics tools, or nothing extra.
   - The ChatGPT group improved their essays the most, but their knowledge gain and transfer didn't differ significantly from the other groups.
   - The authors warn about dependence and "metacognitive laziness."

5. **Peer-reviewed article.** Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), Article 6. https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006
   - Surveys and interviews with 666 participants across age groups. Frequent AI use correlated with lower critical thinking scores, and cognitive offloading explained part of that link.
   - This is a correlation study of adults and young adults, not an experiment and not about homework.

6. **Peer-reviewed article.** Klar, M. (2025). Using ChatGPT is easy, using it effectively is tough? A mixed methods study on K-12 students' perceptions, interaction patterns, and support for learning with generative AI chatbots. *Smart Learning Environments, 12*(1), Article 32. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40561-025-00385-2
   - An experiment with 106 secondary students, apparently in Germany based on the author's affiliation.
   - Without guidance, students underused the chatbot's adaptation features (such as adjusting response length or difficulty). A guided group used about twice as many adaptation prompts.
   - Knowledge gains were similar in both groups.

**Preprints, not yet peer reviewed**

7. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2024). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.09047
   - Combines a field study with two pre-registered lab experiments. It found no average effect on learning.
   - Students who used the chatbot *instead of* doing the work (generating solutions) covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used it *alongside* the work (asking for explanations) understood more.
   - The abstract I read doesn't give participants' ages. Search summaries describe university programming students.
   - *Why it's here:* it speaks directly to how students use chatbots. *What to watch for:* no journal has reviewed it yet.

8. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Dai, X., Wen, Z., Jiang, J., Liu, H., & Zhang, Y. (2025). *How students use AI feedback matters: Experimental evidence on physics achievement and autonomy.* arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2505.08672
   - Two randomized trials with 387 high school physics students over five weeks.
   - Effects varied by how the AI was used and by the student's achievement level. Low achievers improved with hint-style help. Medium achievers declined when given conventional answers.
   - *What to watch for:* not peer reviewed, and the abstract doesn't name the country.

**Research organization reports**

9. **Research organization report.** Sidoti, O., Park, E., & Gottfried, J. (2025, January 15). *About a quarter of U.S. teens have used ChatGPT for schoolwork – double the share in 2023.* Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork/

10. **Research organization report.** McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI.* Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
    - A fall 2025 survey of 1,458 U.S. teens. 54% use chatbots for schoolwork help, and 10% say they do all or most of their schoolwork with them.
    - 59% say AI cheating happens at their school at least somewhat often.
    - *Same caveat:* it tells you about use and perceptions, not learning.

## Questions these results raise

- Several studies find that effects differ by prior achievement, which could be a third variable alongside the way students use the tool.
- Students may not actually choose how they use a chatbot. Bastani and Klar both suggest the tool's design or the guidance students get shapes their use.
- Better grades on practice work don't always mean more learning (Bastani, Fan). That bears on how you measure "help."
