## Sources

**Peer-reviewed articles.** I confirmed each journal through Crossref this session. All six meet your requirement of at least 3 peer-reviewed sources.

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*). Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122 · [free full text on PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/)
   This was a randomized experiment with nearly 1,000 students in grades 9–11 at one high school in Turkey. It compared a plain ChatGPT-style tool ("GPT Base"), a version that gives hints instead of answers ("GPT Tutor"), and no AI. GPT Base raised practice scores by 48% but lowered later no-AI exam scores by 17%. GPT Tutor's exam scores were statistically indistinguishable from the no-AI group. The authors report that the most common message to GPT Base was "What is the answer?" A later correction changed only one author's affiliation, not the results.

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers & Education*). Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   This review combines 69 experiments (2022–2024), mostly at the university level. Overall, ChatGPT improved academic performance and reduced mental effort. The authors warn that many studies may have measured the quality of ChatGPT's output rather than what students learned. *(I read the abstract only; the publisher page blocked me.)*

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*). Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   In this randomized experiment, 117 university students did a writing task with help from ChatGPT, a human expert, writing tools, or nothing. The ChatGPT group improved their essay scores the most, but their knowledge gain and transfer were no different from the other groups. *(I read the abstract and the arXiv preprint; the journal page blocked me.)*

4. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers in Human Behavior*). Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, 108386. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386
   This study randomly assigned 91 university students to research a science question with ChatGPT or with Google. The ChatGPT group reported lower mental effort but wrote lower-quality reasoning. *(Abstract only.)*

5. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Scientific Reports*). Kestin, G., Miller, K., Klales, A., Milbourne, T., & Ponti, G. (2025). AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: An RCT introducing a novel research-based design in an authentic educational setting. *Scientific Reports, 15*, 17458. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6 · [full text on PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)
   This was a randomized trial with 194 Harvard undergraduates in introductory physics. Students learned more in less time from an AI tutor built on teaching best practices than from an in-class active-learning lesson.

6. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Societies*, MDPI). Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), 6. https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006
   This was a survey and interview study of 666 people of mixed ages, not specifically students. Heavier AI use was linked to lower critical-thinking scores, partly through "cognitive offloading" (handing thinking over to the tool), and younger participants relied on AI most. *(Abstract only.)*

**Preprint, not yet peer reviewed**

7. Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.09047 · [arXiv page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047)
   *Why it's here:* it is one of the few studies that compares different ways of using a chatbot. *What to watch for:* it hasn't been peer reviewed, and it studied university programming students. In two lab experiments the authors found no overall effect on learning. In a field study, students who used the LLM to get solutions covered more topics but understood each less, while students who asked for explanations understood more.

8. Sidoti, O., Park, E., & Gottfried, J. (2025, January 15). *About a quarter of U.S. teens have used ChatGPT for schoolwork – double the share in 2023*. Pew Research Center. [Link](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork/)

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plentiful:** experiments with university students, and surveys of how many teens use chatbots.
- **Thin:** experiments with high schoolers. Bastani is the only one I found, and it's one school in one country, in one subject.
- **Almost missing:** studies where students *chose* to check their work versus get answers, with learning measured afterward. Bastani and Lehmann come closest, but in Bastani the difference came from how the *tool* was designed, not from a choice students made. I found no long-term studies.
- **Dropped:** a widely cited meta-analysis (Wang & Fan, 2025, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*) kept showing up in searches, but it has been **retracted**. Worth knowing in case you see it quoted elsewhere.

## Questions the results raise

- Whether "checking your work" and "getting answers" are a student's choice or something a tool's design pushes them toward.
- Whether findings from college students transfer to high schoolers.
- Whether "homework scores went up" and "learning went up" are the same thing in these studies. Deng and Fan both suggest they aren't.
