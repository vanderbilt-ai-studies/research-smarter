## Sources

**Peer-reviewed articles** (these five meet your 3-peer-reviewed requirement)

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*). Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   This field experiment involved nearly 1,000 high school math students. They used either a standard ChatGPT-style tutor or one built with safeguards for learning. Both raised scores during practice, but once access was removed, students who'd had the standard version scored about 17% lower than students who never had access. The safeguarded version largely avoided that drop. The journal record lists a published correction; I didn't see what it changes.

2. **Peer-reviewed article, meta-analysis** (*Computers & Education*). Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   It pools 69 experimental studies from 2022–2024 and reports that ChatGPT improved academic performance and higher-order thinking and reduced mental effort. It found no effect on self-efficacy. The authors flag weaknesses in the studies they pooled, including how learning was measured after the intervention. I read only the abstract.

3. **Peer-reviewed article, meta-analysis** (*International Review of Research in Open and Distributed Learning*). Doo, M. Y., & Park, Y. (2026). A meta-analysis of ChatGPT's influence on learning achievement. *The International Review of Research in Open and Distributed Learning, 27*(1), 265–289. https://doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v27i1.8775
   It pools 22 studies and finds a moderate positive effect on achievement. The effect looked larger for middle and high school students than for undergraduates, but the difference wasn't statistically significant. Most participants were undergraduates.

4. **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*). Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   This lab study randomly assigned 117 university students to write with ChatGPT, a human expert, analytics tools, or no help. The ChatGPT group improved their essays the most but didn't gain more knowledge or transfer it better. The authors raise the idea of "metacognitive laziness."

5. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Social Sciences & Humanities Open*). Barcaui, A. (2025). ChatGPT as a cognitive crutch: Evidence from a randomized controlled trial on knowledge retention. *Social Sciences & Humanities Open, 12*, 102287. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssaho.2025.102287
   120 business undergraduates in Brazil were randomly assigned to study with ChatGPT or without it. On a surprise test 45 days later, the ChatGPT group scored 57.5% against 68.5% for the others. I confirmed the journal record, but the numbers come from a university research repository's summary because the full text was blocked.

**Preprints, not yet peer reviewed**

6. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   In two lab experiments, chatbot access had no overall effect on learning. Students who used it to generate solutions covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used it for explanations understood more. *Why it's here:* it's the closest match I found to a "how students use it" comparison. *Watch for:* it hasn't been through peer review, and the abstract doesn't say who the participants were.

7. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed** (accepted to an ACM computing conference). Valle Torre, M., Specht, M., & Oertel, C. (2025). *LLM chatbots in high school programming: Exploring behaviors and interventions* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18985
   This study logged the chatbot questions of Dutch high school programming students. Students who asked a larger share of "just give me the solution" questions had much lower midterm grades. After a lesson on help-seeking, that share dropped. *Watch for:* there were only about 37 students, the groups weren't randomly assigned, and it shows a correlation, not a cause.

**International agency working paper**

8. **World Bank working paper, not peer reviewed.** De Simone, M. E., Tiberti, F. H., Barron Rodriguez, M. R., Manolio, F. A., Mosuro, W., & Dikoru, E. J. (2025). *From chalkboards to chatbots: Evaluating the impact of generative AI on learning outcomes in Nigeria* (Policy Research Working Paper No. 11125). World Bank. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:11125
   This randomized trial gave Nigerian secondary students six weeks of after-school English tutoring with Copilot (GPT-4). English scores rose by 0.23 standard deviations. *Why it's here:* it's a rare randomized study of high schoolers. *Watch for:* it's a supervised school program, not homework at home. I only saw the abstract.

**Survey report**

9. **Research organization report** (Pew Research Center). McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
   This fall 2025 survey of 1,458 U.S. teens found that 54% have used chatbots for schoolwork, and 1 in 10 do all or most of their schoolwork with them. 59% say students at their school use AI to cheat at least somewhat often. *Why it's here:* it's a survey, not a study of learning. It's useful for how teens use chatbots, not for whether that helps them.

## What I found and didn't find

- **No study directly compares "checking my work" with "getting answers."** The closest are the solution-vs-explanation split (Lehmann) and the "executive" vs. "instrumental" help-seeking split (Valle Torre). Neither one measures checking finished homework.
- **High school evidence is thin.** Only Bastani, Valle Torre, and the World Bank paper study high schoolers. Most of the rest study undergraduates.
- **Real homework at home is almost unstudied.** Nearly everything here took place in a lab, a classroom, or a supervised program.
- **Long-term evidence is scarce.** Barcaui's 45-day test is the longest follow-up here.
- **I dropped one source.** A widely cited 2025 meta-analysis by Wang & Fan in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* shows up a lot in search results, but the journal record marks it **retracted**.

## Questions the results raise

- In Bastani's study, the tool's design (guardrails or none) made a big difference, not only students' choices. That bears on whether your position is about student behavior, tool design, or both.
- The meta-analyses mostly find average gains, while several single studies find harm on later tests. The gap may come from *when* learning is measured: during practice or after the tool is taken away.
