**Peer-reviewed research**

- **Peer-reviewed article (*PNAS*).** Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), Article e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
  A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students. They used either a standard ChatGPT-style tool or a version with built-in prompts meant to protect learning. Both raised scores during practice. Once the tool was taken away, the standard-version group did worse than students who never had AI, and the safeguarded version largely prevented that drop. *(PNAS's site blocked me. I confirmed the details and read the abstract through Crossref and OpenAlex.)*

- **Peer-reviewed article (*British Journal of Educational Technology*).** Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
  A randomized lab study of 117 university students doing a writing task with ChatGPT, a human expert, writing-analytics tools, or no extra support. The ChatGPT group's essay scores improved more, but knowledge gain and transfer didn't differ significantly between groups. The authors suggest the tool may encourage dependence. A free preprint version is at https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09315.

- **Peer-reviewed systematic review and meta-analysis (*Computers & Education*).** Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, Article 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
  Pools 69 experimental studies from 2022–2024, mostly at university level. It reports gains in academic performance and higher-order thinking and lower mental effort, with no effect on self-efficacy. It also flags weak methods in many of the studies. *(I read only the abstract.)*

- **Peer-reviewed article (*Smart Learning Environments*).** Klar, M. (2025). Using ChatGPT is easy, using it effectively is tough? A mixed methods study on K-12 students' perceptions, interaction patterns, and support for learning with generative AI chatbots. *Smart Learning Environments, 12*(1), Article 32. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40561-025-00385-2
  An experiment with 106 secondary school students. Those given short guidance on using a chatbot used its features about twice as often as the control group. Both groups had similar knowledge gains. *(I read only the abstract.)*

- **Peer-reviewed article (*Computers in Human Behavior*).** Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, Article 108386. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386
  91 university students researched a science question using either ChatGPT or Google. The ChatGPT group reported less mental effort but produced weaker reasoning and justifications. A free copy is listed at https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/125262/.

**Preprint and working paper**

- **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed (arXiv).** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047; Version 2). arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.09047
  Two pre-registered lab experiments found no overall effect on learning. In exploratory analyses, students who used the AI to *replace* work (such as generating solutions) covered more topics but understood each less. Students who used it to *complement* their work (such as asking for explanations) understood more. *Why it's here and what to watch:* it's the closest match to your sub-question 2, but it hasn't been peer reviewed, the "how students used it" results are exploratory, and the abstract doesn't say how old the students were.

- **International agency working paper (World Bank).** De Simone, M. E., Tiberti, F. H., Barron Rodriguez, M. R., Manolio, F. A., Mosuro, W., & Dikoru, E. J. (2025). *From chalkboards to chatbots: Evaluating the impact of generative AI on learning outcomes in Nigeria* (Policy Research Working Paper No. 11125). World Bank. Listing: https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:11125
  A randomized trial of six weeks of after-school English tutoring with Microsoft Copilot (GPT-4) for first-year senior secondary students. It reports a 0.23 standard deviation gain in English. *Why it's here and what to watch:* it's a rigorous secondary-school study, but working papers aren't peer reviewed, and the tutoring was structured and supervised, which is different from homework at home. *(The World Bank's own page and the PDF blocked me, so I read only the abstract on the listing above.)*

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plentiful:** university-level experiments and reviews.
- **Thin:** studies of high schoolers specifically. I found three: Bastani, Klar, and the World Bank paper.
- **Not found:** any study of high schoolers doing *homework at home* that directly compares "checking work" with "getting answers." Bastani's practice sessions took place at school, and Lehmann's comparison of replacing versus complementing work is the closest I found.
- **Not found:** long-term studies. Most interventions lasted days to weeks.

## Questions the results raise

- Researchers split student use in different ways: replacing vs. complementing, a guardrailed vs. a standard tool, guided vs. unguided. "Checking work" may or may not fit cleanly into any of those.
- Some of these studies change the *tool* (Bastani's guardrails) and others look at the *student's behavior* (Lehmann). Your position is about student behavior.
- Results from university students and from in-class sessions may or may not carry over to teenagers doing homework alone.
