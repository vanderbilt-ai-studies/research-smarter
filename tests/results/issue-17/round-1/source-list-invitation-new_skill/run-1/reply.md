**Peer-reviewed studies that measure outcomes**

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*The Lancet Regional Health – Europe*; abstract only)
   Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S. A. M., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P. H., Quinlan, J. I., Sitch, A. J., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. J. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, Article 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211
   This study compared 1,227 students aged 12–15 in 30 English schools with restrictive or permissive phone policies. Students at restrictive schools used their phones less during school time. Their total phone use across weekdays and weekends was no different, and neither was their wellbeing. It didn't measure interaction or bullying.

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Journal of Behavioral Addictions*; abstract only)
   King, D. L., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B. A., & Wade, T. D. (2024). "Phones off while school's on": Evaluating problematic phone use and the social, wellbeing, and academic effects of banning phones in schools. *Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 13*(4), 913–922. https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.2024.00058
   This study surveyed about 1,280 students in Grades 7–12 at five South Australian schools before a ban and one month after. Ban and no-ban schools didn't differ in school belonging. Bullying was slightly higher in the ban group, and it fell significantly in both groups.

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Journal of Youth and Adolescence*; abstract only)
   Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R. J. J. M., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
   This study compared 1,398 Dutch students (average age 16) at partial-ban and full-ban schools. Bullying and wellbeing didn't differ. Full bans were linked to weaker student–teacher connectedness and, for girls, a lower sense of school belonging.

4. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Frontiers in Developmental Psychology*; I read about the first quarter in full and the rest in summary)
   Vereecke, J., Vanherle, R., Nassen, L.-M., Boen, H., Eggermont, S., Beullens, K., Vandenbosch, L., & Schreurs, L. (2026). More than disconnecting? School smartphone bans and adolescents' social wellbeing. *Frontiers in Developmental Psychology, 4*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fdpys.2026.1796471
   This study followed 960 students at one Belgian school for seven months after it moved from a partial to a total ban. It had no comparison school. Support from friends stayed stable, social loneliness rose slightly, and cyberbullying stayed low with a small dip. The authors note that cyberbullying "often occurs outside school hours," but the study doesn't measure where it happens.

5. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers in Human Behavior*; abstract only)
   Chen, S.-C., Huang, T.-F., Chang, K., Chang, F.-C., Chiang, S. C., Chiu, C.-H., Chen, P.-H., Miao, N.-F., & Chuang, H.-Y. (2025). Association between school phone restriction policies and adolescents' cyberbullying, gambling, and substance use behaviors. *Computers in Human Behavior, 177*, Article 108898. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108898
   This was a one-time survey of 3,158 high school students in 35 Taiwanese schools. Students who believed their school had no phone restrictions reported more cyberbullying and other online risk behaviors. Because it measured everything at one point in time, it shows a link, not a cause.

6. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Journal of Human Resources*; abstract only, full text is paywalled)
   Abrahamsson, S. (2026). Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health. *Journal of Human Resources*. Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.0224-13403R2
   This study used administrative and survey data on middle schools as bans rolled out over time. It reports lower bullying for both boys and girls after bans. The abstract doesn't name the country. Search results describe it as Norway, so confirm that in the paper.

**Peer-reviewed qualitative studies (students' own accounts)**

7. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers in Human Behavior*; abstract only)
   Bar, E., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B. A., Wade, T. D., & King, D. L. (2025). Student perspectives on banning mobile phones in South Australian secondary schools: A large-scale qualitative analysis. *Computers in Human Behavior, 167*, Article 108603. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108603
   This study analyzed open-ended answers from 1,549 students at five schools. Students named more face-to-face social interaction as one benefit of the ban. They also reported feeling less independent and having trouble managing distress without their phones.

8. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Social Science & Medicine*; abstract only)
   Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S. A. M., Michail, M., Patterson, P. H., Sitch, A. J., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. J. (2026). How school phone policies influence adolescent phone use and wellbeing (SMART Schools): A qualitative comparative case study. *Social Science & Medicine, 398*, Article 119094. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2026.119094
   This study ran 40 focus groups with 177 students, parents, and staff at seven English schools. It found positive and negative effects of both restrictive and permissive policies, including effects on "connectedness." The authors stress looking at school and out-of-school settings together.

9. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Academic Pediatrics*; read in part on PubMed Central)
   Bisom-Rapp, E., Otmar, C. D., Potes, C., Nayak, S., Engle, K., Ganson, K. T., & Nagata, J. M. (2026). The need for autonomy, safety, and structure: A qualitative study of adolescent perspectives on school phone restrictions. *Academic Pediatrics, 26*(5), Article 103333. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.acap.2026.103333
   The authors interviewed 30 U.S. 16- to 19-year-olds. Some said phones get in the way of connecting, some found socializing harder without them, and others said phones help social interaction. It's a small sample recruited through social media ads.

**Peer-reviewed review**

10. **Peer-reviewed scoping review** (*Journal of Psychologists and Counsellors in Schools*; abstract only)
    Campbell, M., Edwards, E. J., Pennell, D., Poed, S., Lister, V., Gillett-Swan, J., Kelly, A., Zec, D., & Nguyen, T.-A. (2024). Evidence for and against banning mobile phones in schools: A scoping review. *Journal of Psychologists and Counsellors in Schools, 34*(3), 242–265. https://doi.org/10.1177/20556365241270394
    This review covers 22 studies on academics, wellbeing, and cyberbullying. It found no randomized trials, and it says the studies define bans and measure outcomes so differently that their results are hard to reconcile. Its reference list is a good place to look for more sources.

**Working paper (not yet peer reviewed)**

11. **Working paper, not yet peer reviewed** (abstract only)
    Allcott, H., Baron, E. J., Dee, T., Duckworth, A. L., Gentzkow, M., & Jacob, B. (2026). *The effects of school phone bans: National evidence from lockable pouches* (NBER Working Paper No. 35132). National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w35132
    I included it because it's a large U.S. study and recent. It doesn't count toward your peer-reviewed five, and its numbers may change before publication. Phone pouches cut phone use. Discipline incidents rose in the first year and then faded, and the authors found no clear change in perceived online bullying.

## What I found and didn't find

- **In-person interaction is rarely measured directly.** Most studies use stand-ins like belonging, connectedness, or friend support. The direct mentions of more face-to-face talk come from what students say in qualitative studies (Bar et al.), not from observing them.
- **I found no study that tracks whether conflict moves to after school.** None of the bullying studies separates school-day incidents from after-school ones. Goodyear et al. (2025) show that *phone use* shifts out of school hours. That's about phone use, not conflict.
- **Few studies focus on U.S. high schools.** Several samples are younger: Abrahamsson studied middle schools, and Goodyear et al. studied 12- to 15-year-olds.
- **Most studies only cover a short time.** King et al. waited one month, and the NBER paper suggests effects change over the first few years.

## What these results raise

- In these studies, "interaction" sometimes means talking, sometimes belonging, and sometimes feeling connected to teachers. You'll need to decide which one your position is about.
- Your second sub-question depends on *where* conflict happens, and that's exactly what the bullying measures leave out.
- Results differ between middle and high school, and between partial and full bans. That bears on how widely your claim can apply.
