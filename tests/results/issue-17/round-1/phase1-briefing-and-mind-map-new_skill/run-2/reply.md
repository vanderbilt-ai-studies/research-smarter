## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Do students learn less?** In Oregon, [Thompson (*Journal of Public Economics*, 2021)](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/12204) found math scores fell 0.044–0.053 standard deviations and reading scores fell 0.033–0.038. He mainly blames earlier start times and losing about 3.5 hours of class a week. An earlier Colorado study by Anderson & Walker (2015) is widely reported as finding math *gains*. I couldn't open it, so check it yourself.
- **Does it help hire and keep teachers?** In one Iowa district, [Hollingworth & An (*The Rural Educator*, 2025)](https://scholarsjunction.msstate.edu/ruraleducator/vol46/iss2/1) report that every open teaching job was filled after the switch. A large Texas study, still a working paper, found teacher turnover fell about 2.7 percentage points, but more than 1 in 5 teachers still left each year ([Education Week, Jan. 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/do-4-day-school-weeks-attract-and-retain-better-teachers-what-the-largest-study-yet-says/2026/01)).
- **Does it save money?** Not much. Districts generally save about 0.4–2.5% of their budget, according to an Education Commission of the States estimate reported by [Education Week (April 2026)](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04).

**Major voices**
- **Economists** do most of the work, especially Paul Thompson, whose name is on several Oregon studies.
- **Research organizations:** RAND published a [research brief](https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/research_briefs/RBA300/RBA373-1/RAND_RBA373-1.pdf) on multiple states. I opened it but couldn't read the text, so I'm not reporting its findings.
- **NWEA and the Education Commission of the States** supply the adoption counts and the savings estimate.
- **District leaders and rural communities** are the ones actually adopting the schedule.

**Patterns**
- **Mostly test-score studies** that compare scores before and after a district switches, one state at a time.
- **Mostly rural.** Four-day weeks are now in more than 2,100 schools in 26 states, up from about 1,600 in 24 states six years earlier (Education Week explainer). Rural districts dominate.
- **Hours may matter more than days.** Several summaries say losses appear mainly where districts cut total class time.

**Open questions**
- **The fifth day.** In Colorado, [Fischer & Argyle (*Economics of Education Review*, 2018)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecoedu/v64y2018icp31-39.html) found about a 20% rise in juvenile offenses, mostly property crime. Meals, sleep and supervision have much less research.
- **Families.** My searches found nothing on parents' jobs or child care.
- **Who's affected.** In Oregon's K–3 grades, [Thompson et al. (*Early Childhood Research Quarterly*, 2023)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9979633) found "minimal differences" on average. The biggest drops were among students who started kindergarten above the median.
- **Does the teacher perk fade** once neighboring districts adopt the schedule too?

The journal articles above are confirmed in Crossref. The DOI for Thompson's 2021 article is 10.1016/j.jpubeco.2020.104308.
