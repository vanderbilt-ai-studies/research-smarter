## First briefing: four-day school weeks

**How widespread it is.** The National Conference of State Legislatures puts the count at about 850 districts in 24 states, up from about 650 in 2020 ([NCSL brief, updated 2023](https://www.ncsl.org/education/four-day-school-week-overview)). Most are small and rural. Texas went from 8 four-day districts in 2019–20 to at least 195 by 2025–26 ([Education Week, Jan. 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/do-4-day-school-weeks-attract-and-retain-better-teachers-what-the-largest-study-yet-says/2026/01)).

**Key debates**
- **Does learning suffer?** In Oregon, Paul Thompson found math scores fell about 5.9% of a standard deviation and reading about 4.2% after districts switched, with bigger drops by year four ([Education Next, 2021](https://www.educationnext.org/shrinking-school-week-effects-four-day-schedule-student-achievement/); a magazine article that summarizes his research, not peer reviewed). A Colorado study found no harm and possibly gains. I only saw that one described in news coverage and couldn't open it.
- **Is it worth the money?** Average savings run about 0.4–2.5% of a budget (NCSL).
- **Does it help keep teachers?** Teachers say they like it. But in Texas, turnover fell only about 2.7 percentage points after districts switched, and more than 1 in 5 teachers still left each year (Education Week).
- **What happens on the day off?** One Colorado study linked the schedule to about a 20% rise in youth crime, mostly property crime ([Chalkbeat, 2018](https://chalkbeat.org/2018/4/2/21104689/four-day-school-weeks-a-nationwide-symptom-of-tight-budgets-lead-to-more-youth-crime-study-finds); a news report on a study in *Economics of Education Review*). An Oregon survey study found teens in four-day schools were more likely to be food insecure ([Tomayko et al., *Journal of School Health*, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8595551)).

**Major voices**
- Economists Paul Thompson (Oregon State) and Emily Morton (NWEA).
- RAND Corporation researchers, whose multi-state report found high satisfaction but slower test-score growth ([Kilburn et al., 2021](https://www.nwea.org/research/publication/does-four-equal-five-implementation-and-outcomes-of-the-four-day-school-week/)).
- Crime researchers Stefanie Fischer and Daniel Argyle.
- NCSL, which tracks state policy.

**Patterns**
- Most studies use state test-score or employment data to compare districts before and after they switch.
- The evidence comes from only a few states (Oregon, Colorado, Texas, Idaho, New Mexico, Oklahoma).
- Results seem to depend on how much total instructional time a district keeps.
- Families report high satisfaction even where test scores dip.
- Effects differ across student groups. In Oregon, early-elementary results were near zero on average, but higher-achieving students were hurt most ([Thompson et al., *Early Childhood Research Quarterly*, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9979633)).

**Open questions**
- How do students actually spend the fifth day?
- Do the results apply to larger, non-rural districts?
- What are the long-term effects?
- Why does the schedule keep spreading while the evidence stays mixed?
