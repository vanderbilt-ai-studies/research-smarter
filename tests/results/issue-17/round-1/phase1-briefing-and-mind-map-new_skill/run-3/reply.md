## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Test scores.** The results disagree with each other:
  - In Oregon, Paul Thompson found small drops in math and reading. He linked them to earlier start times and about 3.5 fewer hours of class a week ([IZA working paper, 2019](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/12204); published in the *Journal of Public Economics*, 2021).
  - A follow-up on Oregon's early grades found almost no difference on average. The effects did differ by student group, though, and not in the way you might expect ([*Early Childhood Research Quarterly*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9979633)).
  - An earlier Colorado study (Anderson and Walker, 2015) reportedly found *gains* in math. I only saw that one in search summaries and couldn't open it.
- **Teachers.** Districts often say the schedule helps them hire and keep teachers. A large Texas study found turnover fell about 2.7 percentage points, but teachers' experience and education levels didn't improve ([*Education Week*, Jan. 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/do-4-day-school-weeks-attract-and-retain-better-teachers-what-the-largest-study-yet-says/2026/01)).
- **Savings.** One estimate puts district savings at about 0.4% to 2.5% of the budget, and researchers disagree about whether that's worth it ([*Education Week* explainer, Apr. 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04)).

**Major voices**
- **Economists of education.** Thompson's Oregon group has published several of the studies above.
- **RAND.** It ran a multi-state study in 2021. I found its research brief but couldn't read the text, so I'm only reporting it secondhand.
- **NWEA.** It tracks how many schools use the schedule: more than 2,100 schools in 26 states in 2024–25, according to *Education Week*.

**Patterns**
- **The same method keeps coming up.** Most studies compare districts before and after they switch, against districts that didn't switch.
- **Most of the evidence comes from a few states,** mainly Oregon, Colorado, Oklahoma, Missouri and Texas.
- **Instructional time keeps showing up as a key factor.** *Education Week* reports that achievement losses appear mainly where total class hours fell.

**Open questions**
- **What happens on the fifth day.** A Colorado study linked four-day weeks to about a 20% rise in juvenile offenses, mostly property crime ([Fischer & Argyle, *Economics of Education Review*, 2018](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:ecoedu:v:64:y:2018:i:c:p:31-39)).
- **Student health.** Oregon students on four-day weeks reported more food insecurity and drug use ([*Journal of School Health*, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8595551)).
- **Behavior at school.** A 2022 Oklahoma study reportedly found less bullying and fighting at school after a switch. I know it only from *Education Week*'s description.
- **Families.** Childcare and the effects on parents are discussed a lot but measured very little.

Before you rely on any of this, open the studies and find the figures yourself, especially the Oregon effect sizes. Two of them (Anderson and Walker, and the Oklahoma bullying study) I've only seen secondhand. You can also pick any claim here, like the Fischer and Argyle 20% crime figure, and ask me how I know it.
