## Sources

**Peer-reviewed articles.** These three meet your "at least 3 peer-reviewed" requirement. Only the first one studied high schoolers.

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*)
   Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), Article e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   This was a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students. Some got a plain ChatGPT-style tutor, some got a tutor designed to protect learning, and some got no AI. According to the abstract, both AI groups did better on practice problems. Once access was removed, though, the plain-ChatGPT group scored 17% lower than students who never had AI, and the "guardrailed" tutor largely prevented that drop. *The publisher's site blocked me, so I worked from the official abstract (via Europe PMC) and confirmed the details through Crossref, which also shows a published correction. Check the correction when you read the full text.*

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*)
   Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   This was a randomized experiment with 117 university students working on a writing task, supported by ChatGPT, a human expert, writing tools, or nothing. The ChatGPT group improved their essay scores the most, but they didn't gain more knowledge or transfer it better. They also did less self-monitoring, such as evaluating their own work. *I read the abstract on arXiv. The journal page was blocked; Crossref confirmed the venue.*

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Societies*)
   Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), Article 6. https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006
   This study used surveys and interviews with 666 people of different ages. People who used AI more often scored lower on critical thinking, and the link ran through "cognitive offloading," meaning handing mental work to the tool. Younger participants relied on AI more. The study shows a correlation, not cause and effect, and the participants weren't high schoolers. *I worked from the official abstract (via OpenAlex). Crossref also lists a correction.*

**Working papers and preprints (not yet peer reviewed).**

4. **Working paper, not yet peer reviewed**
   Strömberg, D., Lei, V., & Wu, Y. (2026). *The generative AI learning penalty: Evidence from Chinese secondary education* [Unpublished manuscript]. https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f240545.pdf
   This paper followed 26,811 students in grades 7–12 in China for 30 months. After students started using AI, their homework scores went up 18% and their monthly exam scores fell about 20%. The authors say the losses were concentrated among the roughly 80% of users whose homework times suggest they were "outsourcing" it. Users who kept spending normal time on homework had small losses.
   *Why it's here:* it's one of the very few large, long-term studies of secondary students. *What to watch:* it hasn't been peer reviewed, and "outsourcing" is inferred from homework time, not observed directly. I read its first pages.

5. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed** (arXiv)
   Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.09047
   This paper ran lab experiments plus a field study. It found no overall effect on learning. Students who used AI *in place of* learning steps covered more topics but understood less, while students who used it *alongside* learning (for example, asking for explanations) understood more.
   *Why it's here:* it directly compares different ways of using AI. *What to watch:* it isn't peer reviewed, and the current abstract doesn't say who the participants were. An earlier version describes university coding classes.

**Survey reports.** These tell you how teens use chatbots, not whether chatbots help them learn.

6. **Research organization report**
   Schwartz, H. L., & Diliberti, M. K. (2026). *More students use AI for homework, and more believe it harms critical thinking: Selected findings from the American Youth Panel* (Report No. RR-A4742-1). RAND Corporation. https://doi.org/10.7249/RRA4742-1
   This survey of 1,214 students ages 12–29 (December 2025) found that AI use for homework rose from 48% to 62% between May and December 2025. 67% said AI harms critical thinking, and 45% said getting direct answers counts as cheating.
   *What to watch:* the figures combine middle school, high school, and college students.

7. **Research organization report**
   McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
   This is a survey of 1,458 U.S. teens ages 13–17 (fall 2025). 54% have used chatbots for schoolwork, and 10% say they do all or most of their schoolwork with chatbot help. These are self-reports of use, not learning outcomes.

8. **Research organization report**
   Sidoti, O., Park, E., & Gottfried, J. (2025, January 15). *About a quarter of U.S. teens have used ChatGPT for schoolwork – double the share in 2023*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork-double-the-share-in-2023/
   This survey from fall 2024 found that 26% of teens had used ChatGPT for schoolwork, up from 13% in 2023. It's useful as an earlier comparison point for source 7.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Thin on high schoolers specifically.** Only source 1 is a peer-reviewed study of high school students. Most experiments are on college students.
- **Almost nothing long-term.** Source 4 is the main exception.
- **No study directly measures "checking work."** The closest are source 4 (homework time as a sign of outsourcing), source 5 (substituting vs. complementing), and source 1 (open tool vs. guarded tool).
- **One source I dropped.** A widely cited 2025 meta-analysis by Wang & Fan in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* reported large learning gains from ChatGPT. Crossref shows it has been **retracted**, so I left it out. Watch for it, because other papers still cite it.

## Questions the results raise

- Several studies found that grades go up *while* students are using AI and then drop afterward. That makes it matter which measure "learning" means in your question.
- In sources 1 and 5, the difference comes from how the tool was designed or used, not from what the student intended. That may bear on your "checking vs. getting answers" split.
