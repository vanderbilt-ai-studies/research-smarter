### Peer-reviewed research

1. ✅ **Peer-reviewed article (field experiment)**
   Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   - Nearly 1,000 high school math students practiced with either a ChatGPT-like tutor or a tutor built with learning safeguards. Both groups did better during practice. Once the AI was taken away, the ChatGPT-like group scored 17% lower than students who never had it, while the safeguarded group didn't show that drop.
   - I read the abstract through Crossref. The journal page blocked me. Crossref also shows a published correction (2025), so check what it changed.

2. ✅ **Peer-reviewed article (randomized controlled trial)**
   Fütterer, T., Bardach, L., Kuhn, J., Keller, S. D., & Gerjets, P. (2026). Enhancing school students' self-regulated learning through generative AI support: A randomized controlled trial. *Educational Psychology Review, 38*(1). https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-026-10133-8
   - 371 students in grades 7–9 used one of two specially designed AI supports or standard ChatGPT across six physics or English lessons. Neither special version beat standard ChatGPT on effort, knowledge, or study strategies. Exploratory analyses suggested that students who engaged more meaningfully with the AI kept their interest longer.
   - I only saw the abstract, and the article page needed a login. Check the article number on the full article; I saw "42" only secondhand.

3. ✅ **Peer-reviewed meta-analysis**
   Doo, M. Y., & Park, Y. (2026). A meta-analysis of ChatGPT's influence on learning achievement. *The International Review of Research in Open and Distributed Learning, 27*(1), 265–289. https://doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v27i1.8775
   - Combines 22 studies from 2022–2024 and finds a moderate positive effect of ChatGPT on achievement. Middle and high school students showed a larger effect than undergraduates, but that difference wasn't statistically significant. About 71% of participants were undergraduates.

4. ✅ **Peer-reviewed meta-analysis**

   - I confirmed that this article exists and where it was published, but I couldn't read the abstract (paywalled). I can't summarize its findings without seeing them. A library copy is the way in.

5. ✅ **Peer-reviewed systematic review**
   Alfarwan, A. (2025). Generative AI use in K-12 education: A systematic review. *Frontiers in Education, 10*, Article 1647573. https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2025.1647573
   - Reviews 30 studies of generative AI (mostly ChatGPT) in K-12 settings. Most of the work it found is on high school students, focuses on STEM, and measures psychological outcomes like motivation more often than test performance. I read only part of the full text.

6. ✅ **Peer-reviewed article (survey and interviews)**
   Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), Article 6. https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006
   - A survey of 666 people of various ages found that heavier AI use went with weaker critical thinking, linked through cognitive offloading. Younger participants relied on AI more.
   - What to watch for: the author says this shows correlation, not causation, and the participants were the general public, not high schoolers. Crossref also lists a correction.

### Reports from international and research organizations

7. **International agency report**
   OECD. (2026). *PISA 2025 results (Volume I): Future-ready students*. OECD Publishing. https://doi.org/10.1787/73451bc5-en (see Chapter 4, "Students' use of artificial intelligence for schoolwork")
   - 15-year-olds across many countries reported how they use AI for schoolwork: "to help me learn," or for tasks like summarizing readings, doing research, and drafting assignments. After accounting for family background, weekly "help me learn" users scored about the same in science as non-users. For the specific tasks, non-users tended to score higher than users.
   - Why it's here: it's huge and international, but the OECD says plainly that these results are correlational. I could only read an archived copy of the OECD page, since the live site blocked me.

8. **Research organization report (evidence review)**
   Walker, S., Fesler, L., Martinez Claeys, J. P., Agnew, C., & Loeb, S. (2026, November 10). *The evidence base on AI in K-12: A November 2026 review*. SCALE Initiative, Stanford Graduate School of Education. [Page](https://scale.stanford.edu/ai/evidence-base-review/evidence-base-ai-k12-november-review). The DOI printed on the page is 10.26300/80wn-ca81. I couldn't confirm it in Crossref, so check that it resolves.
   - Pulls together 52 causal studies. It says AI "appears to be better for learning when students use it for learning support than when they use it for producing work," but that "neither type of use consistently improves learning." It also says this pattern doesn't show that the way students used AI caused the difference.
   - Why it's here: it's a strong roadmap to individual studies. Watch for the fact that many of the studies it draws on are preprints, and most involve college students.

9. **Research organization report (survey)**
   Sidoti, O., Park, E., & Gottfried, J. (2025, January 15). *About a quarter of U.S. teens have used ChatGPT for schoolwork – double the share in 2023*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork-double-the-share-in-2023/
   - In fall 2024, 26% of U.S. teens (ages 13–17) said they used ChatGPT for schoolwork, up from 13% in 2023. Teens were much more accepting of using it to research topics (54%) than to write essays (18%).
   - Why it's here: it shows how common the behavior is, not whether it helps students learn.

10. **Research organization report (survey)**
    McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
    - In fall 2025, about half of U.S. teens said they had used chatbots for schoolwork help, and 10% said they used them for all or most of their schoolwork. 59% said AI cheating happens at their school at least somewhat often.
    - The same caveat applies: this is about how teens use chatbots and what they think of them, not about learning.

### Preprint

11. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed**
    Kosmyna, N., Hauptmann, E., Yuan, Y. T., Situ, J., Liao, X.-H., Beresnitzky, A. V., Braunstein, I., & Maes, P. (2025). *Your brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of cognitive debt when using an AI assistant for essay writing task* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2506.08872
    - 54 participants wrote essays using ChatGPT, a search engine, or no tools while their brain activity was measured with EEG (sensors on the scalp). The ChatGPT users showed the weakest brain connectivity and felt the least ownership of their essays.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plenty:** surveys on how many teens use chatbots, and meta-analyses that mostly cover college students.
- **Thin:** high-school-specific experiments. I confirmed only two (Bastani and Fütterer).
- **Not found:** any study that sorts high schoolers by whether they *checked their work* with a chatbot or *got answers* from it, then measured what they learned. The closest are Bastani, which compared two tool designs rather than student choices, the OECD report, which compared kinds of use but only by correlation, and the Stanford review.
- **Almost nothing long-term:** most studies run weeks, not semesters.
- **Left out on purpose:** you'll probably come across Wang & Fan (2025), a meta-analysis in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* that reported large learning gains from ChatGPT. Crossref shows it was **retracted** on April 22, 2026, over "discrepancies in the meta-analysis," so I haven't listed it.

## Questions these results raise

- Checking your work isn't quite the same thing as using a tool with built-in safeguards, which is what Bastani tested.
- Students who choose to check their work might already be stronger or more motivated students. Both the OECD and the Stanford review warn about this kind of self-selection.
- Fütterer found that a specially designed AI didn't beat plain ChatGPT. That sits a little awkwardly next to Bastani's result.
