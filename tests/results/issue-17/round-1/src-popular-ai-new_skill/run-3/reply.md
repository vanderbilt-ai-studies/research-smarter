1. **Peer-reviewed article (PNAS).** Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey, comparing a standard ChatGPT-style tutor with one built to protect learning. With access, grades rose 48% for the standard tutor and 127% for the guarded one. Once access was removed, the standard-tutor group scored 17% lower than students who never had access. The guarded tutor largely avoided that drop. *The publisher's page blocked me, so this comes from the abstract on Crossref, not the full text.*

2. **Peer-reviewed article (Computers & Education).** Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, Article 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   This review combines 69 experimental studies from 2022–2024, mostly at the university level and often in language classes. Overall, ChatGPT improved academic performance and lowered mental effort. The authors warn about method problems, including small samples and tests that may measure the quality of ChatGPT's output rather than what students learned. *I read the abstract only.*

4. **Peer-reviewed article (British Journal of Educational Technology).** Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   A randomized experiment with 117 university students on a writing task. They got help from ChatGPT, a human expert, writing-analytics tools, or no extra tool. The ChatGPT group improved their essay scores most, but knowledge gain and transfer didn't differ across groups. The ChatGPT group also showed fewer self-monitoring steps, such as evaluating their own work. *Open preprint copy: https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09315*

5. **Peer-reviewed article (Computers in Human Behavior).** Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, Article 108386. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386
   91 university students researched a science question using either ChatGPT or Google. The ChatGPT group reported less mental effort but gave lower-quality reasoning in their final recommendations.

**Preprint, not yet peer reviewed**

6. Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   Two pre-registered lab experiments in coding lessons found no overall effect of chatbots on learning. Students who used them as a *substitute* (for example, generating solutions to exercises) covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used them as a *complement* (asking for explanations) understood more. Chatbots also widened the gap between students with low and high prior knowledge. *Why it's here: it's the closest match to your "how they use it" sub-question. Watch for: it hasn't been peer reviewed, and the participants weren't high schoolers.*

- **High school evidence is thin.** Only Bastani et al. and the World Bank trial studied high schoolers. Most of the experiments are with university students.
- **No study I found measured "checking your work" directly.** The closest comparisons are Lehmann's substitute-versus-complement split and Bastani's guarded-versus-unguarded tutor. In Bastani's study the *tool design* differed, not the students' choices.
- **Homework specifically** (students using chatbots alone, at home) barely shows up. Most studies took place in lab sessions or supervised settings.
- **No long-term studies.** Everything I found ran for weeks, not years.

## Questions the results raise

- Whether "asking for explanations" is the same thing as "checking work," or a third way of using chatbots.
- Whether the gap in these studies comes from how students *choose* to use the tool or from how the tool is *built*.
- Lehmann found that prior knowledge matters. That raises whether the students who check their work were already the stronger students to begin with.
