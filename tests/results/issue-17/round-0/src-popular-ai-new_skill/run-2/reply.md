**Peer-reviewed articles.** These three meet your 3-peer-reviewed requirement. I confirmed each journal and DOI through Crossref or PubMed Central. The publishers' own pages blocked me, so I worked from abstracts, not full texts.

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*). Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey. Students practiced with either a plain ChatGPT-style tool or a "tutor" version built with safeguards. Both groups did better during practice. Once the AI was taken away, the plain-tool group did worse than students who never had it, and the safeguards largely prevented that drop. Crossref also lists a correction published in August 2025 (10.1073/pnas.2518204122). I haven't seen what it changes.

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers & Education*). Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   A meta-analysis that pools 69 experimental studies from 2022–2024. I couldn't open the abstract. Search summaries say it found better academic performance and more higher-order thinking compared with control groups, but no change in self-efficacy. Check that against the abstract.

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Journal of Intelligence*). Zhao, Y., Yue, Y., Sun, Z., Jiang, Q., & Li, G. (2025). Does generative artificial intelligence improve students' higher-order thinking? A meta-analysis based on 29 experiments and quasi-experiments. *Journal of Intelligence, 13*(12), 160. https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence13120160
   Pools 29 studies and finds a moderate positive effect on higher-order thinking. The effect was largest for problem-solving and small for creativity. It was strongest for interventions lasting 8–16 weeks and for students with stronger self-regulated learning skills. The studies mix K-12 and college students.

**Research organization and agency reports**

4. **Research organization report** (RAND). Schwartz, H. L., & Diliberti, M. K. (2026). *More students use AI for homework, and more believe it harms critical thinking: Selected findings from the American Youth Panel* (RR-A4742-1). RAND Corporation. https://doi.org/10.7249/RRA4742-1
   A December 2025 survey of 1,214 people aged 12–29. Homework use among middle school and older students rose from 48% to 62% between May and December 2025. 67% said that the more students use AI for schoolwork, the more it will harm their critical thinking. Getting direct answers was the only use that many students (45%) called cheating. *Why it's here:* it's a survey of what students do and believe, not a measure of whether they learned.

5. **Research organization report** (Pew). McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/

**Preprints, not yet peer reviewed**

8. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047) [Preprint]. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.09047
   Two lab experiments found no overall effect of chatbots on learning. The effect depended on how students used them. Generating solutions let students cover more topics but understand each one less. Asking for explanations improved understanding. Chatbots also widened the gap between students with more and less prior knowledge. *Why it's here:* it tests the kind of use difference you care about. Search summaries say the participants were university coding students, which I couldn't confirm on the page. I found no published journal version.

9. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Abdelghani, R., Kaiser, P., & Murayama, K. (2026). *Regulating the AI tutor: Intentions, help-seeking, and self-regulated learning in adolescent GenAI use* (arXiv:2606.08568) [Preprint]. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2606.08568
   Chat logs from 98 ninth graders in Germany who used an AI tutor to prepare for a math exam. Before the chat, most students said they wanted step-by-step support. In the chat, they mostly asked for direct help and almost never checked or evaluated what the AI told them. Post-test scores were lower than pre-test scores. *Why it's here:* it's one of the only studies of how teenagers actually use a chatbot. It covers one session and the authors call it a work in progress.

## Questions the results raise

- In Bastani et al., the guardrails were built into the tool. That leaves open whether "checking vs. getting answers" is a choice students make or something the tool's design pushes them toward.
- In Abdelghani et al., students *meant* to use the tutor for support but drifted toward asking for answers. Your position treats the type of use as fairly stable, and this finding suggests it may not be.
- Lehmann et al. found the gap grew with prior knowledge. Who tends to check their work in the first place may matter as much as the checking itself.
