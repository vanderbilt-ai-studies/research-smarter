## Sources

**Peer-reviewed research** (all six count toward your requirement of at least 3 peer-reviewed sources)

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*). Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   This was a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey. Some got a standard ChatGPT-style GPT-4 tutor and some got a tutor with built-in safeguards. While they had access, both groups did better on practice work. Once access was removed, students who'd had the standard tutor scored about 17% lower than students who never had AI. The safeguarded tutor mostly prevented that drop. *The publisher's page blocked me, so I worked from the abstract on an indexing site.*

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers & Education*). Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   This combines 69 experiments from 2022–2024. Across them, ChatGPT improved academic performance, motivation, and higher-order thinking, and it reduced mental effort. It had no effect on self-efficacy. Most of the studies were at universities, and the authors flag weaknesses in how the studies measured learning. *I read the abstract only.*

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence*). Meyer, J., Jansen, T., Schiller, R., Liebenow, L. W., Steinbach, M., Horbach, A., & Fleckenstein, J. (2024). Using LLMs to bring evidence-based feedback into the classroom: AI-generated feedback increases secondary students' text revision, motivation, and positive emotions. *Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence, 6*, 100199. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.caeai.2023.100199
   This was a randomized study of 459 secondary students learning English as a foreign language. Students who revised an argumentative essay using GPT-3.5 feedback revised it better and reported more motivation and more positive emotions than students who revised without feedback. The gains were small to moderate. *The records disagree on whether it's dated 2023 or 2024. Check the journal page for the year.*

4. **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*). Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   This was a lab experiment with 117 university students on a writing task. Students with ChatGPT improved their essay scores the most. However, they didn't gain more knowledge or transfer it better than the other groups, and they engaged less in planning and monitoring their own work. *I read the abstract only.*

5. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers in Human Behavior*). Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, 108386. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386
   91 university students researched a science issue using either ChatGPT or Google. The ChatGPT group found the work less mentally demanding, but the reasoning in their final recommendations was weaker.

6. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Societies*). Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), 6. https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006
   This combined surveys and interviews with 666 participants. People who used AI more often scored lower on critical thinking, and handing mental work off to the tools ("cognitive offloading") explained part of that link. The design is correlational, so it shows a relationship, not a cause. The participants were adults, not high schoolers, and the journal has published a correction to the article.

**Preprint**

7. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.09047
   *Why it's here:* it's the only study I found that directly compares different ways of using a chatbot. Treat it with care until it's peer reviewed. The study used two lab experiments and a field study with university students. Students who had the chatbot do work for them, like generating solutions to exercises, covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who asked it for explanations understood more. Overall there was no net effect, and the chatbot widened the gap between students who started with high and low prior knowledge.

**Research organization reports**

8. **Research organization report.** Schwartz, H. L., & Diliberti, M. K. (2026). *More students use AI for homework, and more believe it harms critical thinking: Selected findings from the American Youth Panel* (RR-A4742-1). RAND Corporation. https://doi.org/10.7249/RRA4742-1
   *Why it's here:* it's a survey, so it tells you how students use AI and what they believe about it, not whether it helps them learn. Between May and December 2025, the share of students using AI for homework rose from 48% to 62%. In December, 67% said AI use harms critical thinking. Most students didn't count common uses as cheating, except getting direct homework answers.

9. **Research organization report.** McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
   *Why it's here:* a large, carefully sampled survey of U.S. teens (1,458, surveyed fall 2025). Like the RAND report, it describes use, not learning. 54% of teens have used chatbots for schoolwork, and 10% do all or most of their schoolwork with chatbot help. 59% say students at their school use AI to cheat at least somewhat often.

10. **Research organization report.** Sidoti, O., Park, E., & Gottfried, J. (2025, January 15). *About a quarter of U.S. teens have used ChatGPT for schoolwork – double the share in 2023*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork/
    *Why it's here:* it shows how attitudes and use changed from 2023 to 2024. 26% of teens used ChatGPT for schoolwork, up from 13%. 54% said using it to research topics is acceptable, but only 18% said the same for writing essays.

## What I found and didn't find

- **High school evidence is thin.** Only Bastani and Meyer actually studied secondary students. The rest of the experiments, and most of the studies in the Deng meta-analysis, involved university students.
- **No study I found tested your exact distinction:** high schoolers who choose to check their work versus high schoolers who copy answers, on real homework. Lehmann compares types of use, but with university students. Bastani compares two tool designs, not student choices.
- **Long-term evidence is scarce.** Most studies ran for weeks, not semesters.
- **A source I removed.** A widely cited 2025 meta-analysis (Wang & Fan, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*) reported large learning gains from ChatGPT. It was **retracted** in 2026. Search results still show it without any warning, so watch for other sources that cite it.

## Questions the results raise

- What counts as "checking your work" with a chatbot is still unclear in the research. Getting feedback on an essay, asking for an explanation, and comparing your answer to the bot's may not work the same way.
- Several studies found students felt they'd learned more than they had, so how students say they use chatbots may not match how they actually use them.
