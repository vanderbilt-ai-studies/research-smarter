1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*)
   Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   This was a field experiment with about 1,000 high school math students in Turkey. Some got a standard ChatGPT-style tutor and some got a "GPT Tutor" built to protect learning. Both raised scores during practice. Once access was removed, the standard-tutor group did about 17% worse on exams than students who never had AI, and the GPT Tutor's guardrails largely prevented that drop. *The PNAS page blocked me, so this comes from the Crossref abstract. Crossref also lists a correction (Aug 2025), so check what it changed.*

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*)
   Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   In this randomized lab experiment, 117 university students wrote essays with ChatGPT, a human expert, writing-analytics tools, or no extra tool. The ChatGPT group improved its essay scores more but showed no significant difference in knowledge gain or transfer. The authors call the pattern "metacognitive laziness." *I read the Crossref abstract only; the Wiley page blocked me.*

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Scientific Reports*)
   Kestin, G., Miller, K., Klales, A., Milbourne, T., & Ponti, G. (2025). AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: An RCT introducing a novel research-based design in an authentic educational setting. *Scientific Reports, 15*, 17458. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6 · [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)
   This was a randomized trial with 194 students in a Harvard introductory physics course. Students who used a custom AI tutor at home scored higher on post-tests (median 4.5 vs. 3.5) than students in an in-class active-learning lesson, in less time. The tutor was designed around the course's teaching methods, so it wasn't a general chatbot.

4. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Societies*, MDPI)

Frequent AI use was linked to lower critical-thinking scores, partly through "cognitive offloading," and younger participants relied on AI more. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed**

8. Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.09047 · [arXiv page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047)
   It's here because it directly tests *how* students use chatbots, but it hasn't been through peer review and the abstract doesn't give participants' ages. It reports two pre-registered lab experiments that found no overall effect on learning. Students who used the AI to *substitute* for work (generating solutions) covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used it to *complement* their work (asking for explanations) understood more. The AI also widened the gap between students with low and high prior knowledge. It doesn't count toward your peer-reviewed minimum.

## Questions these results raise

- A tool built to give hints rather than answers isn't the same as a student who decides to use a chatbot to check their work. Which of the two your position is really about may matter.
- Lehmann's finding about prior knowledge suggests the effect may depend on who the student is as well as how they use the tool.
- Several studies measure scores *while* AI is available and again *after* it's taken away. Which of those counts as "learning" in your paper is something to decide.
