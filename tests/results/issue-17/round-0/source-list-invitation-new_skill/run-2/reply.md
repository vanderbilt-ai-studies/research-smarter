**Peer-reviewed research articles**

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Vereecke, J., Vanherle, R., Nassen, L.-M., Boen, H., Eggermont, S., Beullens, K., Vandenbosch, L., & Schreurs, L. (2026). More than disconnecting? School smartphone bans and adolescents' social wellbeing. *Frontiers in Developmental Psychology, 4*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fdpys.2026.1796471
   One Belgian secondary school (960 students) was surveyed eight times over the year a total ban began. Right after the ban, there was no change in loneliness, friend support, or cyberbullying victimization, and cyberbullying perpetration dipped slightly. Over the year, loneliness rose a little. There was no comparison school.

2. **Peer-reviewed article.** Bonilla Aparicio, E., Sigmarsdottir, B., Haskell, E., Sirotina, U., Sveinbjornsdottir, B., Asgeirsdottir, B. B., Valdimarsdottir, H. B., Odgers, C. L., Sigfusdottir, I. D., Allegrante, J. P., & Halldorsdottir, T. (2026). School phone bans and adolescent mental health in Iceland. *JAMA Network Open, 9*(10), e2640893. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.40893 (full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13714660)
   This study compared 70 Icelandic schools before and after 22 of them adopted bans, using national survey data from students aged 13–15. It found no significant link between bans and mental health, nor with the secondary outcomes, which included in-person bullying and cyberbullying.

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
   This was a one-time survey of 1,398 Dutch students in grades 9–12 at schools with partial or full bans. Every school had some ban, so there was no no-ban comparison. Bullying and cyberbullying didn't differ between ban types. Full bans were linked to weaker student–teacher connectedness and, for girls, a weaker sense of school belonging.

4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211 (full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11984610/)
   The study surveyed 1,227 students aged 12–15 at 30 English schools. Students at restrictive schools used phones and social media less during school hours. Across whole weekdays and weekends, though, their use didn't differ, and neither did their wellbeing.

I couldn't open the full text, so the details here come from the university's press release (https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/news/2026/school-phone-bans-or-not-digital-lives-of-students-affect-the-school-day). 9. **Working paper.** Allcott, H., Baron, E. J., Dee, T., Duckworth, A. L., Gentzkow, M., & Jacob, B. (2026). *The effects of school phone bans: National evidence from lockable pouches* (NBER Working Paper No. 35132). https://www.nber.org/papers/w35132

## What I found and didn't find

- **Almost no study measures in-person talk directly.** Most use stand-ins like friend support, loneliness, or belonging. The only direct accounts I found come from interviews and student surveys.
- I left out one survey study that asked high schoolers directly about face-to-face interaction (*Journal of Social Sciences*, 2025, published by SCIRP). I couldn't confirm how that journal reviews papers. If you want to use it, check the journal on Think. Check. Submit. first.
- **I found nothing that measures conflict by time of day.** None of these studies tracks conflict after school separately from conflict during school, so none can show it "moving." The closest is the qualitative SMART Schools paper (source 5).
- **The bullying results point in different directions.** Abrahamsson and Böttger & Zierer report decreases. The Icelandic, Dutch, and U.S. pouch studies find little or no change.
- **Many samples are ages 12–15**, and peer-reviewed U.S. high school studies were thin.

## Questions these raise

- When a study finds that cyberbullying didn't change, that could mean conflict moved elsewhere, or that it simply stayed the same. None of these designs can tell the two apart.
- In the Lancet study, school-day phone use dropped but whole-day use didn't. That could matter for the "after school" half of your position, but how is your call.
- "Conflict" covers different things across these sources: bullying, cyberbullying, suspensions, and "blow-ups." How you define it will shape which sources count as evidence.
