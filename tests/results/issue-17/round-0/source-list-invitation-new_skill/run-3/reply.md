## What I found and didn't find

- **Lots of evidence on bullying, but it points different ways.** Spain and Norway found drops. Australia, the Netherlands and the US national study found little or no difference between ban and no-ban schools.
- **Thin evidence on face-to-face interaction.** It mostly comes from what students and staff say (Bar et al.; Goodyear 2026). No study I found actually counts or observes conversations, and two studies say outright that they didn't measure it.
- **Nothing tracks when or where conflict happens.** I found no study that measures bullying or cyberbullying separately in school hours and after school before and after a ban. That's exactly the gap behind the second half of your position.
- **Few studies of high school specifically.** Most samples mix middle and high school ages, and most cover only the first few months or years.

## Questions the results raise

- The Goodyear (2026) press release describes conflict moving the *other* way too: fights that start online outside school end up face to face at school. That's worth weighing against the idea that conflict only moves to after school.
- The studies measure different things: bullying, cyberbullying, officially reported cases, perceived online bullying. Which of these your word "conflict" means will shape which sources can speak to your position.
- One student in Bar et al. said bullying rose because students were making up for boredom. That connects to the counterargument in your log.
- Vanluydt et al. describe Goodyear (2025) as showing that students use phones more after school to compensate. Goodyear's own paper reports similar total weekday and weekend use. It's worth reading both to see whether those two descriptions match.
