2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Frontiers in Developmental Psychology*). Vereecke, J., Vanherle, R., Nassen, L.-M., Boen, H., Eggermont, S., Beullens, K., Vandenbosch, L., & Schreurs, L. (2026). More than disconnecting? School smartphone bans and adolescents' social wellbeing. *Frontiers in Developmental Psychology, 4*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fdpys.2026.1796471
   - The researchers followed 960 students at one Belgian secondary school over eight waves: one survey before a total ban and seven after it.
   - Two weeks after the ban, there was no change in loneliness, friend support or cyberbullying victimization, and cyberbullying perpetration dipped slightly. Over the year, loneliness rose modestly.

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Journal of Youth and Adolescence*). Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
   - The study surveyed 1,398 Dutch students (average age 16) at 24 schools. Some banned phones only in class (partial bans); others banned them on the whole school grounds (full bans).
   - Bullying and cyberbullying didn't differ between the two kinds of ban, and neither did wellbeing. Full bans were linked to weaker connection between students and teachers, and to lower school belonging among girls.

4. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Applied Economic Analysis*). Beneito, P., & Vicente-Chirivella, Ó. (2022). Banning mobile phones in schools: Evidence from regional-level policies in Spain. *Applied Economic Analysis, 30*(90), 153–175. https://doi.org/10.1108/AEA-05-2021-0112
   - The study compares two Spanish regions that banned phones in 2015 with Spain's other regions.
   - It reports that officially recorded bullying cases fell by about 9.5–18%. Those are recorded cases, not cyberbullying specifically.

## What I found and didn't find

- **More in-person talk.** None of these studies directly measures conversation. The evidence comes from what students and staff report in interviews (7, 8), and the survey studies measure related things like loneliness and connectedness (2, 3).
- **Bullying at school.** Results differ: some studies report decreases (4, 6, 9) and others find no difference (2, 3).
- **Conflict after school.** I found almost nothing that measures conflict outside school hours. The closest are:
  - phone use making up for itself outside school (1)
  - the interview reports of online conflict carrying on (7)
- **Gaps.** Very little evidence is from the US, specifically on high school students, or longer than one school year.

## Questions these results raise

- Your position depends on what counts as "conflict." Cyberbullying, bullying recorded by the school, and fights that start online and spill into school are measured differently in these studies.
- Some studies compare schools at a single point in time (1, 3); others follow the same students before and after a ban (2). The two designs can support different kinds of claims.
