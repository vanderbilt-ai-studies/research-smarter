1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*)
   Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), Article e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey. Students used either a standard ChatGPT-style tool or a tutor version with safeguards. While they had access, grades on practice problems rose 48% with the standard tool and 127% with the tutor. Once access was taken away, the standard-tool group scored 17% lower than students who never had access. The safeguards "largely mitigated" that drop. *The PNAS page blocked me, so I read only the abstract through Crossref. Crossref also lists a correction issued in August 2025, which I haven't read.*

2. **Peer-reviewed article, systematic review** (*Computers & Education*)
   Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, Article 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   A review of 69 experimental studies from 2022–2024. Overall, ChatGPT improved academic performance and higher-order thinking and reduced mental effort. Most of the studies were done at universities. The authors themselves warn about weak methods in many of the studies. *Abstract only.*

3. **Peer-reviewed article, meta-analysis** (*International Review of Research in Open and Distributed Learning*)
   Doo, M. Y., & Park, Y. (2026). A meta-analysis of ChatGPT's influence on learning achievement. *The International Review of Research in Open and Distributed Learning, 27*(1), 265–289. https://doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v27i1.8775
   Combines 22 studies and finds a moderate positive effect on achievement (g = 0.573). Middle and high school students showed a larger effect (g = 0.928) than undergraduates (g = 0.538), but the difference wasn't statistically significant. About 71% of participants were undergraduates. *Abstract only.*

4. **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*)
   Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   A randomized lab study of 117 university students writing essays with ChatGPT, a human expert, analytics tools, or no help. The ChatGPT group improved their essay scores the most, but no group gained more knowledge or did better on transfer. The authors suggest ChatGPT may encourage dependence, which they call "metacognitive laziness." *Abstract only.*

5. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers in Human Behavior*)
   Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, Article 108386. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386
   91 university students researched a science question using either ChatGPT-3.5 or Google. The ChatGPT group reported less mental effort but produced weaker reasoning and arguments. *Abstract only, read on the [TUM research portal](https://portal.fis.tum.de/en/publications/cognitive-ease-at-a-cost-llms-reduce-mental-effort-but-compromise/).*

**Not yet peer reviewed**

6. **Working paper, not yet peer reviewed** (posted on an NBER conference site)
   Strömberg, D., Lei, V., & Wu, Y. (2026). *The generative AI learning penalty: Evidence from Chinese secondary education* [Unpublished manuscript]. https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f240545.pdf
   30 months of school records for 26,811 students in grades 7–12 in China. When students started using AI, homework scores rose 18% and homework time fell 30%, but monthly exam scores dropped 20% within six months. About 80% of AI users finished homework unusually fast with high scores, which the authors read as outsourcing, and that group accounts for most of the losses. AI users who kept spending normal time on homework had small losses. *Why it's here:* it's the largest and longest study of secondary students I found. *What to watch for:* it hasn't been through peer review, and "outsourcing" is inferred from homework time rather than observed directly. I read the title page and abstract.

7. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed** (arXiv)
   Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.09047
   Two pre-registered lab experiments found no overall effect on learning. Students who used the chatbot *instead of* doing the work (for example, having it generate solutions) covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used it *alongside* their own work (for example, asking for explanations) understood more. *Why it's here:* it's the closest match I found to comparing types of use. *What to watch for:* no journal version is listed, and the abstract doesn't say who the participants were. Abstract only.

**Survey reports**

8. **Research organization report** (Pew Research Center)
   McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
   A fall 2025 survey of 1,458 U.S. teens aged 13–17. 54% have used chatbots for schoolwork, and 10% do all or most of their schoolwork with them. 59% say AI cheating happens at least somewhat often at their school. *Why it's here:* it shows how common the behavior is and what teens believe. It can't tell you whether chatbots help or hurt learning.

9. **Research organization report** (Pew Research Center)
   Sidoti, O., Park, E., & Gottfried, J. (2025, January 15). *About a quarter of U.S. teens have used ChatGPT for schoolwork – double the share in 2023*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork/
   26% of teens used ChatGPT for schoolwork in fall 2024, up from 13% in 2023. 54% think using it to research new topics is acceptable, compared with 29% for solving math problems and 18% for writing essays. *Same caveat as above:* it measures opinions and usage, not learning.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Most studies are on college students.** I found only two studies focused on high schoolers, Bastani and Strömberg, plus the middle/high school subgroup in Doo & Park.
- **No study I found directly measured "checking work" as a type of use.** The closest are Lehmann's "complementing vs. substituting" and Strömberg's use of homework time to guess at outsourcing. Neither is quite the same as checking work.
- **Long-term evidence is thin.** Strömberg is the only study I found that follows students for more than a single semester.
- **A retracted meta-analysis to avoid:** Wang & Fan (2025), in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*. It's widely cited, including by Strömberg's paper, but its Crossref record is now titled "RETRACTED ARTICLE." If you see it cited elsewhere, keep that in mind.
- **For four of the five peer-reviewed articles I read only the abstract.** The exceptions are Doo & Park and the Pew reports, whose pages opened; for Doo & Park I still read only the abstract.

## Questions the results raise

- Whether "checking work" can be observed by researchers, or only inferred from behavior like time spent, which ties back to the counterargument in your log about what teachers can and can't see.
- How much of the harm comes from the student's choices and how much from the tool's design, since Bastani's safeguarded tutor changed the result.
- Whether findings from university students carry over to high schoolers.
