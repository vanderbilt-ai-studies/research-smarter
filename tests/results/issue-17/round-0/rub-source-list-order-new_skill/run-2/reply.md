### Peer-reviewed articles (5, which meets your requirement of at least 3)

**Peer-reviewed article** (*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*)
Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey. Some got a tutor that worked like standard ChatGPT ("GPT Base"), and others got one built with prompts meant to protect learning ("GPT Tutor"). Both raised scores during practice. Once access was taken away, the GPT Base students did 17% worse than students who never had access, while the GPT Tutor version largely avoided that drop. PNAS published a correction to this paper in August 2025, so check what it changed.

**Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers & Education*)
Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
A meta-analysis (a study that pools results from many studies) of 69 experiments from 2022 to 2024. It reports that ChatGPT improved academic performance, motivation and higher-order thinking, reduced mental effort, and had no effect on self-efficacy. The authors themselves warn that weaknesses in the studies they pooled mean the results should be read cautiously.

**Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*)
Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
A randomized experiment with 117 university students doing a writing task with ChatGPT, a human expert, analytics tools, or no extra help. The ChatGPT group's essay scores improved more, but their ability to apply what they learned to new tasks (transfer) didn't differ. The authors coin "metacognitive laziness" for students handing off the work of planning and monitoring their own learning.

**Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers in Human Behavior*)
Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, 108386. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386
91 university students were randomly assigned to research a science question using either ChatGPT or Google. The ChatGPT group reported less mental effort, but their final recommendations were weaker and less well argued.

**Peer-reviewed article** (*Societies*, MDPI)
Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), 6. https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006
A survey-and-interview study of 666 people of different ages. People who used AI more often scored lower on critical thinking, and the link ran largely through "cognitive offloading" (handing mental work to the tool). The study is correlational (it shows a link, not a cause), it's about adults generally rather than students, and Crossref lists a correction to it.

### Working paper and preprint (not yet peer reviewed)

**Working paper, not yet peer reviewed** (CEPR Discussion Paper)
Strömberg, D., Lei, V., & Wu, Y. (2026). *The generative AI learning penalty: Evidence from Chinese secondary education* (CEPR Discussion Paper No. 21577). Centre for Economic Policy Research. https://ideas.repec.org/p/cpr/ceprdp/21577.html
30 months of data on 26,811 students in grades 7 to 12 in China. Using AI raised homework scores by 18% and cut homework time by 30%, but monthly exam scores fell within six months, and entrance-exam scores later fell too. The authors trace most of the losses to students outsourcing their homework.
*Why it's here:* it's the largest study I found of secondary students. *Watch for:* it hasn't been through peer review, and it's from one region of China.

**Preprint, not yet peer reviewed** (arXiv)
Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.09047
Lab experiments and a field study found no overall effect on learning. Students who used the tool in place of their own work (for example, generating solutions) covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used it alongside their own work (for example, asking for explanations) understood more deeply. Access also widened the gap between students with weaker and stronger prior knowledge.
*Why it's here:* it directly compares different ways of using a chatbot. *Watch for:* it isn't peer reviewed, and the abstract doesn't say the participants were high schoolers (the field data came from university coding courses).

### Research organization reports

**Research organization report** (Pew Research Center)
Sidoti, O., Park, E., & Gottfried, J. (2025, January 15). *About a quarter of U.S. teens have used ChatGPT for schoolwork – double the share in 2023*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork-double-the-share-in-2023/
A fall 2024 survey of 1,391 U.S. teens. 26% had used ChatGPT for schoolwork, up from 13% in 2023. 54% said using it to research new topics is acceptable, against 18% for writing essays.

**Research organization report** (Pew Research Center)
McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
A fall 2025 survey of 1,458 U.S. teens and their parents. 54% of teens had used chatbots for schoolwork help, and about 1 in 10 said they do all or most of their schoolwork with them.
*Why these two are here:* they show how many U.S. teens use chatbots and what they think is acceptable. *Watch for:* they're surveys of opinions and habits, not studies of whether chatbots help anyone learn.

### What I found and didn't find

- **Plenty:** experiments with university students, and good survey data on how many U.S. teens use chatbots.
- **Thin:** studies that measure learning in high schoolers. Only two (Bastani and Strömberg), and neither is in the U.S.
- **Not found:** any study that measures students who use a chatbot specifically to *check* work they've already done. The closest are the "replace vs. support" split in Lehmann, the guardrail tutor in Bastani, and Strömberg's split by homework time.
- **Not found:** long-term studies. Strömberg's 30 months is the longest.
- **Left out on purpose:** a widely shared 2025 meta-analysis by Wang and Fan in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* was retracted in April 2026. Don't cite it if you come across it.

### Additional questions the results raise

- Whether "checking my work" is the same behavior as "asking for explanations" or "keeping homework time the same." The studies define good use in different ways, and your position will depend on which definition you adopt.
- In Bastani, the guardrails were built into the tool, not chosen by the students. That bears on whether good outcomes come from students' habits or from how the tool is designed.
- Lehmann's finding that chatbots widen the gap by prior knowledge raises the possibility that the students who check their work are also the students who were already doing well.
