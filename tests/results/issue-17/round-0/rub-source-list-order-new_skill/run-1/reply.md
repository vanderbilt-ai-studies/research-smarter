## Sources

### Peer-reviewed journal articles
Six of these meet your peer-review requirement: these five plus the conference paper below.

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   A field experiment with almost 1,000 high school math students. Some practiced with a standard ChatGPT-style tool and others with a "tutor" version that has built-in safeguards. Both tools raised scores during practice. On a later exam taken without AI, the standard-tool group scored 17% lower than students who never had access, and the tutor version mostly avoided that drop. *Abstract only; the publisher's page blocked me. Older news coverage calls this "not yet peer reviewed," but it has since been published in PNAS.*

2. **Peer-reviewed article.** Klar, M. (2025). Using ChatGPT is easy, using it effectively is tough? A mixed methods study on K-12 students' perceptions, interaction patterns, and support for learning with generative AI chatbots. *Smart Learning Environments, 12*, Article 32. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40561-025-00385-2
   An experiment with 106 secondary school students. One group got guidance on how to prompt the chatbot. They used its features about twice as often, but both groups learned about the same amount. *Abstract only.*

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Ding, L., O'Berry, R., Tallent, H., Gharti Chhetri G C, S., & Gapud, A. (2026). Learning with large language models: Beyond prompt engineering. *Education and Information Technologies, 31*(9), 2877–2901. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10639-026-13924-2
   Physics students went over their wrong exam answers with ChatGPT. Most engaged only at a surface level, but those who asked deeper follow-up questions showed higher learning gains. The abstract doesn't say whether these were high school or college students. *Abstract only.*

4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   A randomized experiment with 117 *university* students on a writing task. The ChatGPT group improved their essay scores the most, but they gained no more knowledge than the other groups. They also showed fewer self-monitoring behaviors, which the authors call "metacognitive laziness." *Abstract only.*

5. **Peer-reviewed article.** Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), Article 6. https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006
   A survey-and-interview study of 666 people of various ages. People who used AI tools more often scored lower on critical thinking, and the study links this to "cognitive offloading," meaning handing mental work over to the tool. It shows correlation, not cause. One flag: a search result mentioned a published correction (September 2025) that I couldn't check. *Abstract only.*

### Peer-reviewed conference paper

6. **Peer-reviewed conference paper.** Vanzo, A., Pal Chowdhury, S., & Sachan, M. (2025). GPT-4 as a homework tutor can improve student engagement and learning outcomes. In *Proceedings of the 63rd Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (Volume 1: Long Papers)* (pp. 31119–31136). Association for Computational Linguistics. https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.acl-long.1502
   A randomized trial in four high school English-as-a-second-language classes. For the treatment group, regular homework was replaced with interactive GPT-4 homework sessions. Learning didn't suffer, one group learned significantly more, and students liked the sessions more. It was published in a computational linguistics venue, not an education journal.

### Preprint, not yet peer reviewed

7. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047v2) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   Two pre-registered lab experiments plus a field study. Overall, LLMs made no difference to learning. Students who used them to *generate solutions* covered more topics but understood each one less well. Students who used them to *ask for explanations* understood more. The abstract doesn't say how old the students were; an earlier version studied university programming courses. *Why it's here:* it's the closest match to your exact sub-question. *Watch for:* it hasn't been peer reviewed, and it isn't about high schoolers.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plentiful:** studies showing that AI raises performance *while students are using it*, and growing evidence that tool design (guardrails, tutor-style prompts) matters.
- **Thin:** studies of high schoolers doing actual homework at home, without supervision. Most of the high school studies here took place in classrooms or after-school programs, and outside the U.S. (Turkey, Italy, Nigeria, Germany).
- **Couldn't find:** a peer-reviewed study of high schoolers that directly compares "checking my work" with "getting answers." The closest are Lehmann et al. (a preprint, not high school students) and Ding et al. (school level unclear). I also found almost no long-term studies; nearly everything covers weeks, not years.

## Questions the results raise

- Several studies point to *tool design* and *guidance from teachers* as the deciding factor, not only what the student chooses to do. That could matter for how your position is framed.
- In Bastani et al., the guardrailed tutor improved practice scores more than the standard tool but did no better on the exam than no AI at all. That's worth thinking about against the idea that "checking work" helps.
