## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Background.** More than 2,100 schools in 26 states used a four-day week in 2024–25. Six years earlier it was about 1,600 schools in 24 states. Most of these districts are rural. Texas grew fastest, from 30 schools in 2020–21 to 506 in 2024–25. ([Education Week, 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04); [The Journalist's Resource, 2025](https://journalistsresource.org/education/four-day-school-week-research/))

**Key debates**
- **Achievement.** A 2024 study of six states (Morton, Thompson & Kuhfeld, *Economics of Education Review*) found smaller reading gains on four-day schedules overall. The effect was small in rural schools and larger in non-rural ones. An Oregon study of early elementary students found only small average differences. ([Thompson et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9979633))
- **Money.** Districts often switch to save money. One national study found the cuts land mostly on support services like buses and meals, not on teaching ([Thompson, 2021, *National Tax Journal*](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:nattax:doi:10.1086/712916)). Estimates of total savings are modest, around 0.4–2.5% of a budget.
- **Teachers.** A 17-year Texas study (a working paper, not yet peer reviewed) found turnover fell about 2.7 percentage points after districts switched. Even so, more than 1 in 5 teachers still left each year, and studies in Colorado, Oregon, and Missouri found no benefit or a negative effect ([Education Week, 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/do-4-day-school-weeks-attract-and-retain-better-teachers-what-the-largest-study-yet-says/2026/01)).
- **Behavior.** In Colorado, juvenile crime rose about 20% where students were on four-day weeks, mostly property crime ([Fischer & Argyle, 2018](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecoedu/v64y2018icp31-39.html)). An Oklahoma study found fewer fights and less bullying at school.

**Major voices.** Economist Paul N. Thompson appears on many of these studies. Emily Morton and Megan Kuhfeld led the multi-state achievement work. The RAND Corporation did a large 2021 study that included parent surveys. The Annenberg Institute's EdWorkingPapers series publishes newer working papers, and the Education Commission of the States tracks savings estimates.

**Patterns**
- Most studies compare districts that switched with similar districts that didn't. That gets closer to cause and effect than a simple correlation, but it isn't a randomized experiment.
- The evidence comes mostly from a few states: Oregon, Colorado, Oklahoma, and Texas.
- How many hours schools keep may matter more than the number of days. A 12-state study found test-score drops only in schools with low total time in school.
- Parents are often satisfied. In RAND's survey, nearly three-quarters of elementary parents in Idaho, New Mexico, and Oklahoma were very satisfied, even as achievement results came out mixed.

**Open questions.** Researchers name several gaps: effects on school meals, physical education, and child care; whether any recruiting advantage lasts once neighboring districts switch too; how the fifth day is actually used; long-term outcomes; and whether results from rural districts hold for the suburban and city districts now considering the schedule.
