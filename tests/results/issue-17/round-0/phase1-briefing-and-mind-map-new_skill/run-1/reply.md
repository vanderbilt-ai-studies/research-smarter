## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Achievement.** Most studies find test scores fall a little or grow more slowly in four-day districts. Several researchers say the size of the drop depends on how much total instructional time a district cuts. A [University of Oregon HEDCO Institute review](https://hedcoinstitute.uoregon.edu/reports/four-day-school-week) of 11 studies found "little evidence of positive outcomes."
- **Teachers.** Districts often adopt the schedule to attract and keep teachers, and superintendents widely believe it works. A 2026 CALDER working paper on Missouri by [Camp, Anglum, Koedel, Lee, and Nguyen](https://caldercenter.org/publications/effects-four-day-school-week-teacher-recruitment-and-retention) found "no consistent evidence" that it improves recruitment or retention. An [Education Week explainer](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04) mentions a Texas study that found lower teacher turnover, so the evidence isn't settled.
- **Popularity vs. outcomes.** The [RAND/NWEA study *Does Four Equal Five?*](https://www.nwea.org/research/publication/does-four-equal-five-implementation-and-outcomes-of-the-four-day-school-week/) (2021) found families highly satisfied with the schedule. The same study found small cost savings, no improvement in attendance, and slower growth in test scores.

**Major voices**
- Economists Paul Thompson and Emily Morton have written many of the achievement studies.
- RAND and NWEA produced the largest mixed-methods study.
- CALDER, a research center at the American Institutes for Research, published the Missouri teacher research.
- The HEDCO Institute at the University of Oregon compiled the review of 11 studies.

**Patterns**
- Most research comes from rural districts in a few states: Oregon, Colorado, Oklahoma, Missouri, Idaho, and New Mexico.
- The studies lean heavily on test scores and statistical comparisons with similar five-day districts. Interviews and surveys show up less often.
- Education Week reports more than 2,100 schools in 26 states on the schedule as of 2024–25. Nearly two-thirds of Colorado's districts use it.

**Open questions**
- Families have been studied much less than achievement: childcare, parents' jobs, and how students actually spend the off day.
- Research has started on youth crime. Two peer-reviewed studies exist, one from [2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econedurev.2018.03.010) and one from [2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/pam.70101), but I could only confirm their titles and journals, not read their findings.
- "Four-day week" covers schedules that differ a lot, for example in day length and which day is off. A 2026 CALDER report, [*Calendar Confusion*](https://doi.org/10.63302/acqw5993), takes this up.
