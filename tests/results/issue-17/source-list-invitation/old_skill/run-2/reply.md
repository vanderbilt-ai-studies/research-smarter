**Peer-reviewed articles** (seven, so your requirement of five is covered. Bar et al. comes with one caveat, explained in its entry.)

- **Peer-reviewed article.** Abrahamsson, S. (2026). Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health. *Journal of Human Resources*. Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.0224-13403R2
  Norway; middle schools; event-study design using administrative and survey data. The abstract says bans had no average effect on education or mental health but lowered bullying for both girls and boys, with academic gains for girls. I could read only the abstract because the full text is paywalled.

- **Peer-reviewed article.** Bar, E., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., Wade, T. D., & King, D. L. (2025). Student perspectives on banning mobile phones in South Australian secondary schools: A large-scale qualitative analysis. *Computers in Human Behavior*, 108603. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108603
  This is a qualitative analysis of roughly 7,000 open-ended survey answers from more than 1,500 students. Flinders University describes the findings as more face-to-face interaction, better conversations, and fewer fights. **Caveat:** the DOI resolves, but I couldn't open the paper, so this summary comes from the university's news page. That page was also inconsistent about which of two papers these findings came from. Check the paper itself before you rely on it. One search result gave volume 167, which I couldn't confirm.

- **Peer-reviewed article.** Beneito, P., & Vicente-Chirivella, Ó. (2022). Banning mobile phones in schools: Evidence from regional-level policies in Spain. *Applied Economic Analysis, 30*(90), 153–175. https://doi.org/10.1108/AEA-05-2021-0112
  Two Spanish regions banned phones around 2015. The authors compare them with the rest of Spain and report drops in officially reported bullying cases among teenagers of roughly 9.5% to 18%, plus gains in PISA scores. The data are police-reported cases, so they don't separate in-school from out-of-school bullying.

- **Peer-reviewed article (scoping review).** Campbell, M., Edwards, E., Pennell, D., Poed, S., Lister, V., Gillett-Swan, J., Kelly, A., Zec, D., & Nguyen, T.-A. (2024). Evidence for and against banning mobile phones in schools: A scoping review. *Journal of Psychologists and Counsellors in Schools, 34*(3), 242–265. https://doi.org/10.1177/20556365241270394
  The review covers 22 studies on academics, wellbeing, and cyberbullying. It found no randomized trials and says differences in study designs make the findings hard to reconcile. The publisher page blocked me, so I read the abstract on QUT's repository: https://eprints.qut.edu.au/251231/

- **Peer-reviewed article.** Goodyear, V., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe*, 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211
  This study compared 30 English schools and 1,227 students aged 12 to 15. Students at restrictive schools used phones less during school hours but no less overall on weekdays or weekends, and their wellbeing didn't differ. I read the abstract on the University of Birmingham repository, not the full paper.

- **Peer-reviewed article.** King, D. L., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., & Wade, T. (2024). "Phones off while school's on": Evaluating problematic phone use and the social, wellbeing, and academic effects of banning phones in schools. *Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 13*(4), 913–922. https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.2024.00058
  This is a preregistered before-and-after comparison of ban and no-ban schools in South Australia (Grades 7–12, about 1,280 students). There was no difference in school belonging, and bullying (online and offline) fell in both groups. Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11737419/

- **Peer-reviewed article.** Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
  This study compared classroom-only bans with whole-school bans in 24 Dutch schools (1,398 students, average age 16.2). It found no difference in bullying. Full bans were linked to weaker student–teacher connectedness and, for girls, a weaker sense of belonging. The DOI goes to a login page; I read the abstract on Utrecht University's repository.

**Journal commentary** (not original research)

- **Journal commentary.** Weiss, H. A., & Bonell, C. (2025). Smartphone use and mental health: Going beyond school restriction policies. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, 101237. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101237
  This is a response to the Goodyear study. It's here because it names in-person social interaction and cyberbullying as things the SMART Schools study didn't measure and calls for research on them. It's expert opinion, not evidence, so don't use it to support a finding.

  This is a qualitative study using focus groups with 66 students aged 10–18 in six Irish schools. It's here because it asks students directly about cyberbullying and socializing. ## What I found and didn't find

- **Face-to-face talk:** nearly all the evidence is students or teachers describing their experience (Bar et al.). I didn't find a study that directly measured how much students talk in person before and after a ban.
- **Conflict moving after school:** I found no study that tracks whether bullying shifts to after-school hours. The closest are Goodyear et al., where total phone use stayed the same, and the Irish report. Where bullying was measured, results go both ways: decreases in Norway and Spain, no clear difference in South Australia and the Netherlands.
- **Age and place:** most studies cover middle school or early teens, and none are from the U.S. Vanluydt et al. comes closest to a high school sample.
- **Designs:** I found no randomized trials, and most studies span a year or less.

## Questions these results raise

- When a study says bullying fell, it matters whether it counted bullying at school, online, or everywhere, because that changes what it can tell you about your "moves to after school" claim.
- The Dutch finding on connectedness raises the question of whether more talk among students and a stronger sense of belonging are the same thing.
