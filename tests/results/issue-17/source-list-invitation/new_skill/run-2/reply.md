1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*The Lancet Regional Health – Europe*)
   Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, Article 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211
   This study surveyed 1,227 students aged 12–15 at 30 English schools: 20 with restrictive phone rules and 10 with permissive ones. In restrictive schools students used their phones and social media less during school hours, but not less across the week. Mental wellbeing didn't differ between the two groups. Face-to-face interaction and bullying weren't measured.

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Journal of Youth and Adolescence*)
   Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
   This study compared 1,398 Dutch students (average age 16.2) at schools that ban phones only in class with schools that ban them everywhere on school grounds. Wellbeing and bullying didn't differ significantly between the two. Whole-school bans went with lower student–teacher connectedness and, for girls, a weaker sense of belonging at school. I read the abstract only.

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Journal of Behavioral Addictions*)
   King, D. L., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., & Wade, T. (2024). "Phones off while school's on": Evaluating problematic phone use and the social, wellbeing, and academic effects of banning phones in schools. *Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 13*(4), 913–922. https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.2024.00058
   This study surveyed about 1,280 students in Grades 7–12 at five South Australian schools before a phone ban and one month after. The pre-registered design means the authors published their plan before collecting data. Schools with and without the ban didn't differ on school belonging or engagement. Bullying was slightly higher in the ban group, but it fell significantly in both groups. The introduction mentions face-to-face socializing as a claimed benefit of bans, but the study didn't measure it.

4. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Applied Economic Analysis*)
   Beneito, P., & Vicente-Chirivella, Ó. (2022). Banning mobile phones in schools: Evidence from regional-level policies in Spain. *Applied Economic Analysis, 30*(90), 153–175. https://doi.org/10.1108/AEA-05-2021-0112
   This study compared two Spanish regions that banned phones in 2014–2015 with regions that didn't, using police-reported bullying cases and test scores. The authors report "noticeable reductions" in bullying among teenagers in the regions with bans. The data don't record where incidents happened or separate out cyberbullying.

5. **Peer-reviewed article, ahead of print** (*Journal of Human Resources*)
   Abrahamsson, S. (2026). Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health. *Journal of Human Resources*. Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.0224-13403R2
   This study looked at Norwegian middle schools that adopted bans at different times. The final article is paywalled, so I only read the abstract of its 2024 working-paper version, and the published numbers may differ. That abstract says bullying went down for both boys and girls after bans, and that girls' grades and their mental-health care visits improved.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Most evidence is on wellbeing, grades, and general bullying.** None of the number-based studies directly measured conversation or face-to-face time. The evidence for more in-person talk comes from what students and staff say (sources 6, 7, and 9).
- **I found no study that tracks *where* or *when* conflict happens before and after a ban.** No source measured whether bullying moves from school hours to after school. Source 7 comes closest, and it describes conflict flowing in the other direction: from online after school into school.
- **Many samples are younger than high school.** Sources 1 and 5 cover ages roughly 12–15 or middle school. The Dutch study (source 2) is the closest match to high school age.
- **I found no peer-reviewed U.S. studies, and almost nothing long-term.** The South Australian study (source 3) followed students for only one month.

## Questions these results raise

- The Dutch study measured "social connectedness," and the Australian one measured "school belonging." These aren't the same as face-to-face talk, so how you define "interaction" will decide which studies count as evidence for your first sub-question.
- Source 7 describes conflict moving into school, not out of it. That's a different pattern from the one your position predicts, and it's worth looking at closely.
- The studies that find less bullying (Spain, Norway) and the ones that find no change (South Australia, the Netherlands) use different data: police reports and records versus student surveys. That difference may explain part of the split.
