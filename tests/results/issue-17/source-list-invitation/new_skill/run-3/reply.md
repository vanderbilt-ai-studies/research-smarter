## Sources

**Peer-reviewed research**

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Vereecke, J., Vanherle, R., Nassen, L.-M., Boen, H., Eggermont, S., Beullens, K., Vandenbosch, L., & Schreurs, L. (2026). More than disconnecting? School smartphone bans and adolescents' social wellbeing. *Frontiers in Developmental Psychology, 4*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fdpys.2026.1796471
   This study followed 960 students at one Belgian secondary school for a school year after it went from a loose restriction to a total ban, with no comparison school. It found no short-term change in feeling excluded at school or in support from friends, and a small rise in loneliness over the year. Online bullying victimization didn't change; perpetration dipped briefly. It didn't measure in-person bullying or phone use outside school.

2. **Peer-reviewed article (review).** Campbell, M., Edwards, E. J., Pennell, D., Poed, S., Lister, V., Gillett-Swan, J., Kelly, A., Zec, D., & Nguyen, T.-A. (2024). Evidence for and against banning mobile phones in schools: A scoping review. *Journal of Psychologists and Counsellors in Schools, 34*(3), 242–265. https://doi.org/10.1177/20556365241270394
   This is a review of 22 studies on bans and academics, mental health, and cyberbullying. It found no randomized trials, and it says the studies differ so much in design and in what counts as a "ban" that their results are hard to reconcile. *The publisher's page blocked me, so I read only the abstract (through OpenAlex). Read the full text before you rely on its specific bullying findings.*

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Abrahamsson, S. (2026). Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health. *Journal of Human Resources.* Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.0224-13403R2
   This study uses Norwegian administrative and survey data and compares schools before and after their bans. The abstract reports lower bullying for both boys and girls, plus benefits for girls' grades and mental-health care use. *It's paywalled, so I read only the abstract. Note that it covers middle schools, not high schools.*

4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, Article 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211 (free full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11984610/)
   This study compared 1,227 pupils aged 12–15 at 30 English schools with restrictive or permissive phone policies, at a single point in time. Pupils at restrictive schools used their phones less during school hours, but their total weekday and weekend use was no different. Wellbeing, sleep, and classroom behavior also showed no differences. It didn't measure bullying or social interaction.

**Other sources (not peer reviewed)**

5. **Working paper, not peer reviewed.** Allcott, H., Baron, E. J., Dee, T., Duckworth, A. L., Gentzkow, M., & Jacob, B. (2026). *The effects of school phone bans: National evidence from lockable pouches* (NBER Working Paper No. 35132). National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w35132
   This is a national U.S. study of schools that adopted lockable phone pouches. Phone use fell sharply. In the first year, disciplinary incidents rose and wellbeing dropped, then both effects faded or reversed. It found "no clear evidence" of change in perceived online bullying. *It's here because it's the largest U.S. study so far and includes high schools. It hasn't been peer reviewed, and I read only the abstract.*

6. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Khare, S. S., Pouwels, J. L., Hendriks, H., & Otten, R. (2025). *Reconnected or disconnected? Secondary school students' loneliness and problematic social media use following a total smartphone ban* [Preprint]. PsyArXiv. https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/d7jrk_v1
   This preprint surveyed 982 Dutch students before a total ban and three months after it. Social media use during breaks dropped a lot. Emotional loneliness rose slightly, and students who were socially anxious beforehand became lonelier. *It's here because it's one of the few studies looking at social life during breaks. It may change before publication, and I found no published version yet.*

7. **News article (commentary by researchers).** Martin, J. D., & Bakour, C. (2025, June 11). We surveyed 1,500 Florida kids about cellphones and their mental health – what we learned suggests school phone bans may have important but limited effects. *The Conversation.* https://theconversation.com/we-surveyed-1-500-florida-kids-about-cellphones-and-their-mental-health-what-we-learned-suggests-school-phone-bans-may-have-important-but-limited-effects-256970
   Two University of South Florida professors describe their survey of 1,510 kids aged 11–13. Kids at schools that limit phones reported *more* cyberbullying, and the authors say plainly that this doesn't show the bans caused it. *It's here as a lead, not as evidence: it's a summary rather than the study itself, and the students are middle schoolers.*

## What I found and didn't find

- **Face-to-face talk is almost never measured directly.** Studies use stand-ins like loneliness, friend support, or social media use during breaks. I found no peer-reviewed study that counted or observed students actually talking in person.
- **Nothing I found tracks conflict moving to after school.** The cyberbullying measures don't record *when* it happens. The SMART Schools study shows phone *use* shifting outside school, which isn't the same as *conflict* shifting.
- **High school evidence is thin.** Much of it covers middle school or mixed ages, and most comes from Europe.
- **Left out:** a 2025 qualitative study that asked students about socializing during breaks. I saw it only in search results and couldn't confirm that its publisher meets the bar. I also left out a 2026 JCMC study and a 2026 experience-sampling study, because neither measured interaction or conflict.

## Questions these results raise

- Several studies describe a rough first year (more discipline, lower wellbeing) before things settle. That matters for how long after a ban a study measures.
- A loneliness measure and a conversation count can tell different stories about "interaction." Your position makes a claim about one of them.
- Your "moves after school" claim needs evidence about timing, and so far none of these sources measures timing.
