## Sources

**Peer-reviewed articles**

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** King, D. L., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., & Wade, T. (2024). "Phones off while school's on": Evaluating problematic phone use and the social, wellbeing, and academic effects of banning phones in schools. *Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 13*(4), 913–922. https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.2024.00058
   A preregistered comparison of five South Australian secondary schools (grades 7–12, about 1,280 students) with and without a ban, surveyed at the start and again one month later. School belonging didn't differ between ban and no-ban schools. Bullying was slightly higher in the ban group and fell significantly in both groups. It didn't directly measure face-to-face talk.

2. **Peer-reviewed article.** Bar, E., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., Wade, T. D., & King, D. L. (2025). Student perspectives on banning mobile phones in South Australian secondary schools: A large-scale qualitative analysis. *Computers in Human Behavior, 167*, 108603. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108603 (free author copy: [Flinders repository PDF](https://researchnow-admin.flinders.edu.au/ws/portalfiles/portal/152076210/Bar_Student_P2025.pdf))
   About 1,550 students from the same five-school project wrote roughly 7,200 open-ended responses. Students reported benefits for face-to-face social interaction, and they also reported having a harder time managing emotional distress without their phones. I couldn't read the full text, so this summary comes from the abstract and the university's news coverage.

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
   This compares full and partial bans in 24 Dutch schools (1,398 students, average age 16.2). It found no significant differences in well-being or bullying. Full bans were linked to weaker student–teacher connectedness and, for girls, a weaker sense of belonging at school. I read the abstract only.

4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Goodyear, V., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe*, Article 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211
   This covers 1,227 students aged 12–15 in 30 English schools. Restrictive schools had less phone and social media use during school hours, but no difference on weekdays overall or on weekends, and no difference in wellbeing. It didn't measure social interaction or bullying. I couldn't confirm the volume number.

5. **Peer-reviewed article.** Beneito, P., & Vicente-Chirivella, Ó. (2022). Banning mobile phones in schools: Evidence from regional-level policies in Spain. *Applied Economic Analysis, 30*(90), 153–175. https://doi.org/10.1108/AEA-05-2021-0112
   This compares two Spanish regions that banned phones in 2015 with the rest of Spain, using region-level statistics. The abstract reports "noticeable reductions of bullying incidence among teenagers" in the two regions. I couldn't read the results section in full, so I can't confirm the exact percentages reported elsewhere.

6. **Peer-reviewed article.** Allcott, H., Baron, J., Dee, T., Duckworth, A. L., Gentzkow, M., & Jacob, B. (2026). The effects of school phone bans: National evidence from lockable pouches. *The Quarterly Journal of Economics*. Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjag050
   A U.S. study of schools that adopted lockable phone pouches, using teacher surveys, GPS data, test scores and school records. Phone use dropped. Discipline incidents rose in the first year and then faded, and wellbeing appeared to recover and turn positive in later years. It found "no clear evidence" of change in perceived online bullying, which was reported by teachers. I read the abstract only.

**Review**

7. **Peer-reviewed article (scoping review).** Campbell, M., Edwards, E., Pennell, D., Poed, S., Lister, V., Gillett-Swan, J., Kelly, A., Zec, D., & Nguyen, T.-A. (2024). Evidence for and against banning mobile phones in schools: A scoping review. *Journal of Psychologists and Counsellors in Schools, 34*(3), 242–265. https://doi.org/10.1177/20556365241270394
   A preregistered review of 22 studies. It found no randomized trials, and the studies use different definitions of a "ban" and different outcome measures. It covers cyberbullying among other outcomes. I could only see the repository abstract, which doesn't give the cyberbullying findings.

**Research organization report**

8. **Research centre report.** Reynolds, M., Esfandiari, M., & O'Higgins Norman, J. (2025). *Restriction or resilience? Smartphone bans in schools: A qualitative study of the experiences of students*. DCU Anti-Bullying Centre, Dublin City University. [Report page](https://www.dcu.ie/antibullyingcentre/restriction-or-resilience)
   *Why it's here:* it's the only source I found that directly discusses conflict moving outside school. *What to watch for:* it isn't peer reviewed, and it reports what 66 Irish students aged 10–18 believe from focus groups, not measured outcomes. Many students doubted that bans prevent cyberbullying and thought it continues after school. Some said bans don't make students who don't want to talk to each other start talking. The authors say the findings can't be generalized.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Face-to-face talk:** All of the evidence I found comes from what students report. I found no study that observed students and counted their conversations. Three studies measured something nearby (belonging, connectedness), and none of them found a clear gain.
- **Conflict moving after school:** I found no study that measured *when* bullying happens before and after a ban. The idea that it moves appears only in students' opinions in the DCU report. The Goodyear study shows that screen time isn't reduced outside school, which is related but isn't conflict.
- **Bullying overall:** Results are mixed. Spain shows a drop. Australia, the Netherlands and the U.S. show no clear ban effect.
- **High school specifically:** Most samples mix middle and high school. The Dutch study and the U.S. pouch study (which reports high schools separately for test scores) come closest.
- I left out a Florida survey of 11–13-year-olds that found more cyberbullying at restrictive schools. It's an opinion piece with no published study behind it, and it covers a younger age group.

## Questions the results raise

- Whether "more talk" that students report is the same thing as stronger connection, given that belonging measures didn't move. This ties back to the counterargument you wrote down.
- Whether a first-year disruption (as in the U.S. study) means studies with one-month or one-year follow-ups are measuring too early.
- Whether "no change in cyberbullying" supports your claim that conflict moves, or just shows the ban didn't touch it.
