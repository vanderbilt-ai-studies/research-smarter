### Peer-reviewed research

**1. Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*)
Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. Free full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/
- **What it is:** A randomized experiment with nearly 1,000 students in grades 9–11 at one high school in Turkey, in fall 2023.
- **Practice results:** During practice, students with a plain ChatGPT-style tool scored 48% higher than students with no AI. Students with a "tutor" version that had safeguards scored 127% higher.
- **Exam results:** On a later exam taken without AI, the plain-tool group scored 17% lower than students who never had AI. The tutor group's harm was "largely mitigated."
- **How students used it:** The authors read students' chat logs and found that students using the plain tool "most often simply ask for the answer."
- **Things to watch:** The sessions were in-class practice, not homework done at home. The publisher's DOI page blocked me, so this link is the free government-archive copy. Crossref also lists a correction notice for this article, and I didn't see what it changes.

**2. Peer-reviewed conference paper** (*Proceedings of ACL 2025*, Long Papers)
Vanzo, A., Pal Chowdhury, S., & Sachan, M. (2025). GPT-4 as a homework tutor can improve student engagement and learning outcomes. In *Proceedings of the 63rd Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (Volume 1: Long Papers)* (pp. 31119–31136). https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1502
- **What it is:** A randomized trial in four high school classes learning English as a second language. GPT-4 sessions replaced regular homework for the treatment group.
- **Results:** Students were more satisfied, learning was not harmed, and one group showed significantly better learning gains.
- **Things to watch:** It's small, and the AI was built as a tutor rather than an open chatbot. I saw the Italy setting only in a secondhand summary, not in the paper. Conference papers are the main peer-reviewed venue in computer science, but some teachers count only journal articles, so ask yours.

**3. Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*)
Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2024). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. Free preprint version: https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09315
- **What it is:** An experiment with 117 university students on a writing task.
- **Results:** The ChatGPT group improved their essays more, but their knowledge gain and transfer were no different from the other groups. The authors warn about dependence on AI, which they call "metacognitive laziness."
- **Things to watch:** The participants were university students, not high schoolers. The journal page blocked me, but I confirmed the journal through Crossref. The link goes to the authors' preprint, so cite the journal version.

### Preprint

**6. Preprint, not yet peer reviewed** (arXiv)
Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
- **What it is:** Two lab experiments plus a field study.
- **Results:** Overall, the AI had no effect on learning. Students who substituted AI for their own work, such as generating solutions, covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used it to complement their work, such as asking for explanations, understood more. AI also widened the gap between students with low and high prior knowledge.
- **Why it's here:** It's the closest match I found to your "how they use it" sub-question.
- **Things to watch:** It hasn't been peer reviewed. The abstract doesn't say who the students were, and a secondhand description says an earlier version studied a university coding course.

### Research organization reports

**7. Research organization report** (Pew Research Center)
Sidoti, O., Park, E., & Gottfried, J. (2025, January 15). *About a quarter of U.S. teens have used ChatGPT for schoolwork – double the share in 2023*. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork
- **Why it's here:** It's a survey of how many teens use ChatGPT, not a study of whether it helps them learn.
- **Findings:** In a fall 2024 survey of 1,391 teens, 26% said they had used ChatGPT for schoolwork, up from 13% in 2023. Teens judged some uses acceptable more than others: 54% said using it to research new topics is fine, but only 18% said the same for writing essays.

**8. Research organization report** (Pew Research Center)

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plenty:** Surveys of how many teens use chatbots, and general debate about AI and learning.
- **Thin:** Experiments on high schoolers specifically. I found only two, sources 1 and 2. Neither studied ordinary homework done at home with a regular chatbot.
- **Missing:** I found no study that directly compares high schoolers who check their work with chatbots against those who get answers. The closest are source 1's chat-log analysis and source 6's substitute/complement split.
- **No long-term studies:** Everything covers weeks or a semester at most.
- **A warning:** A widely cited meta-analysis (Wang & Fan, 2025, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*) now has a Crossref record titled "RETRACTED ARTICLE." If you see it cited in news coverage, don't use it.

## Questions the results raise

- **Is "checking work" really "complementing"?** Your idea of "checking work" may not map neatly onto source 6's "complementing," which is about asking for explanations.
- **Design or use?** Source 1 suggests the tool's design changed how students used it. That's a different claim from saying students choose how to use it.
- **Self-perception:** Students in source 1 didn't notice their learning had suffered. That matters for any evidence based on students describing their own use.
