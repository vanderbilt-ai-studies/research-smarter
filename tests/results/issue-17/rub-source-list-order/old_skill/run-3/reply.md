## Sources

**Peer-reviewed research.** These three meet your requirement of at least three peer-reviewed sources. I confirmed each journal through the Crossref and OpenAlex databases. The publishers' pages blocked my automated access, so I haven't given links. Find them through your library, and add each DOI to your APA reference once you've opened the article yourself.

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*). Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122.
   A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey. Some had a standard ChatGPT-style tool, some had a "tutor" version with prompts designed to protect learning, and some had no AI. Both tools raised grades on practice problems while students had access. Once access was removed, students who had used the standard tool scored 17% lower than students who never had AI. The tutor version largely avoided that drop.
2. **Peer-reviewed article, systematic review and meta-analysis** (*Computers & Education*). Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, Article 105224.
   This review pools 69 experiments from 2022–2024, mostly at the university level. It finds ChatGPT improved academic performance and reduced mental effort. The authors warn that methodological weaknesses call for caution, including weak post-test design and no long-term follow-up.
3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Societies*). Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), Article 6.
   A survey-and-interview study of 666 people of all ages, not just students. Heavier AI use went with lower critical-thinking scores, partly through "cognitive offloading," meaning handing mental work to the tool. Younger participants relied on AI more. The design is correlational, so it shows a link, not a cause.

**Working papers and preprints, not yet peer reviewed.** I've included these because several of them look at *how* students use AI, which your second sub-question asks about. Watch for changes when or if they're published.

4. **Working paper, not peer reviewed.** Strömberg, D., Lei, V., & Wu, Y. (2026). *The generative AI learning penalty: Evidence from Chinese secondary education* (CEPR Discussion Paper No. 21577). Centre for Economic Policy Research. https://ideas.repec.org/p/cpr/ceprdp/21577.html
   This paper follows 26,811 students in grades 7–12 in China over 30 months. Once students adopted AI, homework scores rose 18% and monthly exam scores fell 20% within six months. Entrance-exam scores also fell. The losses were concentrated among about 80% of users whose behavior suggested they were outsourcing their homework. Users who kept spending normal time on homework showed small losses. *Why it's here:* it's the largest and longest study of real secondary students I found. *What to watch:* the data come from one Chinese county, and "outsourcing" was inferred from how long homework took, not observed directly.
5. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   Two pre-registered lab experiments found no average effect on learning. The breakdown by use was different. Students who used the AI to replace learning steps, such as generating solutions, covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used it to add to their learning, such as asking for explanations, understood more. *What to watch:* the abstract doesn't say these were high school students.
6. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Dai, X., Wen, Z., Jiang, J., Liu, H., & Zhang, Y. (2025). *How students use AI feedback matters: Experimental evidence on physics achievement and autonomy* (arXiv:2505.08672) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.08672
   Two five-week randomized trials with 387 high school physics students. The results differed by achievement level and by whether students chose when to ask the AI for help. *Why it's here:* it's one of the few high school experiments. *What to watch:* the samples are small and the setting is narrow.
7. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Kosmyna, N., Hauptmann, E., Yuan, Y. T., Situ, J., Liao, X.-H., Beresnitzky, A. V., Braunstein, I., & Maes, P. (2025). *Your brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of cognitive debt when using an AI assistant for essay writing task* (arXiv:2506.08872) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872
   This MIT study had 54 participants write essays using ChatGPT, a search engine, or no tool, while measuring brain activity. The ChatGPT group showed the weakest brain engagement and had trouble quoting their own essays. *What to watch:* the sample is small, the participants weren't high schoolers, and the task was essay writing rather than homework. Media coverage often overstates it.

**Research organization reports (surveys).** These two are surveys, not studies of learning. They're useful for how many teens use chatbots and how, not for whether chatbots help them learn.

8. **Research organization report.** Sidoti, O., Park, E., & Gottfried, J. (2025, January 15). *About a quarter of U.S. teens have used ChatGPT for schoolwork – double the share in 2023*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork-double-the-share-in-2023/
   In a fall 2024 survey of 1,391 U.S. teens, 26% said they used ChatGPT for schoolwork. Teens' views on what's acceptable depended on the task: 54% said researching new topics was fine, compared with 18% for writing essays.
9. **Research organization report.** McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
   In a fall 2025 survey of 1,458 U.S. teens, 54% said they use chatbots for schoolwork, and 1 in 10 said they do all or most of their schoolwork with chatbot help. 59% said students at their school use AI to cheat at least somewhat often.

## What I found and didn't find

- **A retracted study.** A widely cited 2025 meta-analysis by Wang and Fan (*Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*) reported large learning gains from ChatGPT. According to Crossref, it was **retracted on April 22, 2026**. News sites and even one of the papers above still cite it, so I left it out. If you see it quoted elsewhere, you'll know why.
- **High school evidence is thin.** Bastani et al. is the only peer-reviewed experiment with high schoolers I found. Most experiments involve university students.
- **Few studies observe how students actually use the tool.** Most assign a tool or infer behavior from indirect signs, such as time spent on homework.
- **No long-term studies of U.S. high schoolers.** The U.S. data I found are surveys, not learning outcomes.

## Questions the results raise

- Several studies split their results by tool design (guarded vs. unguarded) or by time spent on homework rather than by "checking vs. getting answers." Whether those splits measure the same thing as your distinction is something you'll need to decide.
- Most of the evidence comes from math, physics, and essay writing. How far it carries to other subjects is still open.
