1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122.
   This was a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students. During practice, students with a standard ChatGPT-style tutor scored 48% higher and students with a tutor built to protect learning scored 127% higher. Once access was taken away, the standard-tutor group scored 17% lower than students who never had access, and the safeguarded tutor largely prevented that drop. *Crossref lists a correction to this paper from August 2025. Read it before you cite specific numbers.*

2. **Peer-reviewed article.** Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530.
   In this randomized experiment, 117 university students did a writing task with ChatGPT, a human expert, writing analytics tools, or no extra support. The ChatGPT group improved their essay scores the most, but their knowledge gain and transfer were no different from the other groups. The authors warn that this kind of help may encourage "metacognitive laziness."

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, 108386.
   91 university students were randomly assigned to research a science issue with ChatGPT or with Google. The ChatGPT group felt less mental effort but produced weaker reasoning and justifications.

4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Darvishi, A., Khosravi, H., Sadiq, S., Gašević, D., & Siemens, G. (2024). Impact of AI assistance on student agency. *Computers & Education, 210*, 104967.
   This randomized experiment involved 1,625 students in 10 university courses, and the AI gave them prompts while they wrote peer feedback. When the AI was removed, students tended to have relied on it rather than learned from it. Self-monitoring checklists partly filled the gap.

5. **Peer-reviewed article.** Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224.
   This review pooled 69 experimental studies from 2022 to 2024, and most of them were at the university level. Overall, ChatGPT use improved academic performance and reduced mental effort. The authors caution that many studies were small and tested students in ways that may measure the quality of ChatGPT's output rather than what the students learned.

6. **Peer-reviewed article.** Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), 6.
   This survey-and-interview study of 666 people found that heavier AI tool use went with lower critical-thinking scores, linked through "cognitive offloading," and younger participants relied on AI more. *It's a correlation study, so it can't show that AI caused the lower scores. Crossref also lists a correction to it.*

**Working paper, not yet peer reviewed**

7. **Working paper (World Bank), not yet peer reviewed.** De Simone, M., Tiberti, F. H., Barron Rodriguez, M. R., Manolio, F. A., Mosuro, W., & Dikoru, E. J. (2025). *From chalkboards to chatbots: Evaluating the impact of generative AI on learning outcomes in Nigeria* (Policy Research Working Paper No. 11125). World Bank. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:11125
   In a randomized trial, first-year senior secondary students in Nigeria used Microsoft Copilot for six weeks in a supervised after-school English program. English scores rose by 0.23 standard deviations, and the largest gains went to girls and students who started with higher scores. *Why it's here: it's a rigorous trial with high-school-age students. Watch for two things: it hasn't been peer reviewed, and the chatbot was used under teacher supervision, not for homework at home.*

**Preprints, not yet peer reviewed**

8. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   Two lab experiments found no overall effect of chatbots on learning. Students who used them to replace learning steps, such as generating solutions, covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used them to complement learning, such as asking for explanations, understood more deeply. *Why it's here: it compares types of use directly. Watch for two things: it isn't peer reviewed, and it studies people learning to code (from what I read, adults in university settings, not high schoolers).*

9. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Kosmyna, N., Hauptmann, E., Yuan, Y. T., Situ, J., Liao, X.-H., Beresnitzky, A. V., Braunstein, I., & Maes, P. (2025). *Your brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of cognitive debt when using an AI assistant for essay writing task* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872
   54 adults wrote essays with ChatGPT, with a search engine, or with no tools while EEG measured their brain activity. The ChatGPT group showed the weakest brain connectivity and had trouble recalling their own essays. *Why it's here: it's widely discussed and looks at how engaged writers were. Watch for three things: it isn't peer reviewed, the sample is small, and the task was short. I didn't confirm the participants' ages.*

### What I found and didn't find

- **Very few studies are about high schoolers.** Only Bastani and the World Bank paper studied high-school-age students. Most of the experimental evidence comes from university students or adults.
- **Almost nothing is about homework done at home.** Every study I found had students using the AI in a lab, in a class, or in a supervised program.
- **Nothing measured "checking work" as its own behavior.** The closest comparisons sort use in other ways: Lehmann's "substitute vs. complement" and Bastani's two tutor designs. In Bastani's study, the tool set the limits, not the student's choice.
- **Almost no long-term studies.** Most studies lasted from one session to a few weeks.
- **A retracted paper to avoid.** A widely cited 2025 meta-analysis by Wang & Fan in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* reported large learning gains from ChatGPT. Crossref shows the journal retracted it on April 22, 2026. You'll probably see it cited in other papers.
- **A study I couldn't verify.** News coverage describes a study of 26,000 Chinese secondary students linking AI homework use to lower exam scores. I couldn't find the original paper, so I left it out.

### Issues the results raise for your position

- Your position is about what students *choose* to do. Bastani's strongest contrast is about how the *tool* was designed. You'll have to decide whether those are the same argument.
- Lehmann found that the gap between students with low and high prior knowledge got wider. That raises the possibility that the student's starting point matters as much as how they use the chatbot.
