**Peer-reviewed research**

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*). Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students, using two GPT-4 tutors: a standard ChatGPT-style one and one built to protect learning. While students had access, grades rose 48% with the standard tutor and 127% with the protected one. Once access was removed, the standard-tutor group scored 17% lower than students who never had AI. The authors say the protected tutor largely prevented that drop. News coverage says the study took place at one school in Turkey; the abstract doesn't say, so I haven't confirmed that.

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers & Education*). Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   A review that combines the results of 69 experimental studies from 2022–2024, mostly at the university level. It reports that ChatGPT improved academic performance and lowered mental effort. The authors caution that many of the studies had small samples and measured outcomes in ways that might reflect the quality of ChatGPT's output rather than what students learned.

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Computers in Human Behavior*). Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, 108386. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386
   91 university students were randomly assigned to research a science issue with either ChatGPT or Google. The ChatGPT group reported less mental effort but produced weaker reasoning in their final recommendations.

4. **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*). Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   A randomized experiment with 117 university students writing essays with ChatGPT, a human expert, writing-analytics tools, or no extra help. The ChatGPT group improved their essay scores the most, but gained no more knowledge or transfer than the other groups. The authors describe signs of dependence on the tool, which they call "metacognitive laziness."

5. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Societies*). Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), 6. https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006
   Surveys and interviews with 666 people of different ages, not specifically students. Heavier AI use was linked to lower critical-thinking scores, through more "cognitive offloading" (handing mental work to the tool). This is a correlation and can't show cause. The abstract also reports that younger participants relied on AI more.

**Other credible sources**

6. **Conference paper** (Association for Computational Linguistics 2025 proceedings). Vanzo, A., Pal Chowdhury, S., & Sachan, M. (2025). GPT-4 as a homework tutor can improve student engagement and learning outcomes. In *Proceedings of the 63rd Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (Volume 1: Long Papers)* (pp. 31119–31136). Association for Computational Linguistics. https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.acl-long.1502
   A randomized trial in four high school English-as-a-second-language classes, where GPT-4 tutoring sessions replaced regular homework. The authors report that learning didn't suffer and one group gained significantly more. *Why it's here:* it's one of the few experiments with high schoolers on homework. *What to watch for:* I confirmed where it was published but not whether it was peer reviewed, so don't count it toward your three. The abstract gives no sample size.

7. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   Two pre-registered lab experiments plus a field study. Overall, chatbots had no effect on learning. Students who used them to *replace* learning activities, such as generating solutions, covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used them to *add to* their learning, such as asking for explanations, understood more. *What to watch for:* it hasn't been peer reviewed, and the abstract doesn't give the participants' ages or sample sizes.

8. **International agency report.** OECD. (2026). *OECD digital education outlook 2026: Exploring effective uses of generative AI in education*. OECD Publishing. https://doi.org/10.1787/062a7394-en
   A broad review of research on generative AI in education. *Why it's here:* it's a major policy overview. *What to watch for:* the OECD's site blocked me, so I read only its catalog summary. News coverage says it separates better performance on tasks from real learning gains, but I couldn't check that in the report itself.

9. **Research organization report** (survey). Sidoti, O., Park, E., & Gottfried, J. (2025, January 15). *About a quarter of U.S. teens have used ChatGPT for schoolwork – double the share in 2023*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork-double-the-share-in-2023/
   A fall 2024 survey of 1,391 U.S. teens ages 13–17: 26% said they used ChatGPT for schoolwork, up from 13% in 2023, and 31% of 11th and 12th graders said so. *This is a survey, not a study of learning:* it shows how common the behavior is, not whether it helps.

10. **Research organization report** (survey). McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
   A fall 2025 survey of 1,458 teens and their parents: 54% of teens have used chatbots for schoolwork help, and 10% say they do all or most of their schoolwork with chatbots. *Also a usage survey:* it doesn't separate using chatbots to get answers from using them to get explanations.

## What I found and didn't find

- **There's a lot of evidence on short-term performance, and much less on learning after the AI is taken away.** Only a few studies, like Bastani and Fan, test students afterward.
- **Most studies use university students.** Only Bastani and Vanzo studied high schoolers directly.
- **The use pattern your position describes is barely studied directly.** Lehmann's split between replacing and adding to learning is the closest match, and it's a preprint. I found no study that sorts high schoolers by "checks work" vs. "gets answers" and compares their results.
- **There are almost no long-term studies.**
- **I dropped one source.** A meta-analysis by Wang & Fan (2025, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*) shows up often in searches, but Crossref lists it as **retracted**. If you see it cited elsewhere, treat that as a warning sign about that source.

## Questions these results raise

- In Bastani, the outcome depended on how the *tool* was built (guardrails or none), not only on how students chose to use it. That's a different variable from the one your position is about.
- Several studies found that scores went up while learning stayed flat or fell. What counts as "helping learning" in your paper is something you'll need to define.
