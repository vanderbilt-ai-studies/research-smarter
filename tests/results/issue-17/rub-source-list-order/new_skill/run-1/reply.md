## Sources

**Peer-reviewed research.** These three meet your "at least 3 peer-reviewed" requirement. I confirmed each journal through Crossref or the journal's own page.

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*)
   Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), Article e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   - A field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students. Students using a standard ChatGPT-style tool ("GPT Base") did 48% better on practice problems. But once the tool was taken away, they scored 17% worse on the exam than students who never had it.
   - A second version built to protect learning ("GPT Tutor") largely avoided that drop.
   - I could only read the abstract because the publisher's site blocked me. Crossref also shows a correction to this paper (August 2025) that I didn't read.

2. **Peer-reviewed systematic review and meta-analysis** (*Computers & Education*)
   Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, Article 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   - Combines 69 experimental studies from 2022–2024. Overall, ChatGPT improved academic performance and reduced mental effort, with no effect on self-efficacy.
   - Most of the studies were at the university level. The authors caution that many studies were too small and that the tests given afterward may have measured the quality of ChatGPT's output rather than what students learned.
   - I read only the abstract, via the OpenAlex database. The publisher page blocked me.

3. **Peer-reviewed meta-analysis** (*International Review of Research in Open and Distributed Learning*)
   Doo, M. Y., & Park, Y. (2026). A meta-analysis of ChatGPT's influence on learning achievement. *International Review of Research in Open and Distributed Learning, 27*(1), 265–289. https://doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v27i1.8775
   - Combines 22 studies and finds a moderate positive effect on achievement.
   - The effect was larger for middle and high school students than for undergraduates, but the difference wasn't statistically significant. About 71% of participants were undergraduates.

**Working papers and preprints (not yet peer reviewed)**

4. **Working paper, not yet peer reviewed** (CEPR Discussion Paper series)
   Strömberg, D., Lei, V., & Wu, Y. (2026). *The generative AI learning penalty: Evidence from Chinese secondary education* (CEPR Discussion Paper No. 21577). Centre for Economic Policy Research. [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cpr/ceprdp/21577.html)
   - Follows 26,811 students in grades 7–12 over 30 months. Using AI raised homework scores by 18% but lowered monthly exam scores by 20%.
   - The losses were concentrated among students who finished homework unusually fast with unusually high scores. Students whose homework time stayed similar to non-users' had small losses.
   - *Why it's here:* it's the largest secondary-school study I found and speaks directly to your second sub-question. But it hasn't been through peer review, it covers one county in China, and it infers how students used AI from their timing rather than observing it. I read the abstract only.

5. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed** (arXiv)
   Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2024). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   - Two lab experiments found no overall effect on learning.
   - In further analyses and a field study, students who used the tool to *replace* their work (for example, generating solutions) covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used it to *add to* their work (for example, asking for explanations) understood more.
   - *Why it's here:* it's the closest match I found to how-students-use-it, but it isn't peer reviewed. The abstract doesn't say how old the students were or what subject they studied, so it may not be about high schoolers. SSRN lists a version under a slightly different title.    - A survey of 1,458 U.S. teens (fall 2025): 54% had used chatbots for schoolwork help, and 10% do all or most of their schoolwork with them.

   - *Why it's here:* it's the same kind of usage survey, and it's newer.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plenty of evidence:** studies comparing scores with and without chatbots, including several meta-analyses, though most of the studies they combine involve college students.
- **Thin evidence:** high school–specific experiments (Bastani is the only peer-reviewed one I found) and long-term effects.
- **Couldn't find:** any study that measures "checking your work" as its own behavior. The closest things are the CEPR paper's homework-timing measure and Lehmann's "replace vs. add to" split. Both are related to your idea, but neither is the same thing.
- **Dropped:** Wang & Fan (2025), a widely shared meta-analysis in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*, is now marked "RETRACTED ARTICLE." Watch for it, because other articles still cite it.

## Questions these results raise

- Whether "checking your work" is the same behavior as "asking for explanations," or something different again.
- Bastani's two tutors suggest the design of the tool matters, not just the student's choices. How that fits with a position built around what students do.
- Most of the "help" evidence comes from college students. How much it applies to high schoolers.
