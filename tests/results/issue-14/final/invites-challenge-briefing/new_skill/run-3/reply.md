## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Why districts switch.** Superintendents and school board members name cost savings as the main reason. The other common reasons are recruiting teachers and improving attendance. RAND's 2021 study of Idaho, New Mexico and Oklahoma found only weak support for all three. ([RAND, *Does Four Equal Five?*](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA373-1.html))
- **How much money is saved.** Average savings run about 0.4% to 2.5% of a district's budget, with a theoretical maximum of about 5.4%. The amount depends on whether buildings stay open on the fifth day. ([NCSL](https://www.ncsl.org/education/four-day-school-week-overview))
- **Learning.** Paul Thompson's Oregon study found lower math and reading scores, with the gap growing the longer schools stayed on four days. ([Education Next, 2021](https://www.educationnext.org/shrinking-school-week-effects-four-day-schedule-student-achievement/)) RAND found slower growth too, but principals, teachers and parents mostly believed learning was the same or better.
- **Teachers.** An Oregon study found teacher turnover rose about 2 percentage points right after a switch, and about 4 points over the long run. Other studies found the schedule helps with hiring. ([Education Week, Oct. 2024](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/4-day-school-weeks-and-their-surprising-effect-on-teacher-turnover/2024/10))

**Major voices**
- RAND (Kilburn, Phillips, Morton and colleagues)
- Paul Thompson, an economist at Oregon State
- The National Conference of State Legislatures (NCSL), which tracks state policy
- District superintendents, who appear often in news coverage

**Patterns**
- Most four-day districts are rural.
- Adoption has grown fast, to roughly 850 districts as of NCSL's 2023 update. Missouri had a big wave of switches.
- Most research compares test scores. Much less of it looks at families.
- The schedule stays popular with families and staff even where the measured results are mixed.

**Open questions**
- **Costs that shift to families.** Childcare, meals and parents' work time move to households on the day off. One 2019 estimate found an 11% drop in employment for married mothers. Only about 20% of four-day schools serve meals on the fifth day. ([K-12 Dive, 2022](https://k12dive.com/news/4-ways-transitioning-to-a-4-day-school-week-impacts-districts/628601))
- **Youth crime.** A 2018 Colorado study linked four-day weeks to more juvenile property crime. I've only seen it described secondhand, in that K-12 Dive article, so the study itself still needs checking.
- **How students use the extra day.** Students gain about 3.5 to 4 hours of free time a week, but little is known about what they do with it.

One thing to weigh: the K-12 Dive article and the Colorado study are journalism and secondhand reporting, not peer-reviewed research. Your peer-reviewed source will need to come from a journal.
