**Peer-reviewed studies**

1. King, D. L., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., & Wade, T. (2024). "Phones off while school's on": Evaluating problematic phone use and the social, wellbeing, and academic effects of banning phones in schools. *Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 13*(4), 913–922. https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.2024.00058
   - I read the abstract at an [open repository copy](https://real.mtak.hu/216606) and a full-text summary on PubMed Central. The publisher's page blocked me.
   - This was a preregistered natural experiment in five South Australian schools (Grades 7–12, about 1,280 students), with surveys taken before the ban and about one month after.
   - Ban and no-ban schools didn't differ on school belonging. Bullying fell in both groups, and the ban group reported slightly more bullying throughout. The study didn't directly measure face-to-face socializing.

2. Bar, E., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., Wade, T. D., & King, D. L. (2025). Student perspectives on banning mobile phones in South Australian secondary schools: A large-scale qualitative analysis. *Computers in Human Behavior, 167*, 108603.
   - The journal page wouldn't load, so I read about it on [Flinders University's news page](https://news.flinders.edu.au/blog/2025/09/18/benefits-of-school-phone-bans/). The volume and article number came from search results, so check them.
   - It analyzed about 7,000 open-ended survey answers from more than 1,500 students.
   - Students reported more face-to-face conversation, better-quality conversation, less cyberbullying and conflict, and friendlier schools. Some students asked for education on responsible phone use instead of outright bans.

3. Goodyear, V., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe*, 101211.
   - I read it on the [University of Birmingham research page](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/school-phone-policies-and-their-association-with-mental-wellbeing/).
   - It compared 30 English schools with 1,227 students aged 12–15.
   - Restrictive schools had less phone and social media use during school hours, but the total for the day was the same. A commentary in the same journal says students made up the time outside school. The study didn't measure in-person interaction or bullying.

4. Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569.
   - I read it on the [Utrecht University portal](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/disconnect-to-reconnect-how-variations-between-types-of-smartphon/).
   - It compared full bans (the whole school grounds) with partial bans (classrooms only), using 1,398 students with an average age of 16.2.
   - Bullying and wellbeing didn't differ between the two kinds of ban. Full bans were linked to weaker student–teacher connection, and for girls, a weaker sense of belonging at school.

5. Beneito, P., & Vicente-Chirivella, O. (2022). Banning mobile phones in schools: Evidence from regional-level policies in Spain. *Applied Economic Analysis, 30*(90), 153–175.
   - I read it on the [Emerald article page](https://www.emerald.com/aea/article/30/90/153-175/59588).
   - It compared two Spanish regions that banned phones in 2015 with the rest of Spain.
   - Police-reported bullying fell about 9–18% among 12- to 17-year-olds after the bans. The authors call this "suggestive evidence."

## What I found and didn't find

- **Face-to-face interaction:** The direct evidence is mostly what students and principals say happened. I found no study that observed or counted conversations before and after a ban.
- **Bullying during school:** There's a fair amount of evidence, and it's mixed. Some studies show a drop and some show no difference.
- **Conflict moving to after school:** This is the biggest gap. The only direct evidence is students in the DCU focus groups saying it happens. I found no study that measured when cyberbullying happens before and after a ban.
- **Age fit:** Several samples include middle schoolers (Grades 7–12, ages 12–15). The Dutch study (average age 16.2) matches high school most closely.
- **Time span:** Almost nothing tracks students for more than a year after a ban.

## What the results raise for your position

- Several studies measure "belonging" or "connectedness" rather than talk. More talking and feeling more connected aren't the same thing.
- The 99% overlap figure suggests online and in-person bullying are often the same conflict. That affects what it would even mean for conflict to "move."
- The UK finding that students make up their phone time after school is about screen time, not conflict. It's worth deciding whether it's relevant to your claim.
