## Sources

**Peer-reviewed studies (quantitative)**

1. Beneito, P., & Vicente-Chirivella, Ó. (2022). Banning mobile phones in schools: Evidence from regional-level policies in Spain. *Applied Economic Analysis, 30*(90), 153–175. https://doi.org/10.1108/AEA-05-2021-0112
   Compares two Spanish regions that banned phones in 2014–15 with the rest of Spain, using a method that builds a statistical stand-in for each region from regions without bans. It reports bullying falling by roughly 10–18% among teens aged 12–17. The authors call this suggestive, because the data cover whole regions rather than individual students and the post-ban period is short.

2. Abrahamsson, S. (2026). Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health. *Journal of Human Resources.* Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.0224-13403R2
   Norwegian middle schools. It combines national records with survey data on each school's phone policy and tracks outcomes before and after each school's ban. Bullying fell for both boys and girls. Girls' mental-health care use dropped and their grades rose. On average there was no effect on education or mental health. I read only the abstract because the full text is paywalled.

3. Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
   Surveys 1,398 Dutch students (average age 16) at 24 schools, comparing classroom-only bans with whole-day, whole-grounds bans. It found no difference in bullying or well-being. Full bans went with weaker student–teacher connection and, for girls, a lower sense of belonging at school. Abstract only.

4. King, D. L., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., & Wade, T. (2024). "Phones off while school's on": Evaluating problematic phone use and the social, wellbeing, and academic effects of banning phones in schools. *Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 13*(4), 913–922. https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.2024.00058
   About 1,280 South Australian students in grades 7–12 at schools with and without a ban, surveyed at the start and one month later. Bullying fell in both groups, and the ban group reported slightly more of it. Sense of belonging at school did not differ. The study didn't measure face-to-face talk directly.

5. Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211
   1,227 students aged 12–15 at 30 English schools, surveyed once rather than tracked over time. Students at restrictive schools used phones less during school, but their total use on weekdays and weekends was about the same. Well-being didn't differ. A commentary in the same journal notes that the study did not measure bullying or in-person socializing. I confirmed the details through the Europe PMC record because the publisher page blocked me.

**Peer-reviewed studies (qualitative and review)**

6. Bar, ., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., Wade, T., & King, D. L. (2025). Student perspectives on banning mobile phones in South Australian secondary schools: A large-scale qualitative analysis. *Computers in Human Behavior, 167*, 108603.
   No link: the publisher page blocked me, and the university's PDF wouldn't convert to text. Search results and university news coverage describe about 7,000 open-ended survey answers from more than 1,500 students. Students reported more interaction among friends and calmer schools, and also saw benefits of having phones. Check the first author's full name and the findings in the paper itself.

7. Böttger, T., & Zierer, K. (2024). To ban or not to ban? A rapid review on the impact of smartphone bans in schools on social well-being and academic performance. *Education Sciences, 14*(8), 906.
   No link: the DOI redirected to the journal page, but the page refused access. Search results describe a meta-analysis that found a small overall benefit, larger for social well-being including bullying. I haven't read the paper, so check it before relying on it.

**Not peer-reviewed**

8. Allcott, H., Baron, E. J., Dee, T., Duckworth, A. L., Gentzkow, M., & Jacob, B. (2026). *The effects of school phone bans: National evidence from lockable pouches* (NBER Working Paper No. 35132). National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w35132
   Compares more than 43,000 US middle and high schools before and after they adopted lockable pouches. Phone use dropped. Discipline incidents rose in the first year and then faded. Well-being dipped and later rose. There was no clear change in perceived online bullying. One of the authors suggested in a Stanford news piece that more in-person interaction could explain the well-being gains, but the study didn't measure it.

9. Reynolds, M., Esfandiari, M., & O'Higgins Norman, J. (2025). *Restriction or resilience? Smartphone bans in schools: A qualitative study on the experiences of students and teachers.* DCU Anti-Bullying Centre. https://www.dcu.ie/antibullyingcentre/restriction-or-resilience
   Focus groups with 66 Irish students aged 10–18. Students said bans don't stop cyberbullying, which they said happens mostly outside school hours. Students also said stricter bans make them look for ways around the rules.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Bullying overall:** there's a fair amount of research, and the results don't agree with each other. Most studies measure bullying in general, not cyberbullying separately.
- **Face-to-face interaction:** I found almost no study that measures it directly. The evidence is mostly students describing their own experience (Bar et al.) or researchers guessing at a cause.
- **Conflict moving to after school:** I found no study that tracks bullying by time of day before and after a ban. The closest evidence is students' own views in the DCU report.
- **Age:** several key studies are about middle schoolers (Norway, the UK sample, part of Spain). Studies limited to high school students are rare.
- **Long-term studies:** almost none. Most follow students for a year or less, except Allcott et al. at three years.
