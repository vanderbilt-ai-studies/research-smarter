## Sources

**Peer-reviewed studies that compare schools with bans to schools without them, or before and after a ban**

1. King, D. L., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., & Wade, T. (2024). "Phones off while school's on": Evaluating problematic phone use and the social, wellbeing, and academic effects of banning phones in schools. *Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 13*(4), 913–922. https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.2024.00058
   About 1,280 South Australian students in grades 7–12 at five public schools were surveyed before a ban and one month after. Students at ban and no-ban schools didn't differ on school belonging or academic engagement. Bullying was slightly higher in the ban group but fell significantly in both groups. *(I read the abstract, not the full text.)*

2. Goodyear, V., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe*, Article 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211
   This compared 1,227 students aged 12–15 at 30 English schools, 20 with restrictive policies and 10 with permissive ones. Restrictive schools had about 30–40 minutes less phone and social media use during school hours. Wellbeing and total weekday or weekend use didn't differ. Press coverage says the study didn't measure bullying or face-to-face interaction. *(I couldn't confirm the volume number.)*

3. Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026).    This compared 1,398 Dutch students at 24 schools with classroom-only bans or whole-school bans. Whole-school bans weren't linked to better wellbeing or less bullying. They were linked to weaker student–teacher connectedness and, for girls, a lower sense of school belonging. *(I worked from the abstract in the Crossref record because the publisher page was blocked.)*

**Peer-reviewed economics studies (larger samples, official records)**

5. Beneito, P., & Vicente-Chirivella, Ó. (2022). Banning mobile phones in schools: Evidence from regional-level policies in Spain. *Applied Economic Analysis, 30*(90), 153–175. https://doi.org/10.1108/AEA-05-2021-0112
   This uses police-reported bullying cases from 2012–2017 to compare two Spanish regions that banned phones with regions that didn't. Bullying fell by roughly 9.5–18% among 12–17-year-olds after the bans, with no effect for under-12s. The data doesn't separate cyberbullying from other bullying and only counts cases families reported to police. *(Open access; I read most of the full text.)*

6. Abrahamsson, S. (2026). Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health. *Journal of Human Resources*. Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.0224-13403R2
   This uses Norwegian middle schools that introduced bans at different times. According to summaries of the 2024 working-paper version, bullying fell for both boys and girls, and girls made fewer mental-health visits and earned better grades. *(I only confirmed the journal record. 9. Reynolds, M., Esfandiari, M., & O'Higgins Norman, J. (2025). *Restriction or resilience? Smartphone bans in schools: A qualitative study on the experiences of students and teachers*. DCU Anti-Bullying Centre. https://www.dcu.ie/antibullyingcentre/restriction-or-resilience

    In a survey of 1,510 kids aged 11–13, those at schools with phone limits reported more cyberbullying. The authors say this doesn't show that bans cause it, because schools with bad cyberbullying may be the ones that adopted bans.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Face-to-face talk:** Almost all of this evidence is self-reported, from students' and teachers' perceptions. I found no peer-reviewed study that directly observed or counted conversations at lunch or between classes. The qualitative studies disagree with each other: the Australian students (#7) described more talk, while the Irish students (#9) didn't.
- **Conflict moving to after school:** I found no study that measured *when* cyberbullying happens before and after a ban. The idea shows up in students' own words (#9) and in a correlational survey (#11), not in a measured result.
- **High school specifically:** Many of the strongest studies cover middle school or ages 11–15 (#2, #6, #11). The ones that include high schoolers are #1, #3, #5 and #10. That gap matters because your question is about high school students.
- **Timing:** Most studies are short-term. #1 followed students for only one month.

## Open issues these results raise

- The studies that found less bullying (#5, #6) counted bullying overall. Whether that drop includes after-school online conflict, or only the in-school kind, is something your argument will have to deal with.
- #3 suggests a *stricter* ban might weaken some kinds of connection. So the type of ban, not just whether there is one, may matter to your claim.
