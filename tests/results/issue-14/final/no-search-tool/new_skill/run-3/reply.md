### Preliminary briefing (unverified, from my training)

**Key debates**
- **Does learning suffer?** This is the biggest fight. Some studies find small drops in test scores, some find almost no effect, and results vary by state and by how carefully the study was designed.
- **Is it worth the money?** Saving money was the original selling point, but analyses tend to find the savings smaller than districts expected.
- **Who gains and who pays?** Families often say they're happy with it. But the day off can be hard for working parents and for kids who rely on school meals.

**Major voices**
- University economists and education researchers who study test-score data
- Research organizations and policy groups that track how widely it's used
- State education departments, which set the rules
- Teachers' groups and local school boards, where the decisions actually get made

**Patterns**
- Most four-day districts are small and rural, many of them in Western and Midwestern states.
- Many districts now say the main reason they switch is to attract and keep teachers, not to save money.
- Most studies compare districts before and after switching, so the findings depend on how well the comparison districts are matched.

**Open questions**
- Long-term outcomes, like graduation and what students do after high school
- What students actually do on the fifth day
- Whether the teacher-recruiting advantage disappears once nearby districts switch too
- Whether findings from rural districts would hold for larger ones

The claim I'm least sure of, and the one that matters most for where you go next, is that districts now mostly switch to recruit teachers rather than to save money. Push back on that one, or any other, and ask me how I know.
