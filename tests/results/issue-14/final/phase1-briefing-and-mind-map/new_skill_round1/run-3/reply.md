## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Does it hurt learning?** This is the central fight. Several multi-state studies find small drops in test-score growth: Morton, Thompson & Kuhfeld (2024) covered six states, and RAND (2023) found similar results. Other studies found no difference (Missouri's education department, 2024) or even gains (Anderson & Walker, 2015, Colorado).
- **Does it help keep teachers?** Districts often switch to attract staff. A Texas study found turnover fell about 2.7 percentage points. An Oregon working paper found turnover *rose* about 4 points over the long term.
- **Is it safer?** In Oklahoma, bullying fell about 39% and fights about 31% (Morton, 2022). A Colorado study found juvenile offenses rose about 20% in areas with four-day schools (Fischer & Argyle, 2018).

**Major voices**
- Economists of education, especially Paul Thompson (Oregon State) and Emily Morton, who did much of the large-scale achievement work.
- RAND Corporation, which ran a multi-state study of how districts put the schedule in place.
- CALDER, a research center that publishes working papers on teacher effects.
- Education Week and the Education Commission of the States, which track adoption and costs.

**Patterns**
- **Instructional time seems to matter.** Thompson & Ward (2022) found test-score drops only in schools with low weekly hours. Schools that lengthened their remaining days showed no change.
- **The evidence comes from a few states.** Most studies cover rural districts in Oregon, Colorado, Oklahoma, and Missouri.
- **Small savings.** Reported savings run about 0.4–2.5% of a district's budget, often offset by new programs for the off day.
- **Parents mostly like it.** Satisfaction is high, but some of the health findings rest on students' own reports.

**Open questions**
- Do findings from small rural districts carry over to suburban and city districts? Adoption there has grown since the pandemic, and evidence is thin.
- What are the long-term effects? Several findings flip between short-term and long-term.
- Which families have the hardest time with the fifth day, for example working parents or families of students with disabilities?
- Why do districts keep adopting the schedule if savings and retention gains are small?
