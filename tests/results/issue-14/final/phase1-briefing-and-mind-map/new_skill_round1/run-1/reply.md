## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Achievement.** Does the schedule hurt learning? A 2021 RAND study of districts in Idaho, New Mexico and Oklahoma found math and English scores grew more slowly in four-day districts than in similar five-day ones. Principals, teachers, parents and students in those districts mostly thought students learned just as much.
- **Hiring teachers.** District leaders often say the schedule helps them hire and keep teachers. A 2025 working paper on 178 Missouri districts found no lasting effect on how many teachers left. That paper hasn't been peer-reviewed. A Texas study found turnover fell about 2.7 percentage points.
- **Money.** Cost was the first reason districts switched, but estimated savings are small, about 0.4% to 2.5% of a budget.

**Major voices**
- RAND (Kilburn, Morton and colleagues).
- Researchers linked to Oregon State and NWEA (Paul Thompson, Emily Morton, Megan Kuhfeld).
- Economists who study effects outside the classroom, such as Fischer and Argyle on crime.
- Groups that track policy, such as NCSL.
- More and more, voters: Missouri's SB 727 (2024) now requires larger districts to win a public vote to use a four-day week.

**Patterns**
- Most districts that switch are small and rural, though some larger districts are now switching.
- Findings often depend on how many total hours of instruction a district keeps, not just how many days.
- Parent satisfaction is consistently high, even where test scores lag.
- Effects outside school point in different directions:
  - Colorado: juvenile offenses rose about 20%.
  - Oklahoma: bullying and fighting incidents fell.
  - Oregon: food insecurity rose, but that study had only 8 schools that switched.

**Open questions**
- Effects on working parents and childcare
- Effects on rural communities as a whole
- Effects on the youngest students
- How school boards actually weigh the evidence when they decide
- What happens as larger, non-rural districts switch
