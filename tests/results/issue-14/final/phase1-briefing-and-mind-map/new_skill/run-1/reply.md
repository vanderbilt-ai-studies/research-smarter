## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Does it hurt learning?** Paul Thompson's Oregon study found math and reading scores fell, with the gap growing over time. He links this to students losing about 3–4 hours of class a week ([Education Next, 2021](https://www.educationnext.org/shrinking-school-week-effects-four-day-schedule-student-achievement/)). Other studies, such as a 2024 Missouri state analysis, found no difference ([Education Week, Apr. 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04)).
- **Does it help keep teachers?** Leaders believe it does. But an Oregon study found teacher turnover went *up* over the long term ([Education Week, Oct. 2024](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/4-day-school-weeks-and-their-surprising-effect-on-teacher-turnover/2024/10)), while a Texas study found a small drop.
- **Is it worth the money?** Saving money is the main reason districts switch, but measured savings run about 0.4–2.5% of a budget.

**Major voices**
- RAND Corporation, whose multi-state study *Does Four Equal Five?* ([2021](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA373-1.html)) found that achievement grew more slowly in four-day districts.
- Oregon State University economist Paul Thompson and colleagues.
- Emily Morton (NWEA), who studied Oklahoma high schools.
- Economists studying side effects, like Jason Ward on parents' jobs and Stefanie Fischer and Daniel Argyle on youth crime.

**Patterns**
- Results often hinge on **how many class hours a district keeps**, not just the number of days.
- **Perceptions and data often disagree.** In RAND's study, many principals, teachers, and parents believed attendance, school climate, and achievement were the same or better than before. The data didn't back that up ([Idaho Education News, 2024](https://www.idahoednews.org/top-news/a-deep-dive-into-four-day-schools-and-researchers-found-contradictions/)).
- Most studies come from one state at a time (Oregon, Oklahoma, Colorado, Missouri) and from rural districts.

**Side effects researchers have found**
- **Behavior at school:** Morton found bullying fell about 39% and fights about 31% per student in Oklahoma ([EdWorkingPaper, 2022](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai21-416)). She notes part of that may simply be fewer hours at school.
- **Youth crime:** Fischer and Argyle linked four-day weeks to almost 20% more youth crime in Colorado, mostly property crime ([Chalkbeat, 2018](https://chalkbeat.org/2018/4/2/21104689/four-day-school-weeks-a-nationwide-symptom-of-tight-budgets-lead-to-more-youth-crime-study-finds)).
- **Parents' jobs:** Ward found employment dropped among married mothers but not single mothers or fathers ([IPUMS bibliography entry, 2019](https://bibliography.ipums.org/citations/show/300e2a54-e3dd-3893-b004-ab4172dd9298)).

**Open questions**
- **Food insecurity:** An Oregon survey study (Tomayko et al., *Journal of School Health*, 2021) found students in four-day schools reported more food insecurity. RAND's surveys didn't find that. I couldn't open the Oregon paper, so I'm going on summaries; check it before relying on it.
- **Longer-term effects:** It's still unclear whether academic and teacher effects change over the long run.
- **The fifth day:** Little is known about what students actually do on their day off.
- **Districts that switched back:** Few studies look at schools that returned to a five-day week.
