## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Do students learn less?** This is the central argument. A multi-state study by Morton, Thompson & Kuhfeld found a negative effect on reading scores (−0.07 SD) and slower growth over the year in both math and reading, with larger effects in non-rural schools ([EdWorkingPaper 22-630](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai22-630)). Other studies found no difference, including a 2024 Missouri state study, as reported by [Education Week (Apr. 2026)](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04).
- **Does it help hire and keep teachers?** Districts often say it does. The largest study so far, by Cade Lawson of Georgia State, covered Texas: turnover fell about 2.7 percentage points, but more than 1 in 5 teachers still left each year, and teacher qualifications didn't improve ([Education Week, Jan. 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/do-4-day-school-weeks-attract-and-retain-better-teachers-what-the-largest-study-yet-says/2026/01)).
- **Is it worth it financially?** The Education Commission of the States estimated savings of 0.4%–2.5% of district budgets. Programs run on the fifth day often eat up those savings (via the EdWeek explainer).

**Major voices**
- Economists Paul Thompson (Oregon State) and Emily Morton (NWEA)
- RAND, which studied districts in Idaho, New Mexico, and Oklahoma ([research brief, Kilburn et al., 2021](https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/research_briefs/RBA300/RBA373-1/RAND_RBA373-1.pdf))
- State education departments
- Education Week, which tracks the research for a general audience

**Patterns**
- **Rural districts dominate.** About 90% of four-day districts are rural.
- **Hours may matter more than days.** Districts with fewer than about 30 hours of class a week seem to drive most of the negative academic results ([NWEA blog, Morton](https://www.nwea.org/blog/2023/what-the-research-tells-us-about-four-day-school-weeks/)).
- **Surveys and hard data often disagree.** Parents and leaders report high satisfaction, while measured outcomes are mixed.
- **Non-academic effects point in different directions.** In Colorado, Fischer & Argyle linked the schedule to about 20% more youth crime, mostly property crime ([Chalkbeat on their study](https://chalkbeat.org/2018/4/2/21104689/four-day-school-weeks-a-nationwide-symptom-of-tight-budgets-lead-to-more-youth-crime-study-finds)). In Oklahoma, bullying and fights at school went down. An Oregon study found more food insecurity among four-day students. I only saw the Oklahoma and Oregon studies through Education Week's summary; I didn't open them.

**Open questions**
- How students and teachers use the fifth day
- Effects on mental health and safety
- How the schedule affects students from different income levels
- Whether short-term effects grow or fade over time
- Effects outside the handful of states where most studies were done

One note for your peer-reviewed requirement: the Morton study above is a working paper. As far as I can tell from its page, it hasn't been peer-reviewed yet.

You can challenge any claim here. The "30 hours a week" threshold is a good one to ask me about, since I got it from an NWEA blog post and not the underlying study.
