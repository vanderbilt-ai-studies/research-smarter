1. **Goodyear, V., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025).** School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe*, Article 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211

Students at restrictive schools used phones about 0.67 hours less during school. There was no difference in total weekday or weekend use, and no difference in wellbeing. *Details come from the [University of Birmingham repository page](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/school-phone-policies-and-their-association-with-mental-wellbeing/). 3. **Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026).** Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
   This surveyed 1,398 Dutch students (average age 16.2) at 24 schools. It compared classroom-only bans with whole-school bans. It found no difference in wellbeing or bullying. Full bans were linked to weaker student–teacher connectedness and, for girls, a lower sense of belonging. *I read the abstract on [Utrecht University's portal](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/disconnect-to-reconnect-how-variations-between-types-of-smartphon/). The publisher's page is paywalled.*

4. **Beneito, P., & Vicente-Chirivella, Ó. (2022).** Banning mobile phones in schools: Evidence from regional-level policies in Spain. *Applied Economic Analysis, 30*(90), 153–175. https://doi.org/10.1108/AEA-05-2021-0112
   This used police-reported bullying cases from 2012–2017. It compared two Spanish regions that banned phones in 2015 with regions that didn't. It found reductions of roughly 10–18% among students aged 12–14 and 15–17, and no effect for under-12s. It does not measure cyberbullying separately, and it only counts cases reported to police. *I read the full text, which is open access.*

5. **King, D. L., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., & Wade, T. (2024).** "Phones off while school's on": Evaluating problematic phone use and the social, wellbeing, and academic effects of banning phones in schools. *Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 13*(4), 913–922. https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.2024.00058
   This was a preregistered study of about 1,280 students in grades 7–12 at five South Australian schools. It found no difference in school belonging between ban and no-ban groups. Bullying was slightly higher in the ban group, and it fell significantly in both groups. The follow-up was only one month. *I read the abstract on [Flinders University's portal](https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/phones-off-while-schools-on-evaluating-problematic-phone-use-and-/).*

7. **Böttger, T., & Zierer, K. (2024).** To ban or not to ban? A rapid review on the impact of smartphone bans in schools on social well-being and academic performance. *Education Sciences, 14*(8), 906. https://doi.org/10.3390/educsci14080906

### Commentary (peer-reviewed journal)

8. **Weiss, H. A., & Bonell, C. (2025).** Smartphone use and mental health: Going beyond school restriction policies. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, 101237. [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850730/)
   This responds to source 1. It argues that students made up for lost phone time outside school. It also points out that school climate, bullying, and connectedness weren't measured, and calls for future studies on them.

### Working papers and reports (not peer-reviewed)

9. **Abrahamsson, S. (2024).** *Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health* (Discussion Paper No. 1/2024). Norwegian School of Economics. [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:hhs:nhheco:2024_001)
   This studied Norwegian middle schools. It reports that bullying fell for both boys and girls after bans, and that girls used fewer mental-health services. I found no published journal version.

10. **Anti-Bullying Centre, Dublin City University (2025).** Report led by Megan Reynolds. [DCU news release](https://www.dcu.ie/commsteam/news/2025/jun/smartphone-bans-have-little-or-no-impact-education-online-bullying-and)
    This combines a review of international research with student focus groups. It concludes that bans have little or no effect on cyberbullying, and that the stricter the ban, the harder students try to get around it.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Fairly plentiful:** studies of phone use during school compared with total daily use, and studies of general wellbeing.
- **Thin:** direct measures of face-to-face talk. The main evidence is students' own accounts (source 6). I found no observational studies, such as researchers counting conversations at lunch.
- **Not found:** any study that tracks *when* conflict or cyberbullying happens (school hours compared with evenings) before and after a ban. Source 2 comes closest. Most bullying measures don't separate in-person bullying from cyberbullying (see sources 4 and 5).
- **Ages:** several studies cover ages 12–15 or middle school, not specifically high school. Sources 3 and 4 include older teens.
- **Not searched:** US-specific evaluations. That gap is mine, not the literature's.

## Questions the results raise

- What counts as "interaction" in your essay: how much students talk, how good the conversations are, or how connected they feel? The sources measure different ones.
- Source 2 describes conflict moving in a different direction than you expected. It's worth thinking about what that means for the "moves to after school" part of your position.
- "Ban" means different things across studies: classroom-only, whole day, or phones physically collected.
