## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Does it hurt learning?** Most newer studies find small declines in test score growth, especially in reading. Some find no effect, like a 2022 Oklahoma study, and an early Colorado study (2015) found gains. One 12-state study (Thompson & Ward, 2022) found declines only in districts with low total instructional time, around 30 hours a week.
- **Does it help keep teachers?** Recruiting teachers is the main reason districts adopt it. Staff surveys and small case studies are often positive. Large data studies are less so: an Oregon study (Ainsworth, Penner & Liu, 2024) found teacher turnover *rose* 2.3 percentage points after districts switched.
- **Is it worth the money?** Savings usually come to about 1–2% of a district's spending, mostly on buses, operations and food service.

**Major voices**
- Economists Paul Thompson (Oregon State) and Emily Morton (NWEA) appear in most of the achievement and cost research.
- RAND did a large six-state study that included parent and student surveys.
- Education-policy researchers using state employment data have studied teachers.
- In policy, state legislatures matter. Missouri passed a 2024 law requiring larger districts to get voter approval and funding incentives for five-day schedules. Think tanks like Missouri's Show-Me Institute are also active.

**Patterns**
- The schedule spreads in waves during crises: the Great Depression, the 1970s energy crisis, the Great Recession and now after the pandemic. Researchers count more than 850 districts in 26 states, mostly rural.
- Results often differ between rural and non-rural districts.
- What people think of it and what the data show often disagree. Parents and staff tend to support it even where outcome data look neutral or negative.
- Most studies compare districts that *chose* the schedule, which makes cause and effect hard to pin down.

**Open questions**
- **Student health and wellbeing** is barely studied. One Oregon study linked switching to higher reported food insecurity among teens. Findings on bullying point in opposite directions in different states.
- **Families on the off day:** child care, parents' jobs and juvenile crime. A Colorado study linked the schedule to more juvenile property crime.
- **Who should decide:** voter-approval laws like Missouri's are new, and almost no research has looked at them yet.
