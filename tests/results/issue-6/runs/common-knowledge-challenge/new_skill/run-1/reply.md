**What the research shows** (based on two pages I opened today):
- **Some studies do find losses.** Paul Thompson, an economist at Oregon State, studied about 690,000 Oregon students in grades 3–8. Math and reading scores dropped after schools switched to four days. The drops were small on average and grew after several years. ([Education Next, 2021](https://www.educationnext.org/shrinking-school-week-effects-four-day-schedule-student-achievement/))
- **Other studies find no harm, or even small gains.** These include Colorado elementary schools (Anderson & Walker, 2015), Oklahoma high schools' ACT scores (Morton, 2022), Oregon kindergartners followed to third grade (Thompson et al., 2023), and rural schools in a six-state study (Morton, Thompson & Kuhfeld, 2024). ([Journalist's Resource roundup, 2025](https://journalistsresource.org/education/four-day-school-week-research/))
- **The amount of class time seems to matter a lot.** Thompson & Ward (2022) found test scores fell only at schools that cut instructional time a lot. Schools that kept their hours close to a five-day week showed no clear drop.

**How confident I am:** I'm fairly confident the evidence is mixed rather than clearly negative. Two caveats:
- I read summaries of these studies, not the peer-reviewed papers themselves. Before you cite any of them, find the original article through your library.
- Thompson's own Education Next article studies only Oregon, where most four-day schools are rural. That's a limit on how far his findings apply.
