"Common knowledge" isn't evidence. It doesn't tell you who found what, or how. Asking "how do you know?" is exactly the right move, including with me.

I checked just now. Here's what I found:

- **The evidence is mixed, and mostly negative for younger students.** The HEDCO Institute at the University of Oregon reviewed 11 studies in 2025. In rural districts, math and reading scores dropped for elementary and middle schoolers. High schoolers did better: math scores and graduation rates went up, but so did chronic absences. The reviewers found no evidence of benefits outside rural districts. They also said many studies didn't track what students did on the day off, and they didn't look at budgets or teachers at all. ([news coverage of the review](https://newschannel9.com/news/nation-world/4-day-school-weeks-offer-mixed-mostly-negative-outcomes-for-students-schools-education-teachers-university-of-oregon-hedco-institute-for-evidence-based-educational-practice). This is a news report about the review, not the review itself, so look up the original before you cite it.)
- **At least one peer-reviewed study points the other way.** Anderson and Walker (2015, *Education Finance and Policy*) studied elementary schools in Colorado. They found the four-day week was generally linked to *higher* reading and math scores, and little evidence that it harmed achievement. ([listing with abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:tpr:edfpol:v:10:y:2015:i:3:p:314-349). I've only read the abstract, not the full paper.)

So the accurate version is roughly "most recent studies find small negative effects, especially for younger kids, but results vary by state and setting." That's very different from "common knowledge that it hurts learning."
