## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Why districts switch.** Districts give three main reasons: saving money, hiring and keeping teachers, and attendance. In Texas, superintendents of small rural districts describe the shorter week as a perk to make up for salaries they can't raise. Other researchers point to budget savings on buses, support staff and food.
- **Does it save much?** NWEA's research summary puts average savings at about 2% of a district's budget, roughly $300 per student. RAND's 2021 study found that some administrators expected to save 4–12%, which RAND said isn't achievable.
- **Does it hurt learning?** Most studies find small negative effects on test scores. An earlier Colorado study found the opposite.
- **Does it help keep teachers?** A 2024 Oregon working paper found teacher turnover rose about 2.3 percentage points right after the switch. A Missouri paper found no lasting effect on hiring or keeping teachers.

**Major voices**
- RAND Corporation: *Can Four Equal Five?* (Kilburn et al., 2021) studied 36 districts in Idaho, New Mexico and Oklahoma.
- Economists Paul Thompson and Emily Morton (Morton is now at NWEA) wrote many of the peer-reviewed achievement studies.
- Penner, Ainsworth and Liu wrote the Oregon study on teacher turnover.
- Turner, Finch and Uribe-Zarain surveyed parents, staff and business leaders in rural Missouri.

**Patterns**
- About 90% of four-day districts are rural, so most of the evidence comes from small rural places.
- **Results depend on hours, not just days.** The drops in test scores show up mostly where the four days add up to fewer than about 30 hours of instruction a week.
- **Local beliefs often don't match the data.** In the RAND districts, people believed attendance, school climate and student well-being had improved. The data didn't show those gains. People's satisfaction with the schedule was still "generally very high."
- Some outcomes point in opposite directions. Fighting and bullying at school went down in Oklahoma, while juvenile crime went up in rural Colorado.

**Open questions**
- What happens on the day off: child care, meals, parents' jobs. Very little research looks at this.
- The recent surge in adoption. Texas went from 8 districts in 2019–20 to about 181, and researchers haven't caught up yet.
- How school boards actually make the decision, and what evidence they use.
- Whether the time in school is used efficiently, and how the schedule affects mental health and school climate.
