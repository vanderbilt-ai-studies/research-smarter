## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Why districts switch.** Districts give several reasons: budget pressure, recruiting teachers, and rural problems like long bus rides and sports travel. A 2020 national study found that some districts adopted the schedule alongside budget cuts, while many rural ones did it for practical reasons ([Oregon State](https://news.oregonstate.edu/news/four-day-school-weeks-becoming-more-common-mixed-results-study-finds)).
- **Effects on learning.** The results go both ways. A 2015 Colorado study found gains, and later studies in Oregon and several other states found small losses. Those losses were larger where total instructional hours dropped or where districts weren't rural ([Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-seven-new-studies-on-the-impact-of-a-four-day-school-week/), [Morton 2023](https://oej.scholasticahq.com/article/89149)).
- **Teachers.** Educators in Missouri widely believe the schedule helps them hire and keep teachers. A 2025 study of 178 Missouri districts found no statistically significant effect on turnover ([The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/k-12-leaders-love-the-four-day-school-week-but-a-new-study-shows-that-it-doesnt-do-what-they-hope/)).

**Major voices**
- Economist Paul Thompson at Oregon State, on costs, achievement, and why districts adopt the schedule.
- Emily Morton (NWEA, later AIR), on achievement and Oklahoma budgets.
- RAND, which surveyed 36 districts in Idaho, New Mexico, and Oklahoma in 2021.
- Stefanie Fischer and Daniel Argyle, who studied juvenile crime in Colorado in a peer-reviewed paper ([Chalkbeat](https://chalkbeat.org/posts/us/2018/04/02/four-day-school-weeks-a-nationwide-symptom-of-tight-budgets-leads-to-more-youth-crime-study-finds/)).

**Patterns**
- **Beliefs and data often disagree.** In the RAND districts, administrators expected to save 4–12%, and RAND found that wasn't achievable. People also believed attendance and school climate improved, and the survey data didn't support either belief. Satisfaction was still "generally very high" ([Idaho EdNews](https://www.idahoednews.org/top-news/a-deep-dive-into-four-day-schools-and-researchers-found-contradictions/)).
- **Measured savings are small.** Most estimates fall between 0.4% and 2.5% of a district's budget ([Education Week, 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04)). In Oklahoma, about a third of the savings came from food service, which federal money partly pays for anyway ([Morton 2023](https://oej.scholasticahq.com/article/89149)).
- **The schedule is spreading.** It went from about 250 schools in 1999 to more than 2,100 schools in 26 states by 2024–25, mostly in rural areas. It's starting to appear in some larger districts too ([Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04)).

**Open questions**
- What students do on the fifth day, and how families with different jobs or incomes handle it.
- Non-academic effects. One Colorado study linked the schedule to about 20% more youth crime, while Oklahoma saw fewer fights and less bullying.
- Whether results from rural districts apply to the newer suburban and urban adopters.
