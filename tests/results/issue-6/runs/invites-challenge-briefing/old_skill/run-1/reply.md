## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Do students learn less?** Many studies find small negative effects on test scores on average. Others, mostly of rural schools, find no effect or a positive one. There's also disagreement over whether the problem is the four-day schedule itself or simply fewer hours in school.
- **Does it save money?** Districts often present the switch as a cost-cutting move. Studies usually find savings of about 0.4 to 3% of a budget, mostly from buses, food service, and support staff hours. Results vary a lot between districts.
- **Does it help keep teachers?** District leaders often promote the schedule as a recruiting tool. The large studies of state employment data find little or no retention benefit, and one Oregon study found turnover went up after the switch.

**Major voices**
- Economists who study education run most of the large studies: Paul Thompson, Emily Morton, Stefanie Fischer, and others.
- RAND, NWEA, and CALDER produce major reports and working papers. Working papers are not peer-reviewed, which matters for your source requirement.
- Superintendents and state legislators shape the public debate, often through local news.

**Patterns**
- About 90% of four-day districts are rural, and the research is concentrated in a few states: Oregon, Colorado, Oklahoma, and Missouri.
- How much weekly instructional time a school keeps comes up again and again as the key variable.
- What people say in surveys tends to be more positive than what the hard data shows. Parents, teachers, and students mostly like the schedule, even where the numbers don't show the expected benefits.
- Districts' stated reasons have changed over time. During recessions they talked about money. More recently, many talk about recruiting teachers.

**Open questions**
- Students' own experiences: they are surveyed occasionally but almost never studied in depth.
- Effects on families: child care, parents' jobs, and food insecurity. The evidence on all three is thin or inconclusive.
- Suburban districts, which are being studied less rigorously than rural ones even as more of them adopt the schedule.
- Whether results from one state apply to another.
