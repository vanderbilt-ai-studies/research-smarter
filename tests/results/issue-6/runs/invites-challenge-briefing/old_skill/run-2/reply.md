## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Do students learn less?** This is the biggest argument. A six-state study (Morton, Thompson & Kuhfeld, *Economics of Education Review*, 2024) found students learned less on average, but rural students did about as well. An earlier Colorado study (Anderson & Walker, 2015) found no harm, and even some gains. ([Journalist's Resource roundup](https://journalistsresource.org/education/four-day-school-week-research/))
- **Does it save money?** Districts often list budget savings as a reason. An Education Commission of the States analysis found that districts actually saved about 0.4–2.5% of their budgets, and that new programs for the day off often used up those savings. ([Education Week, 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04)) *I only saw that analysis through Education Week's coverage, not the original report. One working paper reportedly finds bigger savings, about 3.1%, but I only saw that number in a search snippet, so check it before you use it.*
- **Does it help keep teachers?** Surveys say teachers like the idea. An Oregon study, though, found teacher turnover went *up* after districts switched ([FutureEd summary](https://www.future-ed.org/research-notes-does-a-four-day-school-week-improve-teacher-retention/)), and a Missouri study found no lasting benefit ([EdSource](https://edsource.org/updates/study-four-day-school-week-hardly-improves-teacher-retention)). A Texas study found a small drop in turnover.

**Major voices**
Economists Paul Thompson (Oregon State) and Emily Morton lead the research on student achievement. Other important sources are RAND, the CALDER research center, the Education Commission of the States, and Missouri researchers such as Cory Koedel.

**Patterns**
- Most studies use state test scores or staffing records from one state or a few states.
- Results seem to depend on schedule design. Declines show up mostly where districts cut total weekly hours (Thompson & Ward, 2022).
- The districts that switch are mostly small and rural. That makes it hard to apply the findings to large or suburban districts.

**Open questions**
- What do families do on the fifth day? Researchers raise child care and missing school meals as concerns, but almost no one has measured them.
- The effects outside academics point in different directions. Bullying went down in Oklahoma, but juvenile crime went up in Colorado.
- How have districts' reasons changed? Adoption grew during the 2008 recession, but since the pandemic, teacher shortages are cited more often.

Many of the studies above come from the Journalist's Resource roundup. I didn't open each original paper, so confirm the details in the papers themselves before you cite any of them. One I did open is peer-reviewed and could count toward your requirement: [Thompson et al., *Early Childhood Research Quarterly*, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9979633).
