## Preliminary briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Learning.** Findings on test scores are mixed. A 2015 Colorado study found gains. A 2024 study across six states found students learned less overall, with almost no effect in rural districts and larger losses in non-rural ones. An Oregon study tied losses to cutting 3–4 hours of class time a week.
- **Teachers.** Leaders and teachers often say the schedule helps with hiring. Studies that measure actual turnover disagree. A 2024 Oregon working paper found turnover *rose* after districts switched, and Missouri data found no hiring benefit.
- **Money.** Estimates of savings are small, about 0.4–2.5% of a district's budget. Most of it comes from buses, food service and building costs, because teacher salaries mostly stay the same.

**Major voices**
- Economists Paul Thompson (Oregon State) and Emily Morton (NWEA) are behind many of the large studies
- The RAND Corporation (a 2021 study of family and staff views)
- CALDER and EdWorkingPapers, which publish working papers that are not peer-reviewed

**Patterns**
- Most research comes from a few rural-heavy states: Oregon, Oklahoma, Colorado and Missouri.
- Districts that lose the least class time seem to see the smallest effects on learning.
- Students, parents and staff report high satisfaction, even where measured outcomes are flat or negative.
- The reasons districts give have shifted. After the 2008 recession, budget cuts were the main reason. Recent coverage says recruiting teachers is now the most common one.

**Open questions**
- What happens on the fifth day: childcare, meals, supervision. One Colorado study found juvenile crime rose about 20%.
- Costs that move onto families and towns and never show up in a school budget
- How school boards actually make the decision, and whose voices count
- Whether the benefits fade once neighboring districts switch too
