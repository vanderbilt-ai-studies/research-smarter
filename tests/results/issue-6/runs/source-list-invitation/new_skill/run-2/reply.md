### Peer-reviewed journal articles (7)

1. **Bar, E., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., Wade, T. D., & King, D. L. (2025).** Student perspectives on banning mobile phones in South Australian secondary schools: A large-scale qualitative analysis. *Computers in Human Behavior, 167*, 108603. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108603
   This paper analyzes about 7,000 open-ended survey answers from roughly 1,500 students at five schools after South Australia's all-day ban. ⚠️ I couldn't read the paper itself. I confirmed the citation details through Crossref. What I know about its findings comes from [Flinders University's news page](https://news.flinders.edu.au/blog/2025/09/18/benefits-of-school-phone-bans/): students reported more face-to-face interaction and richer conversations, and some reported fewer fights and less online bullying. That page also mixes up details between two studies, so check the paper itself.

2. **Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025).** School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe*, 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211
   The study covers 1,227 students aged 12–15 at 30 English schools. Students at restrictive schools used their phones less during school hours, but total weekday and weekend use was about the same, and wellbeing didn't differ. The abstract doesn't mention social interaction. (The record I saw didn't list a volume number, so add it from the journal page.)

3. **Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Michail, M., Patterson, P., Sitch, A., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2026).** How school phone policies influence adolescent phone use and wellbeing (SMART Schools): A qualitative comparative case study. *Social Science & Medicine, 398*, 119094. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2026.119094
   This is a qualitative follow-up from the same team. According to the [University of Birmingham's summary](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/news/2026/school-phone-bans-or-not-digital-lives-of-students-affect-the-school-day):
   - Students at restrictive schools reported more in-person interaction during the day.
   - They also said they used their phones more after school to make up for it.
   - Conflicts that started in group chats outside school sometimes turned into face-to-face confrontations at school.

   I couldn't read the paper or its abstract, only that summary.

4. **Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026).** Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
   The study compares 1,398 students (average age 16.2) at schools with a full ban against schools with a classroom-only ban. Bullying didn't differ between the two groups. Full bans were linked to weaker student–teacher connection, and for girls, to a weaker sense of belonging at school.

5. **Beneito, P., & Vicente-Chirivella, Ó. (2022).** Banning mobile phones in schools: Evidence from regional-level policies in Spain. *Applied Economic Analysis, 30*(90), 153–175. https://doi.org/10.1108/AEA-05-2021-0112
   Two Spanish regions banned phones in 2015, and the authors compare them with the rest of Spain. Bullying fell by roughly 15–18% among 12–14-year-olds. The data counts only cases that families reported to police, which the authors note probably captures only serious incidents.

6. **Chen, S. C., Huang, T. F., Chang, K., Chang, F. C., Chiang, S. C., Chiu, C. H., Chen, P. H., Miao, N. F., & Chuang, H. Y. (2026).** Association between school phone restriction policies and adolescents' cyberbullying, gambling, and substance use behaviors. *Computers in Human Behavior, 177*, 108898. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108898
   This is a one-time 2022 survey of 3,158 high school students at 35 schools in Taiwan. Students who said their school had no phone rules reported more cyberbullying. Because it's a single survey, it shows a correlation, not cause and effect.

7. **Campbell, M., Edwards, E., Pennell, D., Poed, S., Lister, V., Gillett-Swan, J., Kelly, A., Zec, D., & Nguyen, T.-A. (2024).** Evidence for and against banning mobile phones in schools: A scoping review. *Journal of Psychologists and Counsellors in Schools, 34*(3), 242–265. https://doi.org/10.1177/20556365241270394
   The review covers 22 studies on academics, wellbeing and cyberbullying. It found no randomized trials, and the studies define "ban" in different ways. I only saw the abstract on [QUT's repository](https://eprints.qut.edu.au/251231/), so you'll need the full text for its cyberbullying findings.

### Working papers (not peer-reviewed yet)

8. **Allcott, H., Baron, E. J., Dee, T., Duckworth, A. L., Gentzkow, M., & Jacob, B. (2026).** *The effects of school phone bans: National evidence from lockable pouches* (NBER Working Paper No. 35132). https://doi.org/10.3386/w35132
   This is a large US study of schools that adopted lockable pouches. Phone use dropped. Discipline incidents rose in the first year and then faded, and wellbeing turned positive in later years. The authors found "no clear evidence" of change in perceived online bullying. Some of the data came from the pouch company.

9. **Abrahamsson, S. (2024).** *Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health* (Discussion Paper No. 1/2024). Norwegian School of Economics. https://swopec.hhs.se/nhheco/abs/nhheco2024_001.htm
   This paper uses data from Norwegian middle schools. The abstract says bullying decreased for both boys and girls after bans.

### Reports and commentary

10. **Reynolds, M., et al. (2025).** *Restriction or resilience? Smartphone bans in schools.* DCU Anti-Bullying Centre. [Press release](https://www.dcu.ie/commsteam/news/2025/jun/smartphone-bans-have-little-or-no-impact-education-online-bullying-and)
    This report combines a review of existing research with student focus groups. It concludes that bans have "little or no impact" on online bullying, and that the stricter the ban, the more students look for ways around it. I only read the press release, so check the full author list in the report.

11. **Martin, J. D., & Bakour, C. (2025).** Survey of 1,500 kids suggests school phone bans have important but limited effects. *The 74*. [Link](https://www.the74million.org/article/survey-of-1500-kids-suggests-school-phone-bans-have-important-but-limited-effects/)
    This is an opinion-style article about a survey of Florida students aged 11–13. Students at schools that restrict phones reported *more* cyberbullying, though the authors don't claim the bans caused it.

### What I found and didn't find

- **Plentiful:** studies on bullying and cyberbullying, though their results point in different directions.
- **Thin:** evidence that students talk face-to-face more comes almost entirely from student and teacher reports (sources 1 and 3). I found no study that actually measured how much students talk in person.
- **Not found:** any study that directly tests whether conflict *moves* to after school, for example by tracking when cyberbullying happens. The closest is source 3. Source 2 found that students' overall weekday phone time stayed the same.
- **Age mismatch:** several samples are middle schoolers (sources 5, 9, 11). Sources 4 and 6 and part of source 8 are closest to high school.

### Questions these results raise

- Source 3 describes conflict flowing *into* school from outside. How does that fit with "conflict just moves to after school"?
- What kind of evidence would show that conflict *moved*, rather than that most of it was already happening outside school?
