**Peer-reviewed studies**

1. Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, Article 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211

Students at restrictive schools used their phones less during the day, but overall daily phone and social media time came out about the same. The study found no link between the policies and wellbeing, sleep or grades. *I couldn't open the paper itself. This summary comes from the Crossref record, the journal commentary below and the [university press release](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/news/2025/school-bans-alone-not-enough-to-tackle-negative-impacts-of-phone-and-social-media-use).*

2. Bar, E., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., Wade, T. D., & King, D. L. (2025). Student perspectives on banning mobile phones in South Australian secondary schools: A large-scale qualitative analysis. *Computers in Human Behavior, 167*, Article 108603. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108603
   - About 7,000 open-ended survey answers from more than 1,500 students. Students described more face-to-face interaction, better conversations, and less conflict and cyberbullying. *The journal page blocked me. This summary comes from [Flinders University's write-up](https://news.flinders.edu.au/blog/2025/09/18/benefits-of-school-phone-bans/), not the paper.*

3. King, D. L., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., & Wade, T. (2024). "Phones off while school's on": Evaluating problematic phone use and the social, wellbeing, and academic effects of banning phones in schools. *Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 13*(4), 913–922. https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.2024.00058 (free full text: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11737419/))
   - Compares five schools (grades 7–12) with and without a ban, surveyed about a month apart. Bullying fell in both groups by a similar amount, so the authors don't credit the ban. School belonging didn't change. The authors say they didn't measure face-to-face interaction directly.

4. Baggio, S., Wade, T., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Billieux, J., Starcevic, V., Quinney, B., & King, D. L. (2025). Psychological consequences of school mobile phone bans: Emulated trial of a natural experiment in South Australia. *Computers in Human Behavior*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108767
   - Found lower psychological distress after South Australia's statewide ban. *I only saw this through the Flinders page and didn't confirm the DOI or volume myself, so check it before citing.*

5. Beneito, P., & Vicente-Chirivella, Ó. (2022). Banning mobile phones in schools: Evidence from regional-level policies in Spain. *Applied Economic Analysis, 30*(90), 153–175. https://doi.org/10.1108/AEA-05-2021-0112 (open access)
   - Compares two Spanish regions that banned phones in 2015 with the rest of Spain. Bullying fell about 9.5–18% among 12–17-year-olds. The data are police-reported cases only, and the authors call the results "suggestive."

6. Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
   - 1,398 students at 24 Dutch schools, average age 16. Schools with whole-school bans showed no difference in bullying from schools that banned phones only in class. The whole-school bans were linked to weaker student–teacher connection, and to lower belonging among girls. *I read the abstract only, through the [Maastricht repository](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/disconnect-to-reconnect-how-variations-between-types-of-smartphon/).*

    - Florida students aged 11–13 at schools that limit phones reported *more* cyberbullying. The authors say this may be because schools with bad bullying were the ones that adopted bans.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plenty:** studies on whether bans change phone time, wellbeing and bullying at school.
- **Thin:** direct evidence on face-to-face talk. Most of it comes from student self-reports (#2) or from belonging and connectedness measures (#3, #6), not from observing how students actually interact.
- **Missing:** I found **no study that measures whether conflict or cyberbullying moves to after school.** The closest are studies showing phone *use* shifts to outside school hours (#1, #7). Moving phone use and moving conflict aren't the same thing.
- Almost all of this is from outside the US, and none of it follows students for long.

## Questions the results raise

- Your position depends on conflict moving to after school, but no study has measured that directly. What would count as evidence for it, and could some of these sources stand in for that evidence?
- Source #6 suggests stricter bans may *weaken* some connections. How does that fit with "more face-to-face talk"?
