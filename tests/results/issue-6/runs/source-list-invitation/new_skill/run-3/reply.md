## Sources

**Peer-reviewed studies**

1. Bar, E., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., Wade, T. D., & King, D. L. (2025). Student perspectives on banning mobile phones in South Australian secondary schools: A large-scale qualitative analysis. *Computers in Human Behavior, 167*, 108603. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108603
   Five South Australian high schools; 1,549 students wrote 7,188 open-ended survey answers. Students reported benefits for face-to-face social interaction, safety and classroom engagement. They also reported downsides: feeling less independent and less trusted, and finding it harder to manage emotional distress without their phones. *I read the abstract, not the full text. It's open access, so you can read all of it.*

2. Baggio, S., Wade, T., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Billieux, J., Starcevic, V., Quinney, B., & King, D. L. (2025). Psychological consequences of school mobile phone bans: Emulated trial of a natural experiment in South Australia. *Computers in Human Behavior, 172*, 108767. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108767
   1,062 students in schools that had already started a ban were compared with students in schools that hadn't yet. The ban was linked to small reductions in psychological distress and negative mood, and no change in positive mood. It doesn't measure social interaction or bullying. *Abstract only.*

3. Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe*, Article 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211
   1,227 students aged 12–15 at 30 English schools. Restrictive schools had about 40 minutes less phone use during the school day. There was no difference in total weekday or weekend use, and no difference in wellbeing. It's a snapshot comparison, so it can't show cause and effect. *Abstract via the [Birmingham repository](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/school-phone-policies-and-their-association-with-mental-wellbeing/). I couldn't confirm the volume number, so check it.*

4. Campbell, M., Edwards, E. J., Pennell, D., Poed, S., Lister, V., Gillett-Swan, J., Kelly, A., Zec, D., & Nguyen, T.-A. (2024). Evidence for and against banning mobile phones in schools: A scoping review. *Journal of Psychologists and Counsellors in Schools, 34*(3), 242–265. https://doi.org/10.1177/20556365241270394
   A pre-registered review of 22 studies covering academic results, mental health and cyberbullying. It found no randomized trials, and the studies use inconsistent definitions, so the authors call the evidence hard to reconcile. *I read the abstract only. News coverage says that inside the review, four studies found small drops in bullying and two found more online harassment at schools with bans. I haven't confirmed those numbers in the paper.*

5. Abrahamsson, S. (2026). Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health. *Journal of Human Resources*. Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.0224-13403R2
   Norwegian middle schools; it links data on school phone policies to national administrative records. Bullying dropped for both girls and boys after bans. Girls' use of health care for psychological symptoms dropped too. *The journal version is paywalled. I only read the [2024 working-paper abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:hhs:nhheco:2024_001), so the published results may differ. Note that these are middle schools, not high schools.*

**Not peer-reviewed, from credible institutions**

6. Allcott, H., Baron, E. J., Dee, T., Duckworth, A. L., Gentzkow, M., & Jacob, B. (2026). *The effects of school phone bans: National evidence from lockable pouches* (NBER Working Paper No. 35132). National Bureau of Economic Research. https://www.nber.org/papers/w35132
   Tracks schools that adopted lockable phone pouches, among more than 43,000 U.S. middle and high schools. Phone use dropped. Discipline incidents rose in year one and then faded. Wellbeing dipped at first and later improved. There was little effect on perceived online bullying. *Abstract plus [Stanford's summary](https://humsci.stanford.edu/feature/national-study-school-cellphone-bans-shows-benefits-are-not-immediate).*

7. Reynolds, M., Esfandiari, M., & O'Higgins Norman, J. (2025). *Restriction or resilience? Smartphone bans in schools: A qualitative study of the experiences of students*. DCU Anti-Bullying Centre. https://www.dcu.ie/antibullyingcentre/restriction-or-resilience
   Focus groups with 66 Irish students aged 10–18. Students said cyberbullying mostly happens outside school and that bans just postpone it until after school. Many doubted that bans make people socialize. They described ways of getting around stricter bans.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plenty:** studies on mental health and wellbeing, and reviews that call the overall evidence mixed.
- **Thin:** evidence on face-to-face interaction comes almost entirely from what students, teachers and principals *report*. I found no study that directly observed or measured how much students talk in person.
- **Not found:** any study that measured whether conflict or cyberbullying actually *increases after school* once a ban starts. The DCU students say it does, but that's their perception, not a measurement. Goodyear shows total phone use doesn't change, which is about phone use, not conflict.

## Questions the results raise

- If conflict "moves" after school, what would you expect total bullying to look like? Abrahamsson reports a drop, while Allcott and colleagues report little change in perceived online bullying.
- Most of the evidence comes from middle schools or mixed ages. How much does that matter for a question about high school?
