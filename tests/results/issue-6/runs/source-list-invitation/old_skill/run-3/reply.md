### Peer-reviewed studies with numbers (surveys, before/after designs)

1. Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
   - The authors surveyed 1,398 Dutch students aged about 16 at 24 schools, comparing classroom-only bans with whole-school bans, all at one point in time. They found no difference in classmate connectedness, bullying, or cyberbullying. Students at full-ban schools reported lower connection with teachers. The study didn't directly measure face-to-face interaction.

2. Vereecke, J., Vanherle, R., Nassen, L.-M., Boen, H., Eggermont, S., Beullens, K., Vandenbosch, L., & Schreurs, L. (2026). More than disconnecting? School smartphone bans and adolescents' social wellbeing. *Frontiers in Developmental Psychology, 4*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fdpys.2026.1796471
   - This study followed 960 students at one Belgian school with surveys before a total ban and every month after it, with no comparison school. Support from friends stayed steady, and loneliness at school rose slightly. Cyberbullying victimization didn't change. Cyberbullying perpetration dipped a little at two weeks, but rates were very low to begin with. Only about 63% of students said they followed the ban, and the study didn't measure conflict outside school. *Check the article number on the journal page; I couldn't confirm it.*

3. Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, Article 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211
   - This compared 1,227 students aged 12–15 at 30 English schools with strict or permissive phone rules. Students at strict schools used phones and social media less during the school day, but their total weekday and weekend use was no different. Wellbeing was also no different. *I read only the abstract, through the [University of Birmingham repository](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/school-phone-policies-and-their-association-with-mental-wellbeing/).*

### Peer-reviewed qualitative studies (students describing their experience)

4. Bar, E., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., Wade, T. D., & King, D. L. (2025). Student perspectives on banning mobile phones in South Australian secondary schools: A large-scale qualitative analysis. *Computers in Human Behavior, 167*, Article 108603. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108603
   - About 1,549 students at five schools answered open-ended questions after a statewide ban. ⚠️ *I couldn't open the full text.* According to the [Flinders University summary](https://news.flinders.edu.au/blog/2025/09/18/benefits-of-school-phone-bans/), students described more in-person talk, more friendships, and less conflict and cyberbullying. Some students also said they relied on their phones to cope emotionally. Read the paper itself before relying on these points.

5. Bisom-Rapp, E., Otmar, C. D., Potes, C., Nayak, S., Engle, K., Ganson, K. T., & Nagata, J. M. (2026). The need for autonomy, safety, and structure: A qualitative study of adolescent perspectives on school phone restrictions. *Academic Pediatrics, 26*(5), Article 103333. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.acap.2026.103333
   - The authors interviewed 30 US students aged 16–19. Students reacted to bans based on needs like independence and feeling safe, and some broke the rules. Once phones were banned, some of the social uses phones had served went unmet. *I read the abstract only.*

6. Hartio, S., Hakkarainen, K., Juuti, K., & Sormunen, K. (2026). "As long as you have your smartphone, you're connected with friends": Lower secondary students' perceptions of smartphones' role in social relationships at school. *Youth & Society*. Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.1177/0044118X261465033
   - Researchers used questionnaires and focus groups with 58 Finnish students, who are younger than high schoolers. Students said phones can make interaction easier but can also cut down conversation and add tension and loneliness. *I read the abstract only.*

### Review

7. Campbell, M., Edwards, E., Pennell, D., Poed, S., Lister, V., Gillett-Swan, J., Kelly, A., Zec, D., & Nguyen, T.-A. (2024). Evidence for and against banning mobile phones in schools: A scoping review. *Journal of Psychologists and Counsellors in Schools, 34*(3), 242–265. https://doi.org/10.1177/20556365241270394
   - This reviewed 22 studies on academics, wellbeing, and cyberbullying. It found no randomized trials, and the studies defined "ban" in different ways. *I read only the abstract, through the [QUT repository](https://eprints.qut.edu.au/251231/). Search results said some included studies found less bullying and two found more online victimization, but I couldn't confirm that myself. The full text is listed as open access.*

### Working paper (not peer-reviewed)

8. Allcott, H., Baron, E. J., Dee, T., Duckworth, A. L., Gentzkow, M., & Jacob, B. (2026). *The effects of school phone bans: National evidence from lockable pouches* (NBER Working Paper No. 35132). National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w35132
   - This is a large US study comparing schools before and after they adopted locked phone pouches. Phone use fell. Discipline incidents rose in the first year and later faded, and wellbeing dipped at first and later improved. The authors found no clear change in students' perceived online bullying.

### What I found and didn't find

- **Plenty:** students' reports that they talk more in person. These come mostly from qualitative studies and surveys.
- **Thin:** direct measurement of face-to-face interaction. None of the number-based studies I found actually counted in-person conversations.
- **Not found:** any study that measures whether conflict *moves* to after school. Several studies measure cyberbullying overall, and some authors suggest it happens outside school hours, but I found no study that tracks when it happens before and after a ban. The closest thing is Goodyear et al. showing that total phone use doesn't drop. That's about screen time, not conflict.
- **Few:** long-term studies. Almost all are from 2024 to 2026.

### Questions these results raise

- If a study finds no change in cyberbullying overall, could you tell whether it moved after school or simply never happened during school hours in the first place?
- Several studies report that only part of the student body followed the ban. How might that affect what any of them can show?
