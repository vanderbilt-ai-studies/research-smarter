**Peer-reviewed studies**

1. Bar, E., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., Wade, T. D., & King, D. L. (2025). Student perspectives on banning mobile phones in South Australian secondary schools: A large-scale qualitative analysis. *Computers in Human Behavior, 167*, 108603. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108603
   About 7,000 written survey answers from over 1,500 students at five schools. Students said they had more face-to-face interaction and better conversations, and they described less cyberbullying and conflict. ⚠️ The journal page blocked me, so this summary comes from [Flinders University's news release](https://news.flinders.edu.au/blog/2025/09/18/benefits-of-school-phone-bans/). I confirmed the DOI through Crossref.

2. King, D. L., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., & Wade, T. (2024). "Phones off while school's on": Evaluating problematic phone use and the social, wellbeing, and academic effects of banning phones in schools. *Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 13*(4), 913–922. https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.2024.00058
   Five South Australian schools surveyed students in Grades 7–12 before a ban and one month after. Bullying fell in both the ban and no-ban groups, and the ban made no significant difference. The bullying measure combined in-person and online bullying. The authors say they did not measure face-to-face communication. (I read the full text on [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11737419/).)

3. Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211
   1,227 students aged 12–15 at 30 English schools. Students at restrictive schools used their phones about 30 minutes less during the school day but made up for it outside school. Wellbeing and sleep showed no differences. ⚠️ I read about this paper through a commentary (source 7), not the paper itself.

4. Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Michail, M., Patterson, P., Sitch, A., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2026). How school phone policies influence adolescent phone use and wellbeing (SMART Schools): A qualitative comparative case study. *Social Science & Medicine, 398*, 119094. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2026.119094
   This is the qualitative follow-up to source 3. Students and staff at restrictive schools described more face-to-face interaction during the day. They also said bullying and conflict that started on phones outside school still "blow up" in person at school. ⚠️ This summary comes from the [University of Birmingham's news release](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/news/2026/school-phone-bans-or-not-digital-lives-of-students-affect-the-school-day).

5. Beneito, P., & Vicente-Chirivella, Ó. (2022). Banning mobile phones in schools: Evidence from regional-level policies in Spain. *Applied Economic Analysis, 30*(90), 153–175. https://doi.org/10.1108/AEA-05-2021-0112
   Compares two Spanish regions that banned phones with regions that didn't. After the bans, reported bullying fell by about 15–18% for ages 12–14 and about 9.5–18% for ages 15–17. The data are bullying cases that families reported to police, and they don't say whether incidents happened online or in person. (I read the full text, which is open access.)

6. Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
   1,398 students at 24 Dutch schools, comparing classroom-only bans with whole-school bans. Bullying and wellbeing showed no significant differences. Full bans were linked to weaker student–teacher connection and, for girls, a lower sense of belonging at school. (Abstract only, from the [Utrecht University portal](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/disconnect-to-reconnect-how-variations-between-types-of-smartphon/).)

7. Weiss, H. A., & Bonell, C. (2025). Smartphone use and mental health: Going beyond school restriction policies. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, 101237. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101237

**Not peer-reviewed** (working paper, report, survey)

8. Allcott, H., Baron, E. J., Dee, T., Duckworth, A. L., Gentzkow, M., & Jacob, B. (2026). *The effects of school phone bans: National evidence from lockable pouches* (NBER Working Paper No. 35132). National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w35132
   A large US study of schools using lockable phone pouches. The pouches cut phone use. Disciplinary incidents rose in the first year and then faded, and there was no clear improvement in students' perceptions of online bullying. (Abstract only.)

9. Reynolds, M., et al. (2025). *Restriction or resilience? Smartphone bans in schools: A qualitative study of the experiences of students*. DCU Anti-Bullying Centre. https://antibullyingcentre.ie/publication/restriction-or-resilience/
   Irish students said stricter bans led more students to look for ways around them. The report says international evidence shows bans have "little or no impact" on online bullying. ⚠️ I only read [DCU's press release](https://www.dcu.ie/commsteam/news/2025/jun/smartphone-bans-have-little-or-no-impact-education-online-bullying-and), and I couldn't confirm the full author list.

10. Martin, J. D., & Bakour, C. (2025). A survey of 1,500 Florida children aged 11–13, reported in [The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/survey-of-1500-kids-suggests-school-phone-bans-have-important-but-limited-effects/). Children at schools with phone limits reported *more* cyberbullying, and the authors say this doesn't show the bans caused it. ⚠️ I found no published paper for this survey.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plenty:** students and staff *saying* they talk face-to-face more under bans (sources 1 and 4).
- **Thin:** studies that actually *measure* face-to-face interaction. Most of the evidence is self-report.
- **Missing:** I found no study that tracks conflict *after school* separately from conflict during school. Several studies measure online bullying, but none ask when or where it happens.
- **Age mismatch:** several samples are 11–15-year-olds, younger than the high school students in your question.
- **Short timeframes:** most follow-ups cover a year or less.

## Questions the results raise

- If online bullying stays the same under a ban, does that show conflict *moved*, or that it was never happening at school in the first place?
- Source 4 describes conflict flowing from outside school *into* school. How does that fit with the direction your position describes?
