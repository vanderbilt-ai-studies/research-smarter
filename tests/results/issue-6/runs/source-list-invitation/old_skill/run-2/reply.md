### Peer-reviewed studies (quantitative)

1. **Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026).** Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
   1,398 students in 24 Dutch schools, comparing partial bans with full bans. The two groups didn't differ on well-being or bullying. Full bans were linked to weaker student–teacher connectedness, and for girls, a weaker sense of belonging at school. *(Abstract only.)*

2. **Vereecke, J., Vanherle, R., Nassen, L.-M., Boen, H., Eggermont, S., Beullens, K., Vandenbosch, L., & Schreurs, L. (2026).** More than disconnecting? School smartphone bans and adolescents' social wellbeing. *Frontiers in Developmental Psychology, 4.* https://doi.org/10.3389/fdpys.2026.1796471
   960 students in one Belgian school, surveyed once before a total ban and monthly for seven months after. Cyberbullying victimization didn't change. Perpetration dipped slightly right after the ban, but it was rare to begin with. Social loneliness rose modestly over the year. Face-to-face interaction wasn't measured. There was no comparison school, and about 63% of students said they followed the ban. *(I couldn't find the article number. Check it on the page.)*

3. **Bonilla Aparicio, E., Sigmarsdottir, B., Haskell, E., Sirotina, U., Sveinbjornsdottir, B., Asgeirsdottir, B. B., Valdimarsdottir, H. B., Odgers, C. L., Sigfusdottir, I. D., Allegrante, J. P., & Halldorsdottir, T. (2026).** School phone bans and adolescent mental health in Iceland. *JAMA Network Open, 9*(10), e2640893. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.40893
   Survey data from 70 Icelandic schools across 2016–2023, comparing the 22 schools that adopted bans with the rest (about 24,000 responses from 13–15-year-olds). Bans weren't linked to meaningful changes in mental health. The study also measured perceived peer support and both in-person bullying and cyberbullying, and found similar null results for those. *(Abstract only.)*

4. **Goodyear, V., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025).** School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe*, Article 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211
   1,227 students aged 12–15 in 30 English schools. Students at restrictive schools used their phones about 40 minutes less during school time. On weekdays overall and on weekends there was no difference, and wellbeing showed no difference either. It's a single snapshot in time, so it shows association, not cause. *(I couldn't confirm the volume number.)*

5. **Chen, S.-C., Huang, T.-F., Chang, K., Chang, F.-C., Chiang, S. C., Chiu, C.-H., Chen, P.-H., Miao, N.-F., & Chuang, H.-Y. (2026).** Association between school phone restriction policies and adolescents' cyberbullying, gambling, and substance use behaviors. *Computers in Human Behavior, 177*, Article 108898. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108898
   ⚠️ I confirmed the citation but couldn't read the abstract (paywalled). A search snippet said students at schools without restrictions reported more cyberbullying. Read the abstract yourself before relying on that.

### Reviews and commentary

8. **Campbell, M., Edwards, E. J., Pennell, D., Poed, S., Lister, V., Gillett-Swan, J., Kelly, A., Zec, D., & Nguyen, T.-A. (2024).** Evidence for and against banning mobile phones in schools: A scoping review. *Journal of Psychologists and Counsellors in Schools, 34*(3), 242–265. https://doi.org/10.1177/20556365241270394
   Reviews 22 studies on academics, wellbeing, and cyberbullying. It found no randomized trials, and the studies define "ban" and measure outcomes in different ways. Secondhand summaries say six of the studies found less bullying after a ban and two found more online victimization. I couldn't open the full text to confirm those numbers.

9. **Weiss, H. A., & Bonell, C. (2025).** Smartphone use and mental health: Going beyond school restriction policies. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, Article 101237. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101237
   A commentary on the SMART Schools study (#4). It argues that phone use outside school has to be addressed too. This is an opinion piece, not a study.

### Working papers (not peer-reviewed)

10. **Abrahamsson, S. (2024).** *Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health* (Discussion Paper No. 1/2024). Norwegian School of Economics. https://EconPapers.repec.org/RePEc:hhs:nhheco:2024_001
    Uses Norwegian school records and the different dates schools adopted bans. Bullying fell for both boys and girls after bans, and girls' grades rose. The abstract doesn't say where or when the bullying happened.

11. **Figlio, D. N., & Özek, U. (2025).** *The impact of cellphone bans in schools on student outcomes: Evidence from Florida* (NBER Working Paper No. 34388). https://doi.org/10.3386/w34388
    Florida schools: suspensions rose sharply in the first year of enforcement, especially among Black students, then dropped after year one. Unexcused absences fell, and test scores improved in year two.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plenty:** large surveys of bans and wellbeing or bullying (Netherlands, Iceland, England, Belgium, Norway).
- **Thin:** direct measures of face-to-face talk. The evidence on that is mostly students describing their own experience in interviews and surveys (#6, #7). I found no study where researchers observed lunchtime conversation.
- **Not found:** any study that tracks whether conflict moves to after school. Some blogs claim it does, but I couldn't trace those claims to data, so I left them out. The closest evidence is #4: phones were used less in school, but overall use on weekdays and weekends was unchanged.
- **Peer-review count:** sources 1–8 are peer-reviewed, which covers your requirement of 5. Sources 10 and 11 are working papers, and 9 is commentary.

## Questions the results raise

- If no study tracks conflict after school, what kind of evidence could stand in for that part of your position, and how far would it stretch?
- Several studies measure *how students feel* (belonging, loneliness, peer support) rather than *how much they talk*. Does "interaction" in your question mean one of those, or both?
