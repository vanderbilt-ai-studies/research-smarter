## Preliminary briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Achievement:** Local leaders and parents often say scores held steady or went up. A multi-state RAND team compared four-day districts with similar five-day districts. They found that students in four-day districts fell slightly further behind each year, and they estimate that after about eight years the gap could match pandemic learning losses ([The 74, 2023](https://www.the74million.org/article/students-in-4-day-a-week-schools-can-suffer-covid-level-learning-losses/)). The argument is partly about which comparison counts.
- **Teachers:** Districts often sell the schedule as a perk for teachers. An Oregon study of state employment data found teacher turnover *rose* 2.3 percentage points right after adoption, and teacher pay in those districts fell further behind over time ([FutureEd, 2024](https://www.future-ed.org/research-notes-does-a-four-day-school-week-improve-teacher-retention/)). I also saw summaries of newer Missouri and Texas studies (2025) that find little effect on retention, but only secondhand, so check those yourself before using them.
- **Families:** One side asks who watches kids on the day off and where they eat. The other points to families who like the longer weekend ([KCUR, 2025](https://www.kcur.org/2025-08-26/pass-or-fail-midwest-families-and-districts-are-learning-from-the-4-day-school-week)).

**Major voices**
- RAND researchers (Christopher Doss, Andrea Phillips, Rebecca Kilburn), who ran a six-state study
- Emily Morton (NWEA)
- Paul Thompson (Oregon State) and colleagues, on health and time in school
- Policy groups like the National Council on Teacher Quality (NCTQ) and the Show-Me Institute
- State legislatures, such as Missouri's, which in 2024 began requiring voter approval in larger districts

**Patterns**
- Most adopters are small, rural districts west of the Mississippi. Their main reasons are budgets, hiring, and keeping up with neighboring districts.
- Reported savings are small, about 0.4–2.5% of a budget in one older report ([Harvard GSE, 2018](https://www.gse.harvard.edu/ideas/usable-knowledge/18/06/four-day-school-week)).
- Studies based on interviews and perceptions tend to be positive. Studies of administrative data (state test-score or employment records) tend to be neutral or negative.
- Several health and safety studies show associations rather than proven causes. One example: an Oregon survey study found higher food insecurity and drug use after schools switched, but only 8 schools switched during the study ([Tomayko et al., *Journal of School Health*, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8595551)). A Colorado study linked the schedule to more juvenile property crime ([K-12 Dive, 2018](https://www.k12dive.com/news/study-4-day-school-week-may-lead-to-higher-crime-rates/520548)). Sources disagree on the size of that crime effect, so check the original paper.

**Open questions**
- Is it the number of days or the total hours that matters?
- Do effects differ between rural and suburban districts?
- What does the day off do to childcare and food access? Much of this is a stated concern, not measured.
- How can tiny districts tell whether their switch worked?
