## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Achievement.** Studies disagree. A 2015 Colorado study (Anderson & Walker) found math and reading scores *rose*. Later multi-state work (Morton, Thompson & Kuhfeld, 2024, six states) found students learned slightly less on average. Rural students did about as well as their five-day peers, but non-rural students lost ground.
- **Whether the cause is the schedule or the hours.** Thompson & Ward (2022, 12 states) found score declines only in schools with low total instructional time (about 30 hours a week).
- **Teachers.** Districts often switch to attract and keep teachers. One working paper found no improvement in Missouri (2025), and another found a slight recruiting gain at suburban Colorado schools (2024). Neither is peer-reviewed yet.
- **Life outside school.** In Colorado, Fischer & Argyle (2018) linked four-day weeks to about a 20% rise in juvenile offenses, mostly property crime. But an Oklahoma study (Morton, 2022) found *less* fighting and bullying inside school.

**Major voices**
- Economists Paul Thompson (Oregon State) and Emily Morton (NWEA) appear across many of the studies.
- RAND ran a large six-state study, *Can Four Equal Five?* (2021).
- The CALDER research center has published the teacher working papers.
- State health and education agencies have also weighed in, such as Oklahoma's 2017 health impact assessment.

**Patterns**
- **Mostly rural, mostly local.** Most studies are small and limited to one state or region, and most four-day schools are rural.
- **Popular with families.** Parents and students tend to like the schedule. The Hechinger Report says RAND found 85% of parents and 95% of students would choose to keep it.
- **Small savings.** Districts often cite money as a reason, but researchers estimate the savings at only about 1–2%.
- **Fast growth.** Thompson estimates more than 2,100 schools in 26 states now use the schedule. Texas alone went from 30 schools in 2020–21 to 506 in 2024–25.

**Open questions**
- What happens on the fifth day: childcare, parents' jobs, and access to school meals. Oklahoma's assessment called the evidence on food insecurity "mostly inconclusive."
- Effects in suburban and urban districts, and for students with disabilities.
- Whether any recruiting advantage disappears once neighboring districts switch too.
- What's driving the newest wave of adoption, and how states are regulating it.
