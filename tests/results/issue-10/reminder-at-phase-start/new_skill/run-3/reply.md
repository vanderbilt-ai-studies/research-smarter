## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Achievement.** The earliest study, from Colorado in 2015, found gains. Most later studies find small losses. The largest one so far, by Morton, Thompson and Kuhfeld (2023), used student-level data from several states. It found spring reading scores about 0.07 standard deviations lower, and slower growth over the school year in both math and reading. ([EdWorkingPaper 22-630](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai22-630))
- **Teachers.** Districts often adopt the schedule to recruit and keep teachers, but the results disagree by state:
  - Texas: turnover fell about 2.7 percentage points, yet more than 1 in 5 teachers still left each year. ([EdWeek, Jan 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/do-4-day-school-weeks-attract-and-retain-better-teachers-what-the-largest-study-yet-says/2026/01))
  - Oregon: turnover rose about 2 points right after the switch and was about 4 points higher five or more years later, while pay fell further behind five-day districts. ([EdWeek, Oct 2024](https://edweek.org/leadership/4-day-school-weeks-and-their-surprising-effect-on-teacher-turnover/2024/10))
- **Life outside school.** In rural Colorado, juvenile crime rose about 20% after four-day weeks were adopted, mostly property crime. ([Fischer & Argyle, 2018](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:ecoedu:v:64:y:2018:i:c:p:31-39))

**Major voices**
- RAND, whose 2021 report *Does Four Equal Five?* includes Emily Morton among its authors ([NWEA summary page](https://www.nwea.org/research/publication/does-four-equal-five-implementation-and-outcomes-of-the-four-day-school-week/))
- NWEA researchers (Morton, Kuhfeld) and Paul Thompson at Oregon State
- Economists of education such as Fischer, Argyle and Cade Lawson
- Education Week and The Hechinger Report, which summarize the research for a general audience

**Patterns**
- **Families love it.** In RAND's survey, about 85% of parents and 95% of students would choose to keep the four-day week. ([Hechinger, 2022](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-seven-new-studies-on-the-impact-of-a-four-day-school-week/))
- **Location matters.** Rural schools often show little or no achievement cost. Small-town and suburban schools show more.
- **Savings are small.** Hechinger reports about 1–2%, because teacher salaries don't change.
- **Instructional time may be the key.** Schools that make up the hours with longer days appear to lose less.
- **Not everyone is affected equally.** One study found Hispanic students harmed more than white students. The 2023 paper found larger effects for girls.

**Open questions**
- **Long-term effects.** Few studies follow students for long, and the 2023 authors say the losses may grow over time.
- **What happens on the fifth day.** Little is known about childcare, parents' jobs, or student wellbeing.
- **Why teacher results differ.** Texas and Oregon disagree, possibly because of pay or because many teachers still have to work on the fifth day.
- **A newer review.** A 2025 systematic review from the University of Oregon's HEDCO Institute appeared in my search, but I couldn't open it. Please don't rely on it until you've read it ([HEDCO page](https://hedcoinstitute.uoregon.edu/does-four-day-school-week-benefit-students-0)).
