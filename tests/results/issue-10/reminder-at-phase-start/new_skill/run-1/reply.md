## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Does it hurt learning?** The larger, newer studies lean negative. Morton, Thompson and Kuhfeld followed students in several states and found that four-day students grew less over the school year: about 0.06 standard deviations less in both math and reading. The effects were larger in non-rural schools and for girls ([EdWorkingPapers 22-630, 2023](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai22-630)). Older work is more mixed. Anderson and Walker (2015) found a *positive* link in Colorado, and Thompson and Ward (2022) found declines only in schools with low total instructional time ([Journalist's Resource roundup](https://journalistsresource.org/education/four-day-school-week-research/)).
- **Does it help teachers?** Districts often sell the four-day week as a way to recruit and keep teachers, and the evidence on that is split by state. The largest study so far covers every Texas teacher since 2006. It found turnover fell about 2.7 percentage points, but more than 1 in 5 teachers still left each year, and the incoming teachers weren't more experienced or better qualified ([Education Week, Jan. 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/do-4-day-school-weeks-attract-and-retain-better-teachers-what-the-largest-study-yet-says/2026/01)). The same article notes that studies in Colorado, Oregon and Missouri found no retention benefit, or a negative one.

**Major voices**
- **Paul Thompson** (an economist at Oregon State) and **Emily Morton** are behind much of the achievement research.
- Teacher-labor economists such as **Cade Lawson** (Georgia State) did the Texas study.
- Most of this work comes out as working papers (EdWorkingPapers, CALDER) before it reaches journals.

**Patterns**
- **Instructional time seems to matter more than the calendar.** Thompson reports that four-day students average about 1,150 instructional hours a year, compared with 1,235 for five-day students ([Oregon State, 2020](https://news.oregonstate.edu/news/four-day-school-weeks-becoming-more-common-mixed-results-study-finds)).
- **Context changes results.** About 90% of four-day schools are rural (same source), and rural and non-rural districts often show different effects.
- **Outcomes reach beyond test scores.** In rural Colorado, juvenile offenses rose about 20% after four-day weeks were adopted, mostly property crime ([Fischer & Argyle, *Economics of Education Review*, 2018](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecoedu/v64y2018icp31-39.html)). In Oklahoma, fighting and bullying fell (Morton, 2022, summarized in the Journalist's Resource roundup above).
- **Surveys of parents and staff are often favorable**, even where test-score studies are neutral or negative.

**Open questions**
- **Money.** Saving money is a common reason districts switch, but the savings figures I saw in search results looked small, roughly 1–3% of spending. I couldn't open the papers behind those figures, so treat that range as unconfirmed.
- **The fifth day.** What students do on the day off (tutoring, meals, childcare, or nothing) is barely studied. Thompson found budget-driven districts are less likely to offer anything on that day.
- **Long-term effects.** Morton and colleagues suggest the harms may grow over time.
