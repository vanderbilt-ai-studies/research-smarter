## Second briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**

- **Achievement: small harm or no harm?** A large RAND study found that achievement in four-day districts grew more slowly than in comparable five-day districts ([Kilburn et al., RAND, 2021](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA373-1.html)). In an essay summarizing that work, RAND authors say the yearly losses were small but added up, and that after eight years they could be about as large as the pandemic's effect ([The 74, 2023](https://www.the74million.org/article/students-in-4-day-a-week-schools-can-suffer-covid-level-learning-losses/); this is an opinion piece). Other studies find little or no harm. These include Oregon kindergartners followed to third grade, Oklahoma high schoolers' ACT scores, and an older Colorado study that found slight gains ([Journalist's Resource roundup, 2025](https://journalistsresource.org/education/four-day-school-week-research/)).
- **Teachers: perk or not?** In RAND's interviews, teachers described the schedule as a "job perk." Studies of actual staffing data tell a different story. In Texas, turnover dropped about 2.7 percentage points, but more than 1 in 5 teachers still left every year, and the shorter week didn't attract stronger teachers ([Education Week, Jan. 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/do-4-day-school-weeks-attract-and-retain-better-teachers-what-the-largest-study-yet-says/2026/01)). In Oregon, turnover was about 4 points *higher* after five or more years, and pay fell further behind five-day districts ([Education Week, Oct. 2024](https://edweek.org/leadership/4-day-school-weeks-and-their-surprising-effect-on-teacher-turnover/2024/10)).
- **The fifth day: family time or unsupervised time?** RAND found that families liked the extra time together and that elementary students got more sleep on school nights. A rural Colorado study found juvenile crime rose about 20% after districts adopted the schedule, mostly property crime ([Fischer & Argyle, *Economics of Education Review*, 2018](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:ecoedu:v:64:y:2018:i:c:p:31-39), peer-reviewed).

**Major voices**

- **Emily Morton and Paul Thompson** have written much of the quantitative achievement research.
- **RAND** (Kilburn, Phillips, Doss and colleagues) produced the biggest mixed-methods study, combining test data with surveys and interviews.
- **Teacher-labor economists** wrote the Texas and Oregon studies.

**Patterns**

- **Context matters.** Morton, Thompson & Kuhfeld (2024) found that rural students did about as well as before, while non-rural students lost ground in reading and math. I only saw this study described in the Journalist's Resource roundup and didn't open the paper.
- **So does instructional time.** In a 12-state study, schools that cut weekly hours the most saw small score drops. Schools that kept their hours up showed no change (Thompson & Ward, 2022, also from the roundup).
- **Satisfaction is high, measured outcomes less so.** Parents, students, and staff mostly like the schedule. The test, crime, and turnover data are much more mixed.

**Open questions**

- Food insecurity and lost childcare come up again and again as concerns, but there's almost no data on them.
- Most of the studies are about rural Western and Midwestern districts. It's unclear whether their results apply elsewhere.
