## Second briefing: four-day weeks, students and teachers

**Key debates**

- **Test scores.** This is the most contested area, and the answer seems to depend on how much school time a district keeps.
  - RAND's 2021 study (Kilburn, Phillips, Gomez, Mariano, Doss, Troxel, Morton, Estes) found that over time, students on four-day weeks had lower English and math scores than students on five-day weeks. ([NWEA summary page](https://www.nwea.org/research/publication/does-four-equal-five-implementation-and-outcomes-of-the-four-day-school-week/))
  - Three of the RAND researchers later wrote in The 74 that the losses are small each year but add up. They estimate that after about eight years, the losses could roughly equal pandemic-era learning loss. ([The 74, April 2023](https://www.the74million.org/article/students-in-4-day-a-week-schools-can-suffer-covid-level-learning-losses/); note this is an opinion essay by the researchers, not the study itself.)
  - Education Week reports two findings that point the other way. A 2024 Missouri state education department study found no significant difference in achievement. An Oregon State study found lower scores overall, but no significant difference in districts that kept high or medium instructional time. ([Education Week explainer, April 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04))
- **Teachers.** Leaders believe the four-day week helps, but the data are mixed.
  - In RAND's interviews, district leaders said the schedule helps them recruit teachers.
  - A Missouri working paper co-authored by Andrew Camp (Brown University) covered the 178 Missouri districts that switched. It found no lasting improvement in recruiting or keeping teachers. The estimated effect was 0.6 job exits per 100 teachers, which is not statistically significant. ([The 74, June 2025](https://www.the74million.org/article/k-12-leaders-love-the-four-day-school-week-but-a-new-study-shows-that-it-doesnt-do-what-they-hope/))
  - Education Week also describes two studies that disagree with each other:
    - A Texas study by Cade Lawson (Georgia State University) found teacher turnover fell about 2.7 percentage points.
    - A 2023 report on the Brighton district near Denver found teachers became *less* likely to return.

    I saw both only through Education Week's summary, not the studies themselves.

**Patterns**

- **Effects beyond test scores.** Emily Morton's study of Oklahoma high schools found bullying fell about 39% and fights about 31% after the switch. It found no detectable change in ACT scores or attendance. ([NWEA page for the article](https://www.nwea.org/research/publication/effects-of-4-day-school-weeks-on-older-adolescents-examining-impacts-of-the-schedule-on-academic-achievement-attendance-and-behavior-in-high-school/), published in *Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis*, 2022. It's peer-reviewed, which matters for your requirement. Its DOI, 10.3102/01623737221097420, resolves to the publisher's page, but that page blocked me, so I worked from the summary.)
- **Families like it.** RAND found high satisfaction among families. Per Education Week, nearly three-quarters of elementary parents were very satisfied.
- **Instructional time may be the hidden variable.** Paul Thompson's national study of 24 states found that four-day schools averaged 1,150 instructional hours a year, compared with 1,235 in five-day schools. It also found that districts switching to save money were less likely to offer anything on the fifth day, such as tutoring or meals. ([Oregon State news release](https://news.oregonstate.edu/news/four-day-school-weeks-becoming-more-common-mixed-results-study-finds); the journal article's DOI page blocked me.)
- **Small budget savings.** An Education Commission of the States study cited by Education Week found savings of only about 0.4% to 2.5% of total budgets.

**Open questions**

- Does a four-day week itself hurt learning, or only a four-day week that cuts total hours?
- Are teachers better off even when they don't stay longer? For example, are they less burned out?
- Who carries the cost of the fifth day, families or students?

Most of these studies cover small, rural districts in a handful of states (Oklahoma, Missouri, Oregon, Colorado, Idaho). Whether they apply to your cousin's district depends on what that district looks like.
