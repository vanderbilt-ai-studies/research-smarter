## Second briefing: four-day school weeks

### Key debates
- **Learning: is the loss real and does it matter?** Most studies with good comparison groups find students in four-day districts score a bit lower. People disagree about whether a gap that size outweighs what families gain.
- **Teachers: does it work as a recruiting perk?** Districts often say the schedule helps them hire and keep teachers. The employment data mostly don't back that up.
- **What happens on the fifth day?** Families get time together. Some kids also get less supervision, and there's evidence that has costs.

### What the research shows
- **Achievement:** A RAND study of 36 districts in Idaho, New Mexico, and Oklahoma found students still improved, but more slowly than they would have on a five-day week. The gap grew over time. Eight years after the switch, scores were about 0.14–0.23 standard deviations lower. The four-day districts had longer days but 25 fewer school days, which added up to about **58 fewer hours of instruction a year**.
- **Size of the effect across states:** Morton, Thompson, and Kuhfeld used test data from students in several states and found small negative effects, about −0.06 to −0.07 standard deviations. The effects were **larger in non-rural schools and for female students**.
- **High school:** In Oregon, students who started high school on a four-day week had lower 11th-grade math scores, more chronic absence, and lower on-time graduation rates. Again, the effects were concentrated in non-rural schools.
- **Teachers:** A Missouri study covered 178 districts that switched, using data from 2009 to 2024. It found **no evidence** that the schedule reduced teacher turnover or improved hiring, even six or more years later. Almost every leader the researchers interviewed believed it did both.
- **Families:** In the RAND survey, 69% of elementary parents said they would *definitely* choose the four-day week again.
- **Youth crime:** In rural Colorado, juvenile offenses rose about 20% after districts switched, mostly property crime.
- **Cost:** Saving money was the most common reason districts gave for switching, but actual savings are estimated at under 5%.

### Patterns
1. **Opinion and data point different ways.** Parents, students, and teachers like the schedule, and many believe it helps. The measured results are neutral to slightly negative.
2. **Total instructional time seems to drive the learning effect.** Researchers like Paul Thompson argue that districts which keep total hours close to a five-day schedule see smaller losses.
3. **Where the district is matters.** Rural and non-rural districts don't get the same results.

### Open questions
- Which students lose most from the extra day off, for example families who can't arrange childcare or kids who rely on school meals? I didn't find solid studies on this yet.
- Is "teacher wellbeing" the same as "teacher retention"? The Missouri researchers suggest teachers may enjoy the schedule without it changing whether they stay.
