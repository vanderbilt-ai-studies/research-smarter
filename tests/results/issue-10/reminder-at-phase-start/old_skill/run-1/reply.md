## Briefing: four-day weeks, students and teachers

**Key debates**
- **Achievement.** RAND's 2021 study of 36 districts in Idaho, New Mexico and Oklahoma found that test scores in four-day districts grew more slowly than in comparable five-day districts. Other findings are mixed. An Oregon State study (2022) found no significant gap where districts kept high or medium total instructional time. Missouri's education department (2024) found no difference.
- **Teachers.** Districts often sell the schedule as a recruiting perk, but the outcome data disagree with each other:
  - Texas, the largest study so far (Lawson et al., 17 years of data): turnover fell about 2.7 percentage points, but more than 1 in 5 teachers still left each year, and teacher quality didn't improve.
  - Oregon (Ainsworth, Penner & Liu, 2024): turnover *rose* 2.3 points right after adoption, then up to 5 points several years later, and salaries fell further behind.
- **Life outside the classroom.** In rural Colorado, juvenile crime rose about 20% after districts adopted the schedule, mostly property crime (Fischer & Argyle, 2018). An Oklahoma study of 411 districts instead found bullying fell about 39% and fights about 31%. I saw that one only through EdWeek's summary, not the paper itself.

**Major voices:** RAND; economists such as Paul Thompson (Oregon State), Emily Morton, Cade Lawson (Georgia State) and Emily Penner (UC Irvine); the Annenberg Institute's EdWorkingPapers series; and the Education Commission of the States on budgets.

**Patterns**
- Most four-day districts are rural and switch to save money. Savings run about 0.4–2.5% of the budget, and they're often eaten up by new programs for the off day.
- Total instructional time seems to matter more than the number of days.
- Satisfaction and outcomes don't match. In RAND's study, about three-quarters of elementary parents were very satisfied, and two-thirds of educators in an EdWeek survey said a four-day week would make them more willing to take a job. The measured effects are weaker than that.
- Much of the health data is self-reported. An Oregon study (Tomayko et al., 2021) linked the switch to more food insecurity and drug use. Its authors call the effects small, and their switching estimates rest on only 8 schools.

**Open questions:** Who exactly is hurt or helped? Low-income families and families who need childcare are the obvious groups to ask about. What happens over the long term? And do results from one state carry over to another?

Treat the Fischer & Argyle 20% crime figure as fair game: ask me how I know it, and I'll show you what it rests on.
