**Phase 2 of 5 · Pre-research stress test**

Welcome back. Your log has you in Phase 2 on the four-day week question, just after the second briefing, about to state a position. You've now stated it: four-day weeks help teachers more than students, because teachers gain a day to plan and rest and kids lose class time. That's a good working thesis for an argumentative essay. It takes a side, and it compares two groups instead of giving one verdict for everyone. I ran two quick searches to test both halves of it.

**"Kids lose class time."** This is only partly true. Most four-day districts make the remaining days longer. In RAND's three-state study, four-day schools ran about 7 hours 55 minutes a day versus 7 hours 6 minutes, with about 25 fewer days a year ([RAND brief](https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/research_briefs/RBA300/RBA373-1/RAND_RBA373-1.pdf); [full report](https://www.nwea.org/research/publication/does-four-equal-five-implementation-and-outcomes-of-the-four-day-school-week/)). RAND's own summaries don't fully agree on whether total yearly time ends up lower or about the same. A follow-up across 12 states found math scores dropped only in four-day districts with low total time, not in districts that kept time up ([EdWeek explainer, 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04)). So the evidence points less at "four days" and more at how many hours a district keeps.

**"Teachers get a day to plan and rest."** The evidence here is more mixed than you might expect:
- Many districts still require teachers to come in on the fifth day for training ([EdWeek, Jan 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/do-4-day-school-weeks-attract-and-retain-better-teachers-what-the-largest-study-yet-says/2026/01)).
- Retention results go both ways. In Oregon, teacher turnover rose after the switch ([FutureEd](https://www.future-ed.org/research-notes-does-a-four-day-school-week-improve-teacher-retention/)). In Missouri, there was no lasting benefit ([EdSource](https://edsource.org/updates/study-four-day-school-week-hardly-improves-teacher-retention)).
- Some Arkansas and Texas work finds modest gains ([Univ. of Arkansas working paper](https://edre.uark.edu/_resources/pdf/edrewp2025-09.pdf)).
- The benefit for hiring seems biggest when a district is the only one nearby on the shorter schedule ([EdWeek, 2025](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/4-day-school-weeks-may-have-diminishing-returns-for-teacher-recruitment/2025/07)).

A thoughtful opponent could argue the teacher half and the student half are really the same question. If the extra day doesn't leave teachers better rested or more likely to stay, what's left of the teacher benefit? And if districts keep total hours up, what's left of the student cost?

Given that total hours seem to matter more than the number of days, how would you need to adjust your thesis so it still holds?
