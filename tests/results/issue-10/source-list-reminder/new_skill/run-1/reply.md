**Peer-reviewed studies**

1. Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211
   1,227 students aged 12–15 at 30 English schools. Students at restrictive schools used phones and social media less during the school day, but total weekday and weekend use was the same, and wellbeing didn't differ. *I read the abstract only.*

2. King, D. L., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., & Wade, T. (2024). "Phones off while school's on": Evaluating problematic phone use and the social, wellbeing, and academic effects of banning phones in schools. *Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 13*(4), 913–922. https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.2024.00058
   About 1,250 students in grades 7–12 at five South Australian schools, surveyed about a month apart. Bullying fell in both ban and no-ban schools, with no difference between them, and school belonging didn't change. The authors say they didn't measure face-to-face communication in detail. *I read the full text.*

3. Bar, E., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., Wade, T. D., & King, D. L. (2025). Student perspectives on banning mobile phones in South Australian secondary schools: A large-scale qualitative analysis. *Computers in Human Behavior, 167*, 108603. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108603
   About 7,000 open-ended answers from over 1,500 students. According to the university's news release, students described more in-person interaction and less conflict. *I couldn't read the paper itself, only the [Flinders news release](https://news.flinders.edu.au/blog/2025/09/18/benefits-of-school-phone-bans/), and that release mixes up details between this study and the next one.*

4. Baggio, S., Wade, T., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Billieux, J., Starcevic, V., Quinney, B., & King, D. L. (2025). Psychological consequences of school mobile phone bans: Emulated trial of a natural experiment in South Australia. *Computers in Human Behavior, 172*, 108767. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108767
   The news release says it found modest drops in psychological distress after the ban. *I couldn't open the abstract or the paper, so treat this description as unconfirmed.*

5. Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
   1,398 students (average age 16) at 24 schools, comparing classroom-only bans with whole-school bans. Bullying and wellbeing didn't differ. Whole-school bans went with weaker student–teacher connection, and, for girls, a lower sense of belonging. *I read the abstract only.*

6. Beneito, P., & Vicente-Chirivella, Ó. (2022). Banning mobile phones in schools: Evidence from regional-level policies in Spain. *Applied Economic Analysis, 30*(90), 153–175. https://doi.org/10.1108/AEA-05-2021-0112
   Compares two Spanish regions that banned phones in 2015 with regions that didn't, using bullying cases reported to police. Reported bullying among 15–17-year-olds fell by about 9.5–18%. The authors call this "suggestive" evidence, and their data only counts cases families reported to police. *I read most of the full text.*

7. Abrahamsson, S. (2026). Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health. *Journal of Human Resources*. Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.0224-13403R2
   Uses Norwegian middle-school administrative data. It finds no overall effect on education or mental health, but bullying fell for both boys and girls, and girls gained the most. *I read the abstract only. The journal page lists it in volume 61, issue 5, but I couldn't confirm the page numbers.*

**Reviews (peer-reviewed)**

8. Campbell, M., Edwards, E., Pennell, D., Poed, S., Lister, V., Gillett-Swan, J., Kelly, A., Zec, D., & Nguyen, T.-A. (2024). Evidence for and against banning mobile phones in schools: A scoping review. *Journal of Psychologists and Counsellors in Schools, 34*(3), 242–265. https://doi.org/10.1177/20556365241270394
   Reviews 22 studies covering learning, mental health and cyberbullying. It found no randomized trials, and the studies were too different from each other to reconcile easily. *I read the abstract only, via the [QUT repository](https://eprints.qut.edu.au/251231/).*

9. Böttger, T., & Zierer, K. (2024). To ban or not to ban? A rapid review on the impact of smartphone bans in schools on social well-being and academic performance. *Education Sciences, 14*(8), 906. https://doi.org/10.3390/educsci14080906
   Combines five studies. It finds a small positive effect (d = 0.16) that is stronger for social wellbeing than for grades, and says bans work best alongside media-literacy teaching. *I read the abstract only, from a citation database, because the journal page blocked me.*

**Not peer-reviewed** (useful for leads, but they don't count toward your five)

10. Saffer, H. (2026). *Youth mental health and school smartphone bans: Early evidence* (NBER Working Paper No. 35181). National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w35181
    Uses U.S. national survey data from the first few states with bans. It finds no clear drop in screen time and no clear improvement in wellbeing so far, and the author calls the results preliminary.

11. Martin, J. D., & Bakour, C. (2025, June 21). Survey of 1,500 kids suggests school phone bans have important but limited effects. *The 74*. https://www.the74million.org/article/survey-of-1500-kids-suggests-school-phone-bans-have-important-but-limited-effects/
    A survey of 11–13-year-olds in Florida run by two university researchers. Students at schools with stricter phone limits reported more cyberbullying. The authors stress this is a correlation and don't claim the bans caused it.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plenty:** bullying and wellbeing outcomes, though the results point in different directions.
- **Thin:** studies that directly measure face-to-face conversation. Most evidence on it comes from students' own descriptions (Bar et al.), and King et al. say outright that they didn't measure it.
- **Not found:** any study that tracks whether conflict *moves* to after school. The closest are Goodyear et al. (phone use drops during school but total use stays the same) and the Florida survey on cyberbullying.
- **Age mismatch:** many samples are middle-school age. Vanluydt et al. and King et al. are the closest to high school.

## Questions these results raise

- The studies define "conflict" in different ways: police reports, self-reported bullying, cyberbullying. Your position may need a choice about which kind you mean.
- "Total phone use stays the same" isn't the same claim as "conflict moves after school." The gap between those two ideas is worth noticing.
