## Sources

**Peer-reviewed journal articles**

1. Vereecke, J., Vanherle, R., Nassen, L.-M., Boen, H., Eggermont, S., Beullens, K., Vandenbosch, L., & Schreurs, L. (2026). More than disconnecting? School smartphone bans and adolescents' social wellbeing. *Frontiers in Developmental Psychology, 4*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fdpys.2026.1796471
   One Flemish secondary school moved from a loose restriction to a total ban, and 960 students were surveyed eight times over a school year, with no control school. Friend support stayed stable, social loneliness rose slightly over time, cyberbullying victimization didn't change, and perpetration (rare to begin with) dipped briefly. The study did not measure face-to-face interaction directly. (The page didn't show an article number, so get that from the published version.)

2. Goodyear, V., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe*, Article 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211 (abstract read via the [University of Birmingham repository](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/school-phone-policies-and-their-association-with-mental-wellbeing/))
   This compares 1,227 students aged 12–15 at 20 restrictive and 10 permissive English schools. Students at restrictive schools used phones and social media less during school hours. There was no difference in total weekday or weekend use and no difference in mental wellbeing.

3. Campbell, M., Edwards, E., Pennell, D., Poed, S., Lister, V., Gillett-Swan, J., Kelly, A., Zec, D., & Nguyen, T.-A. (2024). Evidence for and against banning mobile phones in schools: A scoping review. *Journal of Psychologists and Counsellors in Schools, 34*(3), 242–265. https://doi.org/10.1177/20556365241270394 (abstract read via [QUT ePrints](https://eprints.qut.edu.au/251231/))
   This review covers 22 studies on academic outcomes, wellbeing, and cyberbullying. It found no randomized trials, and the studies define "ban" in different ways. The abstract doesn't give the cyberbullying results, so you'll need the full text for those.

4. Bravo, L., & Elupula, K. (2025). The effect of a mobile phone ban on perceived academic and social life: A qualitative analysis. *Open Journal of Social Sciences, 13*(10), 83–96. https://doi.org/10.4236/jss.2025.1310005
   This surveyed 209 students at one private high school in Charlotte, NC, about three months into a zero-access policy. 46.5% reported more in-person conversation during free time, 43.5% no change, and about 10% less. Some students who relied on group chats felt more left out. Its publisher, Scientific Research Publishing, has been criticized for light peer review, so check with your teacher or a librarian whether it counts toward your five.

**Working papers (not yet peer-reviewed)**

8. Allcott, H., Baron, E. J., Dee, T., Duckworth, A. L., Gentzkow, M., & Jacob, B. (2026). *The effects of school phone bans: National evidence from lockable pouches* (NBER Working Paper No. 35132). National Bureau of Economic Research. https://www.nber.org/papers/w35132
   This studies US schools that adopted Yondr-style pouches. Disciplinary incidents rose in year one and then faded. Well-being dipped in year one and was above its pre-ban level by year three. There was no clear change in perceived online bullying. In [Stanford's news coverage](https://humsci.stanford.edu/feature/national-study-school-cellphone-bans-shows-benefits-are-not-immediate), the authors suggest students may interact more in person, but they say that wasn't measured.

9. Abrahamsson, S. (2024). *Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health* (Discussion Paper No. 1/2024). Norwegian School of Economics. https://swopec.hhs.se/nhheco/abs/nhheco2024_001.htm
   This uses Norwegian administrative data on middle schools. The abstract reports that bullying went down for both boys and girls after bans, along with mental-health and grade effects for girls. I couldn't confirm whether it has since been published in a journal.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Fairly plentiful:** large studies of wellbeing and mental health, and several studies that measure bullying or cyberbullying in some form.
- **Thin:** direct measures of face-to-face interaction. Only Bravo & Elupula asked about it, and that was self-report at one school. The pouch study and the Flemish study discuss it as a possibility without measuring it.
- **Not found:** any study that measures *when or where* conflict happens, for example after school versus during the day. The bullying measures I saw were overall or "perceived online bullying" rather than split by time of day. Also, almost all the research is on mixed middle and high school samples, not high school alone.

## Questions the results raise

- The studies measure very different things: "social wellbeing," "connectedness," "perceived cyberbullying," and "bullying." Those may not mean the same thing as "interaction" and "conflict" in your position.
- The time frames differ (three months, one school year, three years). The pouch study's year-one results look different from its year-three results.
- Some studies compare countries and ban types (full-day vs. lessons-only), and that could matter for what you're claiming about high schools.
