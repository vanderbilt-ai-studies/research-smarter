## Sources

**Peer-reviewed studies**

- King, D. L., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., & Wade, T. (2024). "Phones off while school's on": Evaluating problematic phone use and the social, wellbeing, and academic effects of banning phones in schools. *Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 13*(4), 913–922. https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.2024.00058 (free full text: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11737419/))
  A preregistered comparison of ban and no-ban schools in South Australia, grades 7–12, about 1,280 students, with a one-month follow-up. Bullying fell in both groups and school belonging didn't differ between them. The authors say they didn't measure face-to-face communication.

- Bar, E., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., Wade, T. D., & King, D. L. (2025). Student perspectives on banning mobile phones in South Australian secondary schools: A large-scale qualitative analysis. *Computers in Human Behavior, 167*, 108603. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108603
  About 7,000 written answers from more than 1,500 students. Students described more in-person contact and better conversations, and some described fewer fights and less cyberbullying. *I couldn't open the full text. This summary comes from [Flinders University's news page](https://news.flinders.edu.au/blog/2025/09/18/benefits-of-school-phone-bans/), and that page gives a wrong DOI, so use the one above.*

- Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
  Compares classroom-only bans with whole-school bans across 24 Dutch schools (1,398 students). Bullying didn't differ between the two. Whole-school bans were linked to weaker student–teacher connectedness and, for girls, a lower sense of belonging. *Abstract only.*

- Goodyear, V., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., et al. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe*, 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211 (record: [University of Birmingham](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/school-phone-policies-and-their-association-with-mental-wellbeing/))
  1,227 students aged 12–15 at 30 English schools. Students in restrictive schools used phones about 40 minutes less during the school day, but total weekday and weekend use was the same, because they made it up outside school. Bullying and face-to-face interaction weren't measured. *Abstract and record only.*

- Beneito, P., & Vicente-Chirivella, Ó. (2022). Banning mobile phones in schools: Evidence from regional-level policies in Spain. *Applied Economic Analysis, 30*(90), 153–175. https://doi.org/10.1108/AEA-05-2021-0112 (open access)
  Compares two Spanish regions that banned phones in 2015 with regions that didn't. Officially reported bullying fell about 15–18% for ages 12–14 and about 9.5–18% for ages 15–17. These are mostly serious cases that families reported to police. Cyberbullying wasn't measured separately.

**Working papers and reports (not peer-reviewed, as far as I could tell)**

- Allcott, H., Baron, E. J., Dee, T., Duckworth, A. L., Gentzkow, M., & Jacob, B. (2026). *The effects of school phone bans: National evidence from lockable pouches* (NBER Working Paper No. 35132). https://doi.org/10.3386/w35132
  A study of over 43,000 U.S. middle and high schools. Discipline incidents rose in year one, then faded, and well-being turned positive by later years. There was no clear change in perceived online bullying. Social interaction wasn't measured; one author speculated about it in [Stanford's write-up](https://ed.stanford.edu/news/national-study-school-cell-phone-bans-shows-benefits-are-not-immediate).

- Reynolds, M., Esfandiari, M., & O'Higgins Norman, J. (2025). *Restriction or resilience? Smartphone bans in schools: A qualitative study on the experiences of students and teachers*. DCU Anti-Bullying Centre. https://www.dcu.ie/antibullyingcentre/restriction-or-resilience
  Focus groups with 66 Irish students aged 10–18. Students said cyberbullying mostly happens outside school, and the authors suggest bans may push it to after hours. These are students' perceptions, not measured rates.

- Abrahamsson, S. (2024). *Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health* [Working paper]. NHH Norwegian School of Economics.
  A widely cited Norwegian study finding that bans reduced bullying, with larger effects for girls. *I found it through [NHH's summary page](https://nhh.no/en/research/impact-cases/smartphones-should-be-out-of-the-classrooms/), but the paper's own page wouldn't load, so I haven't seen its data. Check it before relying on it. It also studied middle schools.*

## What I found and didn't find

- **Plenty:** studies on bullying, well-being, and total phone use.
- **Thin:** direct measures of face-to-face talk. No study I found observed or counted in-person interaction. The evidence comes from what students said (Bar et al.) or what researchers guessed (Stanford).
- **Not found:** any study that tracked *when* cyberbullying happens (during school versus after) before and after a ban. The DCU report raises the idea; nobody I found tested it.
- **Age gap:** much of the data covers ages 12–15 or middle school. Results for high school specifically are scarcer.

## Questions the results raise

- If students make up their phone time after school (Goodyear), is that the same thing as conflict moving after school, or a separate thing?
- The Dutch study measured connectedness with teachers and belonging. Does "interact in person" in your question include those, or only peer-to-peer talk?
