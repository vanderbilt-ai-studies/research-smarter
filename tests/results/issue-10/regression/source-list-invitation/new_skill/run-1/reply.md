## Sources

**Peer-reviewed studies**

1. Bar, E., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., Wade, T. D., & King, D. L. (2025). Student perspectives on banning mobile phones in South Australian secondary schools: A large-scale qualitative analysis. *Computers in Human Behavior, 167*, 108603. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108603
   Analyses 7,188 written survey answers from 1,549 students at five South Australian schools. Students described more face-to-face interaction and better conversations. They also described trouble managing emotional distress without their phones, and some wanted to be taught responsible phone use instead of a ban. *I read summaries and an excerpt, not the full paper. The DOI resolves to the publisher's page for this article.*

2. King, D. L., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., & Wade, T. (2024). "Phones off while school's on": Evaluating problematic phone use and the social, wellbeing, and academic effects of banning phones in schools. *Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 13*(4), 913–922. https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.2024.00058 (free full text: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11737419/))
   A preregistered natural experiment with about 1,280 students in grades 7–12 at 5 schools, measured before the ban and one month after. Bullying fell in both the ban and no-ban groups, and the ban did not change that trend. Sense of school belonging didn't change. The authors say they didn't measure face-to-face communication.

3. Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
   Compares 1,398 students (average age 16.2) at 24 Dutch schools with either classroom-only bans or whole-school bans. The two ban types showed no difference in bullying or well-being. Whole-school bans were linked to weaker connection between students and teachers, and to a weaker sense of belonging among girls. *Paywalled. I worked from the abstract on Utrecht University's [repository page](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/disconnect-to-reconnect-how-variations-between-types-of-smartphon/).*

4. Goodyear, V., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe*, 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211
   Compares 1,227 students aged 12–15 at 30 English schools with strict or relaxed phone policies. Students at strict schools used their phones about 30 minutes less during the school day but made up the time outside school, so their total screen time was about the same. Bullying and face-to-face interaction were not measured. *I worked from summaries, including the commentary in item 7.*

5. Beneito, P., & Vicente-Chirivella, Ó. (2022). Banning mobile phones in schools: Evidence from regional-level policies in Spain. *Applied Economic Analysis, 30*(90), 153–175. https://doi.org/10.1108/AEA-05-2021-0112
   Uses police-reported bullying cases from 2012 to 2017 in two Spanish regions that banned phones in 2015. Bullying fell by roughly 9.5–18% among 12- to 17-year-olds. The authors call the results "suggestive," and only bullying that families reported to police is counted.

6. Abrahamsson, S. (2026). Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health. *Journal of Human Resources*. https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.0224-13403R2
   Uses Norwegian national records on middle schools. The 2024 working-paper abstract says bullying went down for both boys and girls after bans. *The journal version is paywalled, so I could only read the [working paper's abstract](https://swopec.hhs.se/nhheco/abs/nhheco2024_001.htm). The effect sizes I saw came from secondhand summaries, so I've left them out. Check the published version.*

**Commentary**

7. Weiss, H. A., & Bonell, C. (2025). Smartphone use and mental health: Going beyond school restriction policies. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, 101237. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101237 ([free text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850730/))
   A commentary on the SMART Schools study (item 4), not new research. It points out that the study's design can't show cause and effect and that only 10% of the schools invited took part. It calls for future studies to measure bullying and social connectedness.

**Research report (not peer-reviewed)**

8. Reynolds, M., Esfandiari, M., & O'Higgins Norman, J. (2025). *Restriction or resilience? Smartphone bans in schools: A qualitative study on the experiences of students and teachers*. DCU Anti-Bullying Centre. https://www.dcu.ie/antibullyingcentre/restriction-or-resilience
   Reviews earlier research and adds focus groups with 66 Irish students aged 10–18. The students said bans move cyberbullying to after school instead of stopping it. The authors note that a lot of cyberbullying already happens outside school hours.

## What I found and didn't find

- **No study I found directly measured face-to-face talk.** The evidence for more in-person interaction is what students and teachers report (Bar et al.), not observed or timed conversation.
- **No study tracked conflict after school.** The "conflict moves after school" idea appears only as student perceptions in the DCU focus groups. On bullying, the large data studies split: the Spanish and Norwegian studies report decreases, while the Australian and Dutch studies show no ban effect.
- **There's very little US high school research.** The studies come from Australia, England, the Netherlands, Spain, Norway, and Ireland, and several focus on younger students (Norway on middle schools, England on ages 12–15).
- **Almost no long-term studies.** King et al. followed students for only one month after the ban.

## Questions the results raise

- If bans are linked to *weaker* student–teacher connectedness (Vanluydt), what does "interaction" in your question include? Does it cover students and adults, or only students with each other?
- The Goodyear finding that students make up their screen time after school is about phone *use*, not conflict. How would you tell "conflict moved after school" apart from "phone use moved after school"?
