3. Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Michail, M., Patterson, P., Sitch, A., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2026). How school phone policies influence adolescent phone use and wellbeing (SMART Schools): A qualitative comparative case study. *Social Science & Medicine, 398*, 119094. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2026.119094
   *The DOI page didn't load. I read the [university's summary](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/news/2026/school-phone-bans-or-not-digital-lives-of-students-affect-the-school-day).* It's based on 40 focus groups with 177 students, staff and parents at 7 schools. Restrictive schools had more in-person interaction. Conflicts that started in group chats outside school sometimes became face-to-face confrontations at school. Some students made up lost phone time by cutting into sleep, exercise and homework.

4. King, D. L., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., & Wade, T. (2024).    *I read it in full at [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11737419/); the DOI page itself was blocked.* It compares five South Australian schools (Grades 7–12) about one month after a ban. Bullying fell in both ban and no-ban schools. School belonging didn't change. The authors say they "lacked detailed measurement of students' social dynamics."

5. Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
   *The DOI page required a login. I read the abstract at [Utrecht University's portal](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/disconnect-to-reconnect-how-variations-between-types-of-smartphon/).* It compares full bans and classroom-only bans across 24 schools. Well-being and bullying didn't differ between the two. Full bans were linked to weaker student–teacher connection and, for girls, a weaker sense of belonging at school.

**Working papers (not peer-reviewed)**

6. Allcott, H., Baron, E. J., Dee, T., Duckworth, A., Gentzkow, M., & Jacob, B. (2026). *The effects of school phone bans: National evidence from lockable pouches* (NBER Working Paper No. 35132). National Bureau of Economic Research. https://www.nber.org/papers/w35132
   It covers U.S. schools nationally. In the first year of pouch bans, discipline incidents rose and well-being may have dipped. Later the discipline effect faded and well-being looked better. There was no clear change in perceived online bullying. A co-author speculated that more in-person interaction may explain the later gains, but the study doesn't measure that.

7. Abrahamsson, S. (2024). *Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health* (Discussion Paper No. 1/2024). Norwegian School of Economics. https://swopec.hhs.se/nhheco/abs/nhheco2024_001.htm
   It covers Norwegian middle schools. Bullying fell for both boys and girls after bans, and girls' grades improved. One social media post claims it was later published in a journal; I couldn't confirm that.

**News report**

8. DutchNews. (2024, May 23). *School ban on phones results in concentration and conversation*. https://www.dutchnews.nl/2024/05/school-ban-on-phones-results-in-concentration-and-conversation
   It reports a Radboud University survey at two Nijmegen schools, three months into a ban. Students talked with each other more. Online bullying fell, but in-person bullying rose. Students' rating of the ban fell from 6.8 to 4.8 out of 10.

## What I found and didn't find

- **Hardly any study directly measures face-to-face interaction.** The evidence on "more talking" comes from self-reports, focus groups and principal surveys.
- **Bullying evidence is mixed.** Some studies show declines, some show no difference, and one shows bullying moving into in-person settings.
- **I found no study that tests whether conflict shifts to after school.** The closest are the 2026 Goodyear focus groups and the evidence that students make up phone time outside school.
- **I found nothing on the quality of conversation.** That leaves your "bored vs. connecting" counterargument untested either way.
- **Most samples are non-U.S. and include younger students** (ages 12–15, or middle school), not specifically high schoolers.

## Questions these results raise

- How "interaction" is defined when it's only ever self-reported
- Which time frame your claims are about, given the Allcott study's pattern of worse-then-better
- Whether conflict moves both ways: out of school, and also into it
- Whether a full ban and a classroom-only ban count as the same thing for your argument
