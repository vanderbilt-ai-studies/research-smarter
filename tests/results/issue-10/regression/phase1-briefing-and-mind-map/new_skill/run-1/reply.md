## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Does achievement suffer?** Findings disagree. RAND's 2021 study of districts in Idaho, New Mexico and Oklahoma found four-day students fell behind five-day students in ELA and math over time ([Kilburn et al., RAND](https://www.nwea.org/research/publication/does-four-equal-five-implementation-and-outcomes-of-the-four-day-school-week/)). An Oregon study of early elementary students found only small differences ([Thompson et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9979633)).
- **Does it help schools keep teachers?** Most Missouri leaders who were interviewed believed it did. A study of 178 Missouri districts found no lasting effect on recruiting or keeping teachers ([The 74, 2025](https://www.the74million.org/article/k-12-leaders-love-the-four-day-school-week-but-a-new-study-shows-that-it-doesnt-do-what-they-hope/)).
- **Does it save money?** Savings are usually about 0.4–2.5% of a district's budget, and off-day programs can cancel them out ([Education Week, 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04)).

**Major voices**
- RAND Corporation and NWEA researchers, including Emily Morton.
- Paul Thompson at Oregon State, who wrote several of the achievement and health studies.
- Economists who study effects outside school, such as Fischer and Argyle on youth crime in Colorado ([Chalkbeat](https://chalkbeat.org/2018/4/2/21104689/four-day-school-weeks-a-nationwide-symptom-of-tight-budgets-lead-to-more-youth-crime-study-finds)).
- Education Week, which tracks the research for school leaders.

**Patterns**
- **Instructional time may matter more than the number of days.** Education Week reports that a 12-state study found no achievement gap in districts that kept high or medium total instructional time ([EdWeek, 2023](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/four-studies-to-know-on-four-day-school-weeks/2023/02)). I opened that paper but couldn't read its findings, so this is secondhand.
- **Perception and data often disagree.** Families report high satisfaction, and leaders believe the schedule helps them hire, yet measured outcomes often don't show those benefits.
- **Outcomes outside academics point in different directions.** Bullying fell in Oklahoma (secondhand through EdWeek; I couldn't open the study). Youth property crime rose in Colorado, and food insecurity rose in Oregon according to self-reported surveys ([Tomayko et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8595551)).

**Open questions**
- Do findings from small rural districts apply to the larger and suburban districts that have adopted the schedule since the pandemic?
- What happens to students on the fifth day, and who is responsible for it?
- Do effects change over many years?
