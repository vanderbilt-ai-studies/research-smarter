## Sources

**Peer-reviewed articles**

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Educational Researcher*). Morton, E. (2021). Effects of four-day school weeks on school finance and achievement: Evidence from Oklahoma. *Educational Researcher, 50*(1), 30–40. Free full text on ERIC: https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1284115.pdf
   Morton uses Oklahoma district data to compare districts before and after they switched. Four-day districts spent less, mainly on operations, transportation and food services, about 2% of the budget. She found no measurable effect on achievement.

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis*). Morton, E. (2023). Effects of 4-day school weeks on older adolescents: Examining impacts of the schedule on academic achievement, attendance, and behavior in high school. *Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis, 45*(1), 52–78.
   This one looks only at Oklahoma high schools. Bullying incidents fell about 39% and fighting about 31%, with no detectable effect on ACT scores or attendance. *The publisher's page blocked me, so I worked from the abstract in the Crossref database. Get the full text through your library and check it before you rely on it.*

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Journal of School Health*). Israel, W., Mulitauopele, C., Ma, M., Levinson, A. H., Cikara, L., & Brooks-Russell, A. (2020). Adolescent health behaviors in schools with 4- versus 5-day school weeks. *Journal of School Health, 90*(10), 794–801.
   This is a survey of Colorado high schoolers. The four-day week was linked to better student engagement and more physical activity, and also to more bullying and less sleep. The RAND report below cites it as finding that four-day students were more likely to take part in extracurriculars. *I found it through RAND's reference list and Crossref but couldn't open the article, so check it before citing it.*

**Research organization reports**

4. **Research organization report.** Kilburn, M. R., Phillips, A., Gomez, C. J., Mariano, L. T., Doss, C. J., Troxel, W. M., Morton, E., & Estes, K. (2021). *Does four equal five? Implementation and outcomes of the four-day school week* (RR-A373-1). RAND Corporation. https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA373-1.html
   This is a large study of districts in Idaho, New Mexico and Oklahoma, using surveys, site visits and interviews. In the full PDF I read several passages:
   - Most districts held sports practices on the fifth day.
   - In one district, parents said their children had band practice on the fifth day (p. 46).
   - No district provided transportation to fifth-day activities (p. 42).
   - High schoolers often used the fifth day to work.
   - Four-day students had about 30 to 40 minutes less free time after school on school days, but about 3.5 more free hours per week overall.

5. **Research organization review.** HEDCO Institute for Evidence-Based Educational Practice. (n.d.). *Does a four-day school week benefit students?* University of Oregon. https://hedcoinstitute.uoregon.edu/4DSW
   This is a systematic review of 11 studies on student outcomes. It found little evidence that four-day weeks help students. It says nothing about music or activities. The page lists no author or date.

**Primary source**

6. **Primary source** (official rule from Texas's statewide school-activities organization). University Interscholastic League. (n.d.). *Eight hour marching band rule FAQ.* https://www.uiltexas.org/music/marching-band/eight-hour-marching-band-rule-qa
   Texas caps marching band rehearsal outside the school day at 8 hours per week. Once the season is underway, a band may add one hour for each non-school day in a week with fewer than five school days. The rule is a policy document, not evidence of what bands actually do.
