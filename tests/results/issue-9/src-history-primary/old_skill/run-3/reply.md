**Primary sources and archives**

**Scholarly and reference works**

- "Philadelphia, Pennsylvania and the 1918–1919 Influenza Epidemic." *The American Influenza Epidemic of 1918–1919: A Digital Encyclopedia.* University of Michigan Center for the History of Medicine and Michigan Publishing. Accessed November 22, 2026. https://www.influenzaarchive.org/f/flu/cities/city-philadelphia.html
  A narrative of the epidemic from late August 1918 to January 1919, built mostly on *Inquirer* and *Evening Bulletin* articles cited by date in its endnotes. It also reports that the *Inquirer* thought the closing orders "went too far." Its endnotes work well as a map of articles to find.
- Stetler, Christina M. "The 1918 Spanish Influenza: Three Months of Horror in Philadelphia." *Pennsylvania History* 84, no. 4 (2017): 462–87. https://pa-history.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/PAH-84.4_04_Stetler.pdf
  A peer-reviewed history of the city's epidemic in general, not the press in particular. The PDF opened, but I couldn't extract its text, so I haven't read it. I took the citation details from search results and from JSTOR Daily's reference to it.
- Wirth, Thomas. "Influenza ('Spanish Flu' Pandemic, 1918–19)." *The Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia.* Rutgers–Camden, 2011. https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/archive/influenza-spanish-flu-pandemic-1918-19/
  It blames the city's toll on staff shortages, overcrowding, and the September 28 parade. It also reports that on September 21 the major papers ran a researcher's claim that the cause had been found, which the *Inquirer* said "armed the medical profession with absolute knowledge."

**Journalism and popular history**

- Shapiro, Walter. "How America's Newspapers Covered Up a Pandemic." *New Republic*, March 31, 2020. https://newrepublic.com/article/157094/americas-newspapers-covered-pandemic
  Argues that big-city papers "sugarcoated the truth" out of concern for wartime morale. Comparing Boston, New York, and Philadelphia papers on October 4, 1918, it finds that only Philadelphia's had belatedly admitted the emergency.
- Little, Becky. "As the 1918 Flu Emerged, Cover-Up and Denial Helped It Spread." *History*, May 26, 2020, updated May 28, 2025. https://www.history.com/articles/1918-pandemic-spanish-flu-censorship
  Says that before the parade, Philadelphia editors refused to print doctors' warnings. It also says it's hard to tell whether that was government pressure or self-censorship.
- Gladstone, Brooke, and John M. Barry. "Why the Press Downplayed the 1918 Flu." *On the Media*, WNYC Studios, November 27, 2020 (a rebroadcast). https://wnycstudios.org/podcasts/otm/segments/why-press-downplayed-1918-flu-on-the-media
  Barry, a historian of the pandemic, calls Philadelphia possibly "the most extreme example" of downplaying, and says the result was a loss of public trust. The transcript is marked as possibly not in final form.
- Meier, Allison C. "The 1918 Parade That Spread Death in Philadelphia." *JSTOR Daily*, November 2019 (the page shows both November 5 and November 9). https://daily.jstor.org/the-1918-parade-that-spread-death-in-philadelphia
  Draws on several historians to connect the parade to the rise in deaths. It notes that before the parade the *Public Ledger* printed a naval official's assurance that "there is no cause for further alarm."
- "The Flu in Philadelphia." *American Experience*, PBS/WGBH. https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/influenza-philadelphia/
  A short overview. It says that after the city admitted the epidemic, "members of the press condemned the closings as a violation of common sense and personal freedom."
- Balkansky, Arlene, with Tom Ewing. "Researching Epidemics in Chronicling America Newspapers." *Headlines & Heroes* (blog), Library of Congress, April 16, 2020. https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/2020/04/researching-epidemics-in-chronicling-america-newspapers/
  A how-to for searching digitized 1918 newspapers, written with a historian of the epidemic. It doesn't discuss Philadelphia.

## Questions the results raise

- The sources disagree on whether the "panic" in October came from the papers or the public. Some describe papers objecting to the closings and repeating reassurances as late as October 8.
- How much did the *Inquirer*, *Ledger*, *Bulletin*, and *North American* differ from each other? The *Ledger*, for example, was in a political fight with the Vare machine.
