4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Kestin, G., Miller, K., Klales, A., Milbourne, T., & Ponti, G. (2025). 5. **Peer-reviewed article.** Brasken, M., Berts, K.-E., Wallin, S., & Frilund, S. (2025). Students' use of ChatGPT in an algebra class: A case study of prompts and attitudes. *LUMAT: International Journal on Math, Science and Technology Education, 13*(1). https://doi.org/10.31129/LUMAT.13.1.2877

The authors conclude that without guidance, students used it for quick answers. I read only the abstract, and it doesn't measure learning outcomes.

**Preprints, not yet peer reviewed**

6. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047v2) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   This preprint reports two pre-registered lab experiments and a field study. Overall, the AI had no average effect on learning. Students who used it *in place of* learning activities, like generating exercise solutions, covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used it *alongside* their own work, like asking for explanations, understood more. The abstract says only "students" and doesn't give their age or level.

7. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Kosmyna, N., Hauptmann, E., Yuan, Y. T., Situ, J., Liao, X.-H., Beresnitzky, A. V., Braunstein, I., & Maes, P. (2025). *Your brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of cognitive debt when using an AI assistant for essay writing task* (arXiv:2506.08872) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872
   This MIT study wrote essays in an LLM group, a search-engine group, and a no-tools group, 54 participants in all, while measuring brain activity with EEG. The LLM group showed the weakest brain connectivity, felt the least ownership of their essays, and had trouble quoting their own work. Only 18 participants completed the final session.

## What I found and didn't find

- **High schoolers specifically are thin.** Only Bastani, Brasken, and the Nigeria paper study secondary students. Fan, Kestin, and probably Kosmyna studied university students or adults. Gerlich surveyed adults aged 17 and up, and Lehmann's abstract doesn't say who the students were.
- **Homework specifically is thinner still.** Most studies take place in class sessions or lab tasks, not at home on real homework.
- **The checking-versus-answers split appears directly in only one source,** the Lehmann preprint. I didn't find a peer-reviewed high school study that sorts students by how they used the chatbot and compares their learning.
- **There are almost no long-term studies.** The longest ones run a semester or a few weeks.
- **I didn't search for your counterargument** about whether teachers can tell the difference. That's still open.

## Questions the results raise

- Several of the studies that found benefits (Kestin, Nigeria, Bastani's tutor version) used tools *designed or supervised* to teach, not an open chatbot. That separates "how the student uses it" from "how the tool is set up," and your position currently focuses on the student.
- Fan found better work without more learning. That gap between the work and the learning could matter for any study that uses homework quality as its measure.
- Lehmann reports that AI widened the gap between students who already knew more and those who knew less. That points to a third variable alongside checking versus getting answers.
