**Peer-reviewed articles**

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*PNAS*). Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   Nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey practiced with either a standard ChatGPT-style tool or a "tutor" version built to protect learning. Both raised scores while students had the tool. When it was taken away, the standard-tool group scored 17% lower than students who never had it. The tutor version mostly avoided that drop. *The publisher's page blocked me, so I read the abstract [on IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html). A correction was published in August 2025. I haven't seen what it changes.*

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*British Journal of Educational Technology*). Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   This was a randomized experiment with 117 university students on a writing task. Students got help from ChatGPT, a human expert, writing-analytics tools, or nothing. Per search summaries, the ChatGPT group's essays improved, but they didn't gain more knowledge and showed less self-monitoring. *The publisher's page blocked me. A free copy is [on arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09315), which I opened. Crossref, a database of publication records, confirms the journal details.*

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Societies*). Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), 6. *No link: the publisher's page blocked me. Crossref confirms the details, and I read the abstract there. Search the title to find it.*
   This study surveyed and interviewed 666 people of different ages, not only students. Frequent AI use correlated with lower critical-thinking scores, and the study says leaning on AI to do the thinking (cognitive offloading) explains that link. Younger participants relied on AI more. 5. **Peer-reviewed article** (*ZDM – Mathematics Education*). Bulut, N., & Borromeo Ferri, R. (2026). **Preprints**

8. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047v2). https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   This preprint reports two lab experiments and a field study with students in a university programming course. Overall, chatbots had no effect on learning. Using the chatbot *instead of* doing the work let students cover more topics but understand each one less. Using it *alongside* the work (for example, asking for explanations) improved understanding. Chatbots also widened the gap between students who started with more and less knowledge. Note that the first version had a more negative title and framing.

9. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed.** Kosmyna, N., Hauptmann, E., Yuan, Y. T., Situ, J., Liao, X.-H., Beresnitzky, A. V., Braunstein, I., & Maes, P. (2025). *Your brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of cognitive debt when using an AI assistant for essay writing task* (arXiv:2506.08872v2). https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872
   This study had 54 adults write essays using a chatbot, a search engine, or no tool, while EEG measured their brain activity. The chatbot group showed the weakest brain connectivity, felt the least ownership of their essays, and had trouble quoting what they'd just written. The authors warn that the results may not apply to other tasks.

**Research organization reports**

10. **Research organization report.** McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
    Pew surveyed 1,458 U.S. teens in fall 2025. 54% had used chatbots for schoolwork, and 1 in 10 do all or most of their schoolwork with them. 59% said students at their school cheat with AI at least somewhat often. The report covers how teens use chatbots, not whether they learn from them.

11. **Research organization report.** Sidoti, O., Park, E., & Gottfried, J. (2025, January 15). *About a quarter of U.S. teens have used ChatGPT for schoolwork – double the share in 2023*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork
    In fall 2024, 26% of teens said they used ChatGPT for schoolwork, up from 13%. Most teens found it acceptable for researching topics (54%) but not for writing essays (42% said not acceptable).

## What I found and didn't find

- **There are few studies of high schoolers specifically.** Only Bastani, Yang, and Bulut study teens. Most of the experiments use university students.
- **I found no study that follows real teens' homework habits, sorts them into "checkers" and "answer-getters," and compares their later grades.** The closest are Lehmann's "instead of vs. alongside" comparison and Bastani's comparison of two tool designs. Both are about *how* the tool is used, but neither measures "checking your work" as such.
- **There are no long-term studies.** Everything runs from weeks to one semester.
- **I left out one source on purpose.** A widely cited meta-analysis (Wang & Fan, 2025, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*) was **retracted on April 22, 2026**. I confirmed this in Crossref. You may still see it quoted in news articles.

## Questions the results raise

- Bastani suggests the *tool's design* may matter as much as the student's choices. That's a different variable from the one in your position.
- Bulut found that teens rarely verified ChatGPT's output, so it's worth knowing how common "checking your work" actually is among high schoolers.
- Lehmann found chatbots widen the gap between stronger and weaker students. That may connect to who ends up using them in which way.
