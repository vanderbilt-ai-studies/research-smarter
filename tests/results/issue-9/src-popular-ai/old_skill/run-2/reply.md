**Peer-reviewed experiments and studies**

- Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
  This was a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students. They used either a standard ChatGPT-style tutor ("GPT Base") or one built with learning safeguards ("GPT Tutor"). Both groups did better on practice while they had access. Once access was removed, the GPT Base group did 17% worse than students who never had it, and the safeguards largely prevented that drop. *The publisher's page blocked me, so I read the abstract on [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html) and confirmed the details in Crossref. Crossref also lists a 2025 correction to this article, which you should check.*

- Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
  This randomized experiment had 117 university students do a writing task with help from ChatGPT, a human expert, writing-analytics tools, or no extra tool. The ChatGPT group's essay scores improved the most. Knowledge gain and transfer didn't differ between groups, and the groups went about regulating their own learning differently. *The journal page blocked me. I read the abstract on the [free arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09315).*

- Kestin, G., Miller, K., Klales, A., Milbourne, T., & Ponti, G. (2025). AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: An RCT introducing a novel research-based design in an authentic educational setting. *Scientific Reports, 15*, 17458. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6
  This was a crossover randomized trial with 194 students in an intro physics course at Harvard. Students learned more from a custom AI tutor at home than from an in-class active-learning lesson, and they did it in less time. *I read this one on [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/).*

- Yang, T.-C., Hsu, Y.-C., & Wu, J.-Y. (2025). The effectiveness of ChatGPT in assisting high school students in programming learning: Evidence from a quasi-experimental research. *Interactive Learning Environments, 33*(6), 3726–3743. https://doi.org/10.1080/10494820.2025.2450659
  In this quasi-experiment, 153 high school students learned programming. The students who used ChatGPT reported lower achievement, lower self-efficacy, and less "flow" than students taught with conventional methods. *The publisher blocked me. I read the abstract through Semantic Scholar's database, so check it against the full text.*

It found that more frequent AI use went with lower critical-thinking scores, with cognitive offloading as the link between the two. Younger participants showed more dependence on AI. **Peer-reviewed meta-analyses (studies that combine many studies)**

- Zhao, Y., Yue, Y., Sun, Z., Jiang, Q., & Li, G. (2025). Does generative artificial intelligence improve students' higher-order thinking? A meta-analysis based on 29 experiments and quasi-experiments. *Journal of Intelligence, 13*(12), 160. https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence13120160
  Across 29 studies, it found a moderate positive effect on higher-order thinking. The effect was largest for problem-solving and smallest for creativity, and strongest for learners who were better at regulating their own learning. *I read it on [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12734368/).*

**Preprints and working papers (not peer-reviewed)**

- Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
  Two lab experiments found no overall effect of LLMs on learning. Exploratory analyses and a field study found that the effect depended on how students used them. Students who had the AI do learning tasks for them (like generating solutions) covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used it as a supplement (like asking for explanations) understood more. The abstract doesn't say how old the participants were.

- Rismanchian, S., Uzun, H., Matayoshi, J., Cosyn, E., & Kurd-Misto, E. (2026). *Faster completion, less learning: Generative AI reduced study time on math problems and the knowledge they build* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21629v1
  This study used 3.2 million interactions in an online math platform over ten years. After ChatGPT came out, time spent on word problems that AI could easily solve dropped for high schoolers (31.3%) and college students. On proctored retention items, college students' odds of answering correctly fell 25%.

- Huntington-Klein, N. (2026). *Little impact of ChatGPT on high school test scores* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08812
  This study compared U.S. areas with heavier and lighter educational AI use. It found no significant difference in high school test scores between them and puts any effect below about 0.04 standard deviations. It's observational and looks at whole areas, not individual students.

- Kosmyna, N., Hauptmann, E., Yuan, Y. T., Situ, J., Liao, X.-H., Beresnitzky, A. V., Braunstein, I., & Maes, P. (2025). *Your brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of cognitive debt when using an AI assistant for essay writing task* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872
  54 participants wrote essays using an LLM, a search engine, or nothing, while wearing EEG headsets. The LLM group showed the weakest brain connectivity and the lowest sense of ownership over their essays.

- Zheng, J., Hao, L., Lu, K., Garg, A., Reese, M., Yap, M.-J., Wang, I.-J., Wu, X., Huang, W., Hoffman, J., Kelly, A., Le, M., Zhang, R., Lin, Y., Faayez, M., & Liu, A. (2025). *Do students rely on AI? Analysis of student-ChatGPT conversations from a field study* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.20244
  This study analyzed 315 conversations between college STEM students and ChatGPT during a quiz. Overall reliance on AI was low, but unhelpful reliance patterns tended to continue from one interaction to the next.

- De Simone, M. E., Tiberti, F. H., Barron Rodriguez, M. R., Manolio, F. A., Mosuro, W., & Dikoru, E. J. (2025). *From chalkboards to chatbots: Evaluating the impact of generative AI on learning outcomes in Nigeria* (Policy Research Working Paper No. 11125). World Bank.
  This was a randomized trial of a six-week after-school program in which secondary students in Nigeria used GPT-4 (through Microsoft Copilot) as a tutor for English. English scores rose by 0.23 standard deviations. *The World Bank site blocked me. I read the abstract on [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:11125).*

## What I found and didn't find

- **A retracted study.** A widely cited meta-analysis by Wang and Fan (2025) in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* reported large positive effects of ChatGPT, but it's now [marked as retracted](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-04787-y.html). News articles and other papers still cite it, so watch for it.
- **Plentiful:** experiments with college students, and short lab studies of a single task.
- **Thin:** studies of high schoolers doing ordinary homework on their own. Bastani, Yang, and the two 2026 preprints come closest.
- **What I couldn't find:** a study that sorts high schoolers by whether they choose to check their work or get answers, and then compares how much they learn. Lehmann et al. comes closest, but it isn't specific to high schoolers. In Bastani, the tool's design differed, not the students' choices.
- **Very little long-term evidence.** Most studies last from one session to one semester.

## Questions the results raise

- Several studies show a gap between how well students do while they have the AI and how well they do once it's gone. That raises the question of what "help" should mean in your paper.
- Some studies are about how the tool was built (guardrails, tutor prompts). Others are about how students chose to use it. Your position is about student choice, and those two may not be the same thing.
- Two sources suggest AI may widen gaps between students who already know more or regulate their own learning better (Lehmann; Zhao). That could matter for who actually ends up "checking their work."
