## Sources

**Peer-reviewed experiments**

1. Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), e2422633122. DOI 10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   Opened: [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nas:journl:v:122:y:2025:p:e2422633122). The PNAS page blocked my tool. About 1,000 high school math students got either a plain ChatGPT-style tool ("GPT Base") or one with learning safeguards ("GPT Tutor"). Both raised practice scores. Once access was removed, the GPT Base group scored 17% lower on the exam than students who never had it. The safeguards "largely mitigated" that drop.

2. Kestin, G., Miller, K., Klales, A., Milbourne, T., & Ponti, G. (2025). AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: An RCT introducing a novel research-based design in an authentic educational setting. *Scientific Reports, 15*, 17458. DOI 10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6
   Opened: [PubMed Central full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/). In a Harvard intro physics course, students who learned with a custom-built AI tutor scored higher on a post-test than students in an active-learning class session, in less time. These were college students, not high schoolers.

3. Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, 108386. DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386
   Opened: [University of Augsburg repository](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/114673). 91 university students researched a science question with either ChatGPT or Google. The ChatGPT users said the task felt easier, but their reasoning and arguments were weaker.

**Peer-reviewed reviews and survey studies**

4. Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, 105224. DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   The publisher page wouldn't load, so these details come from [BibBase](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/deng-jiang-yu-lu-liu-doeschatgptenhancestudentlearningasystematicreviewandmetaanalysisofexperimentalstudies-2025), a secondary listing. It pools 69 experimental studies from 2022–2024. It reports better academic performance and higher-order thinking with ChatGPT, lower mental effort, and no change in self-efficacy.

5. Weidlich, J., Gasevic, D., Drachsler, H., & Kirschner, P. (2025). ChatGPT in education: An effect in search of a cause. *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning, 41*(5), e70105. DOI 10.1111/jcal.70105
   Opened: [Open Universiteit record](https://research.ou.nl/en/publications/chatgpt-in-education-an-effect-in-search-of-a-cause/). This is a critique that uses the Deng meta-analysis as its example. The authors checked a sample of its studies and found only a small minority met basic design standards. They conclude that the observed gains "cannot, at this time, be confidently attributed to ChatGPT."

6. Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies, 15*(1), 6.
   Opened: [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:gam:jsoctx:v:15:y:2025:i:1:p:6-:d:1559787). The DOI in my search results wouldn't open, so confirm it yourself. In surveys and interviews with 666 people, more frequent AI use went along with lower critical-thinking scores, and handing mental work to the tool ("cognitive offloading") helped explain that link. Younger participants relied on AI more. The study is correlational, and the author says so.

**Preprint and working paper (not peer-reviewed)**

7. Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047v2) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
   The authors ran two pre-registered lab experiments and a field study. Overall, LLMs had no effect on learning. Students who used them to generate solutions covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who used them to ask for explanations understood more. LLMs also widened the gap between students with more and less prior knowledge.

8. De Simone, M. E., Tiberti, F. H., Barron Rodriguez, M. R., Manolio, F. A., Mosuro, W., & Dikoru, E. J. (2025). *From chalkboards to chatbots: Evaluating the impact of generative AI on learning outcomes in Nigeria* (Policy Research Working Paper No. 11125). World Bank. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:11125
   This was a randomized trial of a six-week, teacher-guided after-school program in which first-year senior secondary students used Microsoft Copilot (GPT-4) as an English tutor. The effect was 0.23 standard deviations on English and 0.31 overall. Students who attended more gained more. The authors' [blog post](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/From-chalkboards-to-chatbots-Transforming-learning-in-Nigeria) gives a rounded "about 0.3."

**Surveys from research organizations**

9. McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/
   Based on 1,458 U.S. teens surveyed in fall 2025. 54% have used chatbots for schoolwork, and about 1 in 10 say they do all or most of their schoolwork this way. 59% think classmates use AI to cheat at least somewhat often.

10. Pew Research Center. (2025, January 15). *About a quarter of U.S. teens have used ChatGPT for schoolwork – double the share in 2023*. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork/
    26% of teens had used ChatGPT for schoolwork in fall 2024, up from 13% in 2023. Most teens found it acceptable for researching (54%), opinion was split on solving math problems, and few accepted it for writing essays (18%). I didn't capture the authors, so get them from the page.

11. Schwartz, H. L., & Diliberti, M. K. (2026). *More students use AI for homework, and more believe it harms critical thinking: Selected findings from the American Youth Panel* (RR-A4742-1). RAND Corporation. https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4742-1.html
    1,214 students aged 12–29 were surveyed in December 2025. Homework use of AI rose from 48% to 62% since May 2025, driven by middle and high schoolers. 67% said AI use harms critical thinking. Getting direct answers was the only use most students counted as cheating. The report gives no high-school-only percentage.

## What I found and didn't find

- **A retracted paper to avoid.** A widely shared 2025 meta-analysis by Wang and Fan in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* reported large learning gains from ChatGPT. The record I opened marks it "RETRACTED ARTICLE," so I left it off the list. Blogs and news sites still cite it, so watch for it.
- **High school evidence is thin.** Only two experiments involve high school students: Bastani and De Simone. Most experiments use college students.
- **No study measured "checking your work" directly.** The closest are Lehmann (asking for explanations vs. generating solutions) and Bastani. Bastani compared two tool designs, so it doesn't show what students chose to do on their own.
- **Almost no long-term studies.** Most studies last a single session or a few weeks.
- **Nothing on whether teachers can tell the difference.** That was the counterargument in your log, and I found no study of it.

## Questions the results raise

- Bastani's safeguards were built into the tool, not chosen by students. That leaves open whether your line between "checking" and "getting answers" is about student choices or about tool design.
- Lehmann found the gap between stronger and weaker students widened, and the Nigeria gains were largest for students who already scored higher. That raises the question of which students tend to "check their work" in the first place.
- The Weidlich critique questions how much the positive experiments can prove at all.
