## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Learning.** The main fight is over test scores. A 2024 multi-state study in *Economics of Education Review* (Morton, Thompson & Kuhfeld) found reasons for concern about achievement and growth, especially in non-rural districts. Other studies find small or no effects. An analysis of the 11 strongest studies by the University of Oregon's HEDCO Institute found "no evidence of large positive effects" ([Journalist's Resource](https://journalistsresource.org/education/four-day-school-week-research/)).
- **Money vs. outcomes.** Districts usually switch to save money, but reported savings are about 0.4% to 2.5% of the budget ([NCSL](https://www.ncsl.org/education/four-day-school-week-overview)). People disagree about whether that's worth it.
- **Teachers.** Districts use the schedule to recruit teachers. One Texas study found turnover fell about 2.7 percentage points ([Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04)). Search results describe a Missouri study that found no retention benefit, but I didn't open it.

**Major voices**
- **Economists of education** do most of the achievement research: Emily Morton, Paul Thompson at Oregon State, and Megan Kuhfeld at NWEA.
- **RAND Corporation** published a widely cited 2021 brief, *Can Four Equal Five?*, on Idaho, New Mexico and Oklahoma. Its PDF wouldn't open in readable form for me, so I only know it through news coverage.
- **Rural education researchers** study parent and community views, for example in the journal *The Rural Educator*.
- **Policy trackers** like NCSL and journalists at Education Week follow how fast the schedule is spreading.

**Patterns**
- **It's spreading fast.** More than 2,100 schools in 26 states used it as of 2024–25 ([Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04)).
- **Most studies use the same method.** They compare districts that switched with similar districts that didn't, using state test or administrative data.
- **Results depend on context.** Rural vs. non-rural, total hours of instruction, and student age keep coming up as factors that change the results.
- **Families like it.** A survey of 584 parents in three rural Missouri districts found strong support in every group. Families with only elementary-age children and families of students in special education were less supportive ([Turner, Finch & Uribe-Zarain, 2019](https://scholarsjunction.msstate.edu/ruraleducator/vol40/iss1/1/)).

**Open questions**
- **The fifth day.** A 2018 Colorado study by Fischer & Argyle linked the schedule to more youth crime ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econedurev.2018.03.010)). A 2026 study by Najam & Thompson in the *Journal of Policy Analysis and Management* found more property and violent crime in non-rural areas. It also found fewer drug- and alcohol-related offenses during school hours. Their DOI link wouldn't open, so I read only the abstract, through Crossref.
- **Little-studied areas.** Food insecurity, childcare and teacher well-being have very little research so far.
- **Is it the day or the hours?** Nobody has settled whether the harm comes from cutting a day or from cutting total hours.

Before you use any of this, open the linked pages and find each figure yourself. Several of these details came from abstracts and news summaries rather than full papers. Pick any claim, like Morton's finding about non-rural districts, and ask me how I know it. I only saw that one in search snippets, so it's the shakiest.
