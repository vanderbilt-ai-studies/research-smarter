**Peer-reviewed articles**

1. **Peer-reviewed article** · Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Michail, M., Patterson, P., Sitch, A., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2026). How school phone policies influence adolescent phone use and wellbeing (SMART Schools): A qualitative comparative case study. *Social Science & Medicine, 398*, Article 119094. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2026.119094
   The researchers ran 40 focus groups with 177 pupils, parents and staff in seven English secondary schools, comparing restrictive and permissive phone policies. Both kinds of policy had good and bad effects on wellbeing. The university's [news release](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/news/2026/school-phone-bans-or-not-digital-lives-of-students-affect-the-school-day) adds two points. Some participants reported more face-to-face interaction in restrictive schools. Phone-related bullying continued there, and conflicts that started online outside school sometimes "blow up" face-to-face in school. (I read the abstract on the [university repository](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/how-school-phone-policies-influence-adolescent-phone-use-and-well/) and the release, not the full paper.)

2. **Peer-reviewed article** · Goodyear, V., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, Article 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211
   The study covered 1,227 students aged 12–15 in 30 English schools. Students in restrictive schools used phones and social media less during school hours. Their overall weekday and weekend use was no different, and neither was their mental wellbeing. (Abstract read on the [university repository](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/school-phone-policies-and-their-association-with-mental-wellbeing/).)

3. **Peer-reviewed article** · Bar, E., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., Wade, T. D., & King, D. L. (2025). Student perspectives on banning mobile phones in South Australian secondary schools: A large-scale qualitative analysis. *Computers in Human Behavior, 167*, Article 108603. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108603
   The researchers analyzed about 7,200 open-ended answers from 1,549 students at five schools. According to [Flinders University's summary](https://news.flinders.edu.au/blog/2025/09/18/benefits-of-school-phone-bans/), students reported more face-to-face interaction and less cyberbullying and conflict. They also said they missed phones for contacting parents and coping with stress. The publisher blocked me from the article page, so this summary comes from the university's write-up.

4. **Peer-reviewed article** · King, D. L., Radunz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B., & Wade, T. (2024). "Phones off while school's on": Evaluating problematic phone use and the social, wellbeing, and academic effects of banning phones in schools. *Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 13*(4), 913–922. https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.2024.00058
   This preregistered study, by the same Australian team, compared students in Grades 7–12 at schools with and without the ban. It found no significant differences in problematic phone use, academic engagement or school belonging. Bullying was slightly higher in the ban group, but it fell in both groups. ([Full text on PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11737419/).)

5. **Peer-reviewed article** · Vanluydt, E., van den Eijnden, R., Vonk, L., Putrik, P., van Amelsvoort, T., Delespaul, P., Levels, M., & Huijts, T. (2026). Disconnect to reconnect: How variations between types of smartphone bans influence students' well-being and social connectedness in Dutch secondary education. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 55*(3), 551–569. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02313-6
   This study compared 1,398 students at Dutch schools with partial bans and full bans. It found no differences in well-being or bullying. Full bans were linked to weaker student–teacher connectedness and, for girls, lower school belonging. (Abstract read on [Maastricht University's repository](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/disconnect-to-reconnect-how-variations-between-types-of-smartphon/). The DOI leads to a paywall.)

6. **Peer-reviewed article** · Abrahamsson, S. (2026). Smartphone bans, student outcomes and mental health. *Journal of Human Resources*. Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.0224-13403R2
   This study used Norwegian middle-school data from before and after schools adopted bans. On average, it found no effect on education or mental health. It did find lower bullying for both boys and girls, and fewer mental-health visits and better grades for girls. (I read the abstract only; the full text is paywalled.)

7. **Peer-reviewed article** · Beneito, P., & Vicente-Chirivella, Ó. (2022). Banning mobile phones in schools: Evidence from regional-level policies in Spain. *Applied Economic Analysis, 30*(90), 153–175. https://doi.org/10.1108/AEA-05-2021-0112
   The authors compared two Spanish regions that banned phones in 2015 with the rest of Spain. They report "noticeable reductions" in officially recorded bullying among teenagers. The bullying data are official counts and don't separate cyberbullying from other bullying.

8. **Peer-reviewed article (rapid review)** · Böttger, T., & Zierer, K. (2024). To ban or not to ban? A rapid review on the impact of smartphone bans in schools on social well-being and academic performance. *Education Sciences, 14*(8), 906.
   This review pooled five European studies and found a small overall benefit from bans (d = 0.16), stronger for social well-being than for grades. I confirmed the article and journal in Crossref, but the publisher blocked me from the article page. This summary comes only from search excerpts, so I haven't given a link.

**Research organization report**

9. **Research organization report** · Reynolds, M., Esfandiari, M., & O'Higgins Norman, J. (2025). *Restriction or resilience? Smartphone bans in schools: A qualitative study on the experiences of students and teachers*. DCU Anti-Bullying Centre. https://www.dcu.ie/antibullyingcentre/restriction-or-resilience
   The researchers held focus groups with 66 Irish students aged 10–18. The report concludes that "students clearly stated that the bans just move any cyberbullying behaviour until after school." Students also said the ban does not promote socialising. This is a university research centre's report, not a peer-reviewed article.

### What I found and didn't find

- **Plentiful:** recent studies from England, Australia, the Netherlands, Norway, Spain and Ireland, most from 2024–2026.
- **Thin:** direct measures of face-to-face interaction. Most of that evidence comes from what students and staff say in surveys and focus groups. I found no study that observed or counted conversations.
- **Not found:** any study that records *when* cyberbullying happens (in school or after school) before and after a ban. The after-school claims rest on what students say, and most bullying measures don't separate online from offline bullying.
- **Not found:** a peer-reviewed US study on high schools. Several samples are middle-school or under-16 students (Norway, SMART Schools).

### A question the results raise

The face-to-face evidence and the after-school cyberbullying claims both come mostly from what students report. Some sources describe students who dislike the ban. How much weight can perception data like that carry in an argument about what actually happens?
