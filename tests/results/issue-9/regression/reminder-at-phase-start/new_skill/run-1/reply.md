## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Achievement against everything else.** The strongest studies find that students in four-day districts learn somewhat less over time. Supporters point to other things: teacher morale, fewer behavior problems, cost savings. A lot of the argument comes down to how much weight test scores should get.
- **Does it actually help with teachers?** Teacher recruitment is the most common reason districts switch, and here the evidence goes both ways. An Oregon study found teacher turnover went *up* by about 2 percentage points after adoption. A Texas working paper found it went *down* by about 2.7 points, though more than 1 in 5 teachers still left each year. A one-district case study found that every open position got filled.
- **Students' lives outside school.** Researchers disagree about what the fifth day does to kids. A Colorado study linked four-day weeks to about a 20% rise in juvenile crime. An Oregon survey study linked them to more food insecurity and more drug use. A study of Oklahoma high schools found bullying fell about 39% and fights about 31%.

**Major voices**
- Emily Morton (NWEA) and Paul Thompson (Oregon State) wrote much of the peer-reviewed work on achievement and student outcomes.
- A RAND team (Kilburn, Phillips, Doss, and others) did the largest mixed-methods study, with interviews in Idaho, New Mexico, and Oklahoma.
- Economists studying teacher labor markets: Ainsworth, Liu, and Penner (Oregon) and Lawson (Texas).

**Patterns**
- Most achievement studies compare districts that switched with similar districts that didn't, and use test scores in grades 3–8.
- The results seem to depend on context. Morton's six-state study found the losses were concentrated in non-rural schools.
- The evidence on teachers is thinner and newer than the evidence on achievement, and much of it is still working papers.
- Most of the research is about rural districts in a handful of states (Colorado, Missouri, Oregon, Oklahoma).

**Open questions**
- What do teachers actually experience, beyond whether they stay or leave? Most studies measure turnover, not wellbeing.
- Whether the effects change with how a district designs the schedule. Some researchers argue that districts which kept total instructional time saw smaller losses. I found that claim in news coverage of a Thompson and Ward study but couldn't confirm the paper itself.
- Missouri's own state study reportedly found no significant effect on achievement. That would conflict with RAND. I couldn't open the state's report, so treat that as unverified.
