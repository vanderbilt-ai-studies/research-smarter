## Briefing: outcomes for voucher users

**Key debates**
- **Test scores vs. long-term outcomes.** Some strong studies find voucher users lose ground on tests:
  - **Louisiana:** students in the Louisiana Scholarship Program lost about 0.4 standard deviations in math, with "large" negative effects in reading, science and social studies. The study compared students who won and lost the voucher lottery. ([Abdulkadiroglu, Pathak & Walters, NBER working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21839); the NBER page lists the published version in *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*, 2018.)
  - **Indiana:** low-income students who switched to private school lost 0.15 SD in math in their first year, with no meaningful effect on English. This study compared voucher users to similar students rather than using a lottery. (Waddington & Berends, *Journal of Policy Analysis and Management*, 2018, [RePEc listing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jpamgt/v37y2018i4p783-808.html).)
  - **Florida:** on college, the [Urban Institute's 2019 brief](https://www.urban.org/research/publication/effects-florida-tax-credit-scholarship-program-college-enrollment-and-graduation) found 57% of Florida Tax Credit scholarship students enrolled in college, compared with 51% of non-participants. Bachelor's degree rates were slightly higher (12% vs. 10% for students who entered in grades 8–10).
- **Is the overall evidence positive or negative?** A meta-analysis of 21 lottery-based studies across 11 programs worldwide found "moderate evidence of positive achievement impacts" but large differences between programs. (Shakeel, Anderson & Wolf, *School Effectiveness and School Improvement*, 2021, DOI 10.1080/09243453.2021.1906283. I confirmed it on OpenAlex but couldn't open the publisher's page.) Critics have challenged its methods. I found that critique in search results but couldn't open it.

**Major voices**
- Economists who use lottery designs (Louisiana).
- Patrick Wolf and colleagues at the University of Arkansas, who led the federal DC evaluation and co-wrote the meta-analysis.
- Berends and Waddington (Indiana).
- Research organizations: the Urban Institute; [RAND](https://www.rand.org/about.html), which calls itself "nonprofit, nonpartisan" on its about page; and [FutureEd](https://www.future-ed.org/about/), a Georgetown think tank that also calls itself nonpartisan.

**Patterns**
- **Older, smaller programs look better than newer statewide ones.** The [2009 federal DC evaluation](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/resource-library/report/evaluation-report/evaluation-dc-opportunity-scholarship-program-impacts-after-three-years) found reading gains but no math gains. A later DC cohort reportedly did worse in math; I saw that only in search results.
- **Early losses may fade.** In search results only, Indiana students recovered by year four and DC students by year three.
- **School quality may explain the losses.** The Louisiana authors suggest the problem may be which private schools chose to join the program.

**Open questions**
- **Who are "the students who use them"?** In new universal programs, many users were already in private school or homeschooled. [RAND's Arizona case study](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA3431-2.html) tells states to expect "a significant share" of such users and reports no achievement results. It was funded by the Walton Family Foundation, per the RAND page.
- **No results yet for the newest programs.** [FutureEd's 2024 report](https://www.future-ed.org/early-returns-first-results-from-the-new-wave-of-public-funding-of-private-schooling/) says learning is hard to measure under universal programs.
- **Are the college gains real?** It's unclear whether they reflect real learning or something else, such as different counseling or school culture.

I'm least sure of FutureEd's statement that most recent statewide studies show negative results: I only saw it in a summary of the page, not the text itself. Open the report and find that passage before relying on it, and ask me how I know anything else in this briefing, like the Louisiana 0.4 figure.
