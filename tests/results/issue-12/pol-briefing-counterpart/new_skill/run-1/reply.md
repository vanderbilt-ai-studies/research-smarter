## Briefing: do vouchers help the students who use them?

### Key debates

**1. Test scores. The best-known recent U.S. studies found zero or negative effects.**
- *Louisiana.* Peer-reviewed article, *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics* (2018). Abdulkadiroğlu, Pathak & Walters, ["Free to Choose: Can School Choice Reduce Student Achievement?"](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.20160634), DOI 10.1257/app.20160634. Families who applied were assigned by lottery, which makes this one of the strongest research designs available. Using a voucher lowered math scores by about 0.4 standard deviations, and reading, science and social studies scores also fell. The authors think lower-quality, lower-tuition private schools may have been the ones that joined the program. Search results mentioned a later follow-up finding the losses faded by year three for some grades. I didn't open that follow-up, so check it before relying on it.
- *Indiana.* Peer-reviewed article, *Journal of Policy Analysis and Management* 37(4), 2018. Waddington & Berends, ["Impact of the Indiana Choice Scholarship Program"](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/impact-of-the-indiana-choice-scholarship-program-achievement-effe/), DOI 10.1002/pam.22086. Low-income voucher students lost about 0.15 standard deviations in math in their first year compared with matched public-school students, and the loss didn't go away over time. There was no meaningful effect on English/Language Arts.
- *Washington, DC.* Government report, U.S. Institute of Education Sciences, May 2019. [*Evaluation of the DC Opportunity Scholarship Program: Impacts After Three Years*](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/resource-library/report/evaluation-report/evaluation-dc-opportunity-scholarship-program-impacts-after-three-years-0), NCEE 2019-4006. This was also a lottery study. It found no effect on math or reading. Students (but not parents) were more satisfied with their schools, and students saw their schools as safer.

**2. Educational attainment: graduating and going to college.** Some studies of these longer-term outcomes point the other way from the test scores.
- *DC, earlier cohorts.* Government report, IES, 2010. [*Evaluation of the Impact of the DC Opportunity Scholarship Program: Final Report*](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/resource-library/report/evaluation-report/evaluation-impact-dc-opportunity-scholarship-program-final-report), NCEE 2010-4018. It found no clear effect on achievement. However, 82% of students offered a scholarship got a high school diploma, compared with 70% of those not offered one (graduation was reported by parents).
- *Florida.* Research organization summary. Chingos & Kuehn (Urban Institute), ["Private School Choice Increases College Enrollment in Florida"](https://www.educationnext.org/private-school-choice-increases-college-enrollment-florida-work-nationally/), Education Next, 2017. This is the authors' own short write-up of their study, not the study itself. They say Florida Tax Credit students enrolled in Florida public colleges at higher rates, and the more years a student stayed in the program, the higher the rate. They also warn that students who stay in the program may differ from those who leave: 37% leave after one year. A 2019 update reported effects on earning a degree too, but I couldn't open the full report, so I haven't seen those numbers. I also couldn't confirm a search snippet saying the Walton Family Foundation partly funded the evaluation.
- *Critique of the Florida update.* Research center review. Jaekyung Lee, [NEPC review of the 2019 Florida update](https://nepc.colorado.edu/node/9706), March 2019. It credits the study's improved data. It also raises three questions about the study's methods and conclusions, warns that voucher advocates may misuse it, and calls for studies that confirm the result.

**3. What the research as a whole shows.**
- Peer-reviewed review article, *Journal of Economic Literature* 55(2), 2017. Epple, Romano & Urquiola, ["School Vouchers: A Survey of the Economics Literature"](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jel.20150679), DOI 10.1257/jel.20150679. Small voucher programs don't reliably improve outcomes, and some have caused harm, though some settings and subgroups do see real gains. The authors conclude the evidence doesn't justify adopting vouchers widely but does justify continued study.
- Working paper, not peer reviewed. Shakeel, Anderson & Wolf (2016), ["The Participant Effects of Private School Vouchers Across the Globe"](https://scholarworks.uark.edu/edrepub/33). This pooled 19 lottery studies of 11 programs and found positive achievement effects overall. Effects were larger for reading than for math, larger outside the U.S., and larger for publicly funded programs. A peer-reviewed version appeared in 2021, but the journal's page blocked me, so I haven't seen it. A search result described an NEPC critique saying nearly half the studies came from the authors' own research group. I couldn't open that critique.

**4. The voucher programs states are creating now are different.** Since about 2022, many states have opened eligibility to nearly every student, and these new programs are mostly not the ones studied above.
- News article. Stone & Peetz Stephens, ["As School Choice Goes Universal, What New Research Is Showing"](https://www.edweek.org/policy-politics/as-school-choice-goes-universal-what-new-research-is-showing/2026/01), *Education Week*, January 2026. It reports that 18 states now have near-universal programs and that their academic effects are largely unknown. Early test results in Iowa and Arkansas look good, but most students in those programs were already in private school. The article also cites an Urban Institute study of Ohio's EdChoice program that found recipients more likely to attend and finish college. I haven't opened that study.
- News article. Linda Jacobson, ["In Arizona, the Typical ESA Recipient Already Attends Private School, Study Finds"](https://www.the74million.org/article/in-arizona-the-typical-esa-recipient-already-attends-private-school-study-finds/), *The 74*, December 2025. It reports a RAND finding that nearly three-quarters of eligible Arizona students were in private school or homeschooled before joining. That share has shifted more recently: per state data, 57% of the past year's new enrollees came from public school. I couldn't find the RAND report itself.

### Patterns
- Test scores and attainment often don't move together. The same program can look bad on one and good on the other.
- Results differ a lot by program, state and year in the program. Early-year losses sometimes shrink later.
- Who counts as "the students who use them" is changing. Older programs targeted low-income students leaving public schools. Many students in the new universal programs were already in private school.
- How strong a study is depends heavily on its design. Lottery studies and matched-comparison studies can reach different answers.

### Open questions in the field
- Why test scores fall in some programs while graduation or college enrollment rises.
- Whether early losses are a temporary adjustment or a lasting effect.
- How the new universal programs affect learning, which is still largely unstudied.
- Which outcomes count when judging whether vouchers "improve outcomes."
