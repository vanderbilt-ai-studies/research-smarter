## Second briefing: voucher effects on the students who use them

**Key debates**
- **Test scores versus long-term outcomes.** Several well-designed recent studies found voucher users' test scores fell or didn't change. Some studies of longer-term outcomes, like graduation and college, found gains. Researchers disagree about which of these matters more.
- **Why scores dropped.** In the Louisiana study, the authors suggest that lower-quality private schools may have been the ones that chose to join the program. If that's right, the program's design drove the result, and vouchers as an idea wouldn't explain it.
- **How to add it all up.** A meta-analysis that pooled randomized trials reported positive average effects on achievement. Critics have challenged how it was put together.

**Major voices and studies**
- **Abdulkadiroğlu, Pathak & Walters (2018), Louisiana.** Peer-reviewed article, *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*. Based on admission lotteries, it found that attending a participating private school lowered math scores by about 0.4 standard deviations, and reading, science, and social studies scores also fell. [Link](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.20160634), DOI 10.1257/app.20160634.
- **Waddington & Berends (2018), Indiana.** Peer-reviewed article, *Journal of Policy Analysis and Management*. It compared voucher students with matched public school students. In the first private school year, math scores dropped 0.15 standard deviations and the loss persisted. There was no meaningful effect in English. [RePEc listing](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:jpamgt:v:37:y:2018:i:4:p:783-808).
- **DC Opportunity Scholarship Program.** Two government reports from the U.S. Department of Education's research arm (IES):
  - [2010 final report](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/resource-library/report/evaluation-report/evaluation-impact-dc-opportunity-scholarship-program-final-report): no clear effect on achievement. Graduation was 82% for students offered a scholarship and 70% for those who weren't. That graduation figure is based on what parents reported.
  - [2019 report](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/pubs/20194006/), on a later group of students: no effect on math or reading after three years. Students were more satisfied with their schools and felt safer.
- **Chingos, Monarrez & Kuehn (2019), Florida.** Research organization report from the Urban Institute. I didn't open its "about" page, so I haven't confirmed its affiliation. It found that students using Florida's tax-credit scholarship were more likely to enroll in and graduate from college, and the effect grew the longer they stayed in the program. One thing to watch: the study matched students on characteristics it could observe and wasn't a lottery, so differences between families, like motivation, may explain some of the result. [Link](https://www.urban.org/research/publication/effects-florida-tax-credit-scholarship-program-college-enrollment-and-graduation).
- **Shakeel, Anderson & Wolf (2021).** Peer-reviewed meta-analysis in *School Effectiveness and School Improvement*. I confirmed the journal through OpenAlex. It pooled randomized trials from 11 voucher programs and reported moderate positive effects in math and reading, with large differences across studies and years. The publisher's page blocked me, so I'm working from the abstract only. DOI [10.1080/09243453.2021.1906283](https://doi.org/10.1080/09243453.2021.1906283). Patrick Wolf, one of its authors, also led the 2010 DC report.
- **Critical review of an earlier version of that meta-analysis**, by Christopher Lubienski, published by the National Education Policy Center. I found the PDF but couldn't read its text, and I couldn't confirm the center's stance. Treat it as a lead to check, not a source yet.

**Patterns**
- The strongest designs use admission lotteries (Louisiana, DC). The Indiana and Florida studies match students instead, which is weaker for ruling out family differences.
- Results vary a lot by program, by state, and by how long students stay in the program.
- "Outcomes" means different things in different studies: test scores, graduation, college, satisfaction, safety.

**Open questions**
- Do early score losses fade or persist over time? The sources here don't agree.
- From my training, not checked this session: most rigorous studies looked at older programs aimed at low-income students. The newer universal programs, open to all families, have barely been studied.
