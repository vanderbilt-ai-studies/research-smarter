## Second briefing: what vouchers do for the students who use them

### Key debates

**1. Test scores and long-term outcomes point in different directions.** In several recent large programs, students who used vouchers did worse on state tests, especially in math. Some of the same programs show higher high school graduation or college enrollment.
- **Louisiana:** Students who won a lottery to attend a private school scored about 0.4 standard deviations lower in math. Reading, science and social studies effects were also negative. The losses were concentrated in lower-tuition schools.
- **Indiana:** Voucher users lost about 0.15 standard deviations in math in their first year, and the loss persisted. There was no clear effect in English.
- **Ohio EdChoice:** Voucher users did worse on state tests, more in math than English. A 2025 follow-up found the same students were 15 percentage points more likely to enroll in college (compared with 48% for similar public school students) and 9 points more likely to finish a bachelor's degree (compared with 15%).
- **Florida's tax-credit scholarship:** Students who entered in elementary or middle school were 6 points more likely to enroll in college. Effects on earning a degree were much smaller, about 1–2 points.
- **Washington, DC:** The program had no clear effect on test scores. Parents reported higher graduation rates (82% vs. 70%). But a later study using lottery data found no difference in college enrollment.

**2. How much to trust each research design.** The Louisiana and DC studies compare lottery winners with lottery losers, which is close to a true experiment. The Indiana, Ohio and Florida studies compare voucher users with similar students who stayed in public school. The Ohio authors warn that their longer-participation results are "at greater risk of bias" because families who stay in a program longer may differ in ways the data can't see.

**3. Whether the answer depends on the program.** The Louisiana authors suggest lower-quality private schools may have chosen to join the program. Newer programs are also very different from the ones studied.
- The Ohio authors note their results come from low-income students who left public schools. They say it's unclear the results hold under today's universal eligibility.
- In Arizona's universal program, a 2025 RAND analysis (reported by K-12 Dive) found that users tend to come from higher-achieving, more affluent districts.

### Major voices

- Economists Atila Abdulkadiroğlu, Parag Pathak and Christopher Walters (Louisiana)
- Mark Berends and Joseph Waddington (Indiana)
- David Figlio and Krzysztof Karbownik (Ohio)
- Matthew Chingos of the Urban Institute (Florida, Ohio, DC college enrollment)
- Patrick Wolf of the University of Arkansas (the DC evaluation and a meta-analysis that pooled lottery studies worldwide and found positive effects overall). Critics have questioned that meta-analysis; I couldn't read their critique, so I'm not summarizing it.

### Patterns

- Older and smaller programs, and the pooled meta-analysis, look more positive on test scores.
- Several large statewide programs from the 2010s look negative on test scores, especially in math.
- Results for long-term outcomes like graduation and college are mostly positive or null, but most of them come from the weaker, non-lottery designs.

### Open questions in the field

- Do early test-score losses fade over time?
- Do college gains hold up when researchers can rule out selection?
- Are state tests a fair way to judge private schools? The Ohio authors argue they may not be.
- What do vouchers do under universal programs, where many users were already in private school?
