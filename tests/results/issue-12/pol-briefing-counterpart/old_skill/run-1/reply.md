## Second briefing: voucher effects on the students who use them

**Key debates**
- **Test scores vs. long-run attainment.** Several recent U.S. evaluations found voucher users' test scores were flat or *lower*. One study of Florida found voucher users were more likely to enroll in and finish college. Researchers disagree about which outcome counts more.
- **Do early losses fade?** Some programs show big first-year drops in scores. People disagree over whether those are temporary "transition" effects or lasting harm.
- **Is the evidence balanced?** A widely cited international meta-analysis reports positive average effects. Critics note that many of the studies it includes come from the same research group.

**Major voices**
- Economists Atila Abdulkadiroğlu, Parag Pathak and Christopher Walters (Louisiana)
- R. Joseph Waddington and Mark Berends (Indiana)
- Federal evaluators at IES/Westat (Washington, D.C.)
- Matthew Chingos and colleagues at the Urban Institute (Florida)
- Patrick Wolf and coauthors at the University of Arkansas (meta-analyses)
- RAND (the new universal "education savings account," or ESA, programs)

**Patterns**
- **Louisiana:** the lottery-based study found that attending a participating private school lowered math scores by about 0.4 standard deviations, with losses in reading, science and social studies too. The authors suggest low-quality private schools may have chosen to join the program.
- **Indiana:** low-income students who switched to private schools lost about 0.15 SD in math in their first year, and that loss persisted. There was no meaningful effect in English.
- **D.C.:** after three years there was no effect on math or reading. Students (but not parents) reported higher satisfaction and felt safer at school.
- **Florida:** 57% of scholarship students enrolled in college vs. 51% of similar public-school students. Bachelor's degree gains were small, for example 12% vs. 10% for students who entered in grades 8–10. This study compares matched students, not lottery winners and losers.
- **Who uses them is changing:** Arizona's universal ESA grew from about 12,000 to nearly 90,000 students between 2021–22 and 2024–25. RAND says many early users were likely already in private school or homeschooled.

**Open questions**
- Do the new universal programs help their users? RAND reports that *no* state yet has the program features and data needed to measure ESA effects on achievement.
- Do tax-credit scholarships and ESAs count as "vouchers"? Your question's scope depends on it.
- Are results driven by the program itself, or by which private schools choose to take part?
