## Second briefing: voucher effects on the students who use them

**Key debates**
- **Test scores vs. long-term outcomes.** Some lottery studies find that test scores stay flat or drop while graduation or college enrollment rises. People disagree about which outcome should count most.
- **Old programs vs. new ones.** Earlier studies were mostly of small programs and found neutral-to-positive results. Some recent large statewide programs found sizable losses, especially in math. Researchers disagree about why.
- **What the pooled evidence says.** A 2021 meta-analysis found "moderate evidence of positive achievement impacts," with large differences between programs. Critics, including a National Education Policy Center review, dispute its methods. I found that review but couldn't open it, so I can't tell you what it argues.

**Major voices**
- Patrick Wolf and colleagues at the University of Arkansas. They led several of these evaluations and the meta-analysis, and they're broadly sympathetic to school choice. Keep that in mind when you weigh their interpretations.
- Economists Abdulkadiroğlu, Pathak and Walters, on Louisiana.
- Waddington and Berends at Notre Dame, on Indiana.
- The federal Institute of Education Sciences (IES), which commissioned the D.C. evaluations.
- Critics at the National Education Policy Center.

**Patterns**
- The strongest studies use **admission lotteries**, which work like randomized experiments.
- **Math losses** show up repeatedly in recent statewide programs. Indiana's average first-year math loss was about 0.15 standard deviations. Louisiana's was about 0.4 standard deviations in the economists' study.
- Effects **vary a lot** by program, grade level and which private schools take part. In Louisiana, the math losses were concentrated in lower-tuition participating schools.

**Open questions**
- Why did older programs and newer statewide programs produce such different results?
- Are test scores even the right way to measure whether a voucher helped a student?
- What about the recent wave of universal, everyone-eligible programs? I found almost no rigorous evidence on them yet.
