**Peer-reviewed studies (quantitative)**

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Allcott, H., Baron, J., Dee, T. S., Duckworth, A. L., Gentzkow, M. A., & Jacob, B. A. (2026). The effects of school phone bans: National evidence from lockable pouches. *The Quarterly Journal of Economics*. Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjag050
   This is a nationwide U.S. study of middle and high schools that adopted lockable phone pouches. It used GPS phone data, teacher surveys, test scores and school records. Phone use dropped, discipline incidents rose in year one and then faded, and well-being dipped at first and later turned positive. It found "no clear evidence" of change in perceived online bullying.

2. **Peer-reviewed article.** Bonilla Aparicio, E., Sigmarsdottir, B., Haskell, E., Sirotina, U., Sveinbjörnsdóttir, B., Ásgeirsdóttir, B. B., Valdimarsdottir, H. B., Odgers, C. L., Sigfúsdóttir, I. D., Allegrante, J. P., & Halldorsdottir, T. (2026). School phone bans and adolescent mental health in Iceland. *JAMA Network Open, 9*(10), e2640893. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.40893
   This study compared 70 Icelandic schools before and after 22 of them adopted bans, covering about 24,000 student responses. Bans weren't linked to meaningful changes in mental health, and results were similar for peer support and for both in-person bullying and cyberbullying. The students were ages 13–15, which is younger than high school.

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Vereecke, J., Vanherle, R., Nassen, L.-M., Boen, H., Eggermont, S., Beullens, K., Vandenbosch, L., & Schreurs, L. (2026). More than disconnecting? School smartphone bans and adolescents' social wellbeing. *Frontiers in Developmental Psychology, 4*, Article 1796471. https://doi.org/10.3389/fdpys.2026.1796471
   This study followed 960 students at one school through eight survey waves before and after a total ban. Friend support didn't change, social loneliness rose modestly over the year, cyberbullying victimization didn't change, and perpetration dropped slightly in the short term. Because it's a single school, it's hard to generalize from.

4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S. A. M., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P. H., Quinlan, J. I., Sitch, A. J., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. J. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): A cross-sectional observational study. *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51*, Article 101211. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211
   This compared 1,227 students aged 12–15 at 30 English schools with strict or permissive phone policies. Students at strict schools used their phones less during school, but there was no difference in total weekday or weekend use and no difference in well-being. It's a snapshot comparison, so it shows associations rather than cause and effect.

5. **Peer-reviewed article.** Chen, S.-C., Huang, T.-F., Chang, K., Chang, F.-C., Chiang, S. C., Chiu, C.-H., Chen, P.-H., Miao, N.-F., & Chuang, H.-Y. (2025). Association between school phone restriction policies and adolescents' cyberbullying, gambling, and substance use behaviors. *Computers in Human Behavior, 177*, Article 108898. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108898
   This was a one-time survey of 3,158 high school students in Taiwan. Students who believed their school had no phone restrictions reported more cyberbullying and other online risk behaviors. Note that it measured what students *perceived* about their school's policy, not the policy itself.

**Peer-reviewed studies (qualitative)**

6. **Peer-reviewed article.** Bar, E., Radünz, M., Galanis, C. R., Quinney, B. A., Wade, T. D., & King, D. L. (2025). Student perspectives on banning mobile phones in South Australian secondary schools: A large-scale qualitative analysis. *Computers in Human Behavior, 167*, Article 108603. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108603
   The researchers analyzed 1,549 secondary students' open-ended survey answers. Students reported benefits for face-to-face interaction. They also reported feeling less trusted and finding it harder to manage emotional distress without their phones. This is self-reported data, not an outcome the researchers measured.

7. **Peer-reviewed article.** Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S. A. M., Michail, M., Patterson, P. H., Sitch, A. J., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. J. (2026). How school phone policies influence adolescent phone use and wellbeing (SMART Schools): A qualitative comparative case study. *Social Science & Medicine, 398*, Article 119094. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2026.119094
   This drew on 40 focus groups with 177 pupils, parents and staff at 7 English schools. It found both positive and negative effects of strict and permissive policies on students' connectedness and well-being, and it stresses how much out-of-school life matters.

**Peer-reviewed review**

8. **Peer-reviewed article (scoping review).** Campbell, M. A., Edwards, E. J., Pennell, D., Poed, S., Lister, V., Gillett-Swan, J. K., Kelly, A. B., Zec, D., & Nguyen, T.-A. (2024). Evidence for and against banning mobile phones in schools: A scoping review. *Journal of Psychologists and Counsellors in Schools, 34*(3), 242–265. https://doi.org/10.1177/20556365241270394
   This reviews 22 studies on academics, mental health and cyberbullying. It found no randomized trials, and the studies it covers define a "ban" in different ways. Its reference list is a good place to look for more studies.

**Other kinds of source**

9. **Research organization report (not peer reviewed).** Silver, D., Rapaport, A., & Polikoff, M. (2026, September 10). *A year later, what teens tell us about school cellphone bans*. Brookings Institution. https://www.brookings.edu/articles/a-year-later-what-teens-tell-us-about-school-cellphone-bans/
   - **What it reports:** USC researchers surveyed U.S. teens across the 2025–26 school year. 58% said their school's policy didn't change their phone use outside school, 30% said they used their phone more, and 12% said less.
   - **Who's behind it:** The authors argue *for* strict bell-to-bell bans. Brookings calls itself "nonpartisan" on its About page.
   - **Why it's here:** It gives national survey numbers. The numbers are self-reports, and the conclusions are the authors' argument.

## Coverage report

- **What I searched:** I ran about 15 searches across both sub-questions. These included general web searches, the OpenAlex scholarly database (8 keyword searches plus individual record checks) and a search of Pew Research.
- **Where evidence is plentiful:** bans and bullying or cyberbullying, and bans and well-being. Large national studies came out in 2025–26.
- **Where evidence is thin:**
  - Direct measures of in-person interaction. Almost everything is students' own reports, and I found no studies where researchers observed lunchrooms or hallways.
  - Whether conflict *moves* to after school. No study I found tracks when bullying happens before and after a ban.
  - Studies limited to high school. Several samples are ages 12–15.
- **What I couldn't reach:**
  - The full texts of most articles, so I worked from abstracts.
  - A 2026 UCL report on a "displacement effect," because the page blocked me.
  - The text of the Ferguson editorial.
  - The original Norwegian study (Abrahamsson, 2024) that's often cited for large drops in bullying. I only saw it described secondhand.
- **Following the trail:** I followed citations only lightly.

## Questions these results raise

- Whether "more face-to-face talk" can be supported with anything other than what students say about themselves.
- What it would take to show that conflict *moved*, as opposed to simply not going down.
- Whether findings from 13–15-year-olds in Iceland or England apply to U.S. high schoolers.
