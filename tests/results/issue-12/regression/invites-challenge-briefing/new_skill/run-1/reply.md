## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Achievement.** This is the main argument. A six-state study in a peer-reviewed journal ([Morton, Thompson & Kuhfeld, *Economics of Education Review*, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econedurev.2024.102524)) found students learned less on average. A [Journalist's Resource summary](https://journalistsresource.org/education/four-day-school-week-research/) of that study says the effect was negligible in rural schools and medium-sized elsewhere. An Oregon study of early grades ([Thompson et al., *Early Childhood Research Quarterly*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecresq.2022.12.009)) found "minimal differences" in 3rd-grade scores.
- **Money.** People disagree about whether the savings are big enough to matter. A 2025 Colorado working paper, not yet peer reviewed ([Bowser, EdWorkingPapers](https://doi.org/10.26300/ccrh-7p86)), estimates savings of 1–3% of total spending, mostly from food service, transportation, and support services. Its author works at the National Council on Teacher Quality, and I couldn't confirm that organization's leaning this session. A peer-reviewed study of savings is [Thompson, *National Tax Journal*, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1086/712916), but I couldn't read its figures.
- **Teachers.** Districts that favor the schedule say it helps them hire. Journalist's Resource reports that one Missouri district's job applications rose more than 360%. Bowser found no significant drop in teacher turnover, and Journalist's Resource summarizes a 2025 CALDER working paper that found no recruitment or retention benefit in Missouri.

**Major voices**
- Economist Paul Thompson at Oregon State University, who appears on many of the studies here
- Emily Morton at NWEA
- RAND, whose six-state report is [*Can Four Equal Five?*](https://doi.org/10.7249/rba373-1) (2021). I found it but couldn't read the text.
- CALDER at the American Institutes for Research
- The University of Oregon's HEDCO Institute, whose review of 11 studies found "no evidence of large positive effects" (as quoted by Journalist's Resource)

**Patterns**
- **Rural vs. non-rural.** Results keep differing by setting. A 2026 study ([Najam & Thompson, *Journal of Policy Analysis and Management*](https://doi.org/10.1002/pam.70101)) found juvenile crime rose in non-rural areas, which echoes an earlier Colorado finding of roughly a 20% rise ([Fischer & Argyle, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econedurev.2018.03.010)).
- **Total hours matter.** Several studies tie the losses to schools that also cut weekly instructional time.
- **Test scores dominate.** Most studies measure scores. Fewer look at health, such as the Oregon survey linking four-day weeks to more food insecurity and less PE ([Tomayko et al., *Journal of School Health*, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/josh.13095)).

**Open questions**
- How working parents and child care are affected. I found almost no direct research on this.
- How districts actually make the decision, and how state rules are changing.
- Whether the hiring advantage fades as more nearby districts switch.

**Scale:** More than 2,100 public schools in 26 states now use a four-day week. Texas alone went from 30 schools in 2020–21 to 506 in 2024–25, according to Journalist's Resource.
