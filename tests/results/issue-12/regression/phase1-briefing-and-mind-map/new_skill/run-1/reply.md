## Briefing: four-day school weeks

**Key debates**
- **Does it hurt learning?** This is the central fight. A RAND study of Idaho, New Mexico and Oklahoma found four-day students fell behind five-day peers in reading and math over time. It also found small cost savings and high satisfaction among parents. ([Kilburn et al., *Does Four Equal Five?*, RAND, 2021](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA373-1.html). RAND calls itself "nonprofit, nonpartisan" on its [about page](https://www.rand.org/about.html).) Other studies find little or no difference.
- **Does it help schools keep teachers?** Districts often switch for this reason. A Missouri and Oklahoma study found no significant overall effect on teacher retention, and turnover went up as more nearby districts switched. ([Thompson, *Economics of Education Review*, 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecoedu/v109y2025ics0272775725000883.html). That's an index listing; the publisher page wouldn't open for me.)
- **Who decides?** Missouri's 2024 law requires a public vote before districts in charter counties or cities over 30,000 people can adopt a four-day week. A "yes" vote sets the schedule for ten years. ([RSMo 171.028](https://www.revisor.mo.gov/main/OneSection.aspx?section=171.028)) I haven't yet opened a source that argues for or against the law itself, so the political debate around it is still a gap.

**Major voices**
- Economist Paul N. Thompson (Oregon State) is behind many of the studies.
- RAND produced the largest mixed-methods report.
- Education Week and The Journalist's Resource summarize the field for general readers ([EdWeek, Apr. 2026](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/the-4-day-school-week-what-research-shows-about-the-alternative-schedule/2026/04); [Journalist's Resource, 2025](https://journalistsresource.org/education/four-day-school-week-research/)).

**Patterns**
- The schedule is mostly rural, but it's spreading. Both news sources put it at more than 2,100 schools in 26 states.
- Most studies compare test scores before and after districts switch. Several find that results depend on how many instructional hours a district keeps. An Oregon working paper links its losses to nearly 3.5 fewer hours of class a week ([Thompson, IZA, 2019](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/12204). This version is not peer reviewed.)
- Averages can hide differences between groups. In Oregon's early grades, the strongest kindergarten performers were the most negatively affected, while several disadvantaged groups showed no significant harm ([Thompson et al., *Early Childhood Research Quarterly*, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9979633)).

**Open questions**
- **What happens on the fifth day?** A Colorado study found about a 20% rise in juvenile offenses, mostly property crime ([Fischer & Argyle, 2018](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecoedu/v64y2018icp31-39.html)). An Oklahoma study reported in EdWeek found less bullying and fighting at school.
- **Do rural findings apply to suburban districts** that are now switching?
- **Childcare, parents' jobs and off-day programs** have barely been studied.

I opened every linked page this session, but for several studies I only saw the abstract or an index listing, so open each one and check that the numbers match before you rely on them. The one to question me on is the RAND achievement finding, since so much of the debate rests on it.
