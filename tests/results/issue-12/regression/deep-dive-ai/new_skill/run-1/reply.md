**Peer-reviewed research**

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(26), Article e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
   This was a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students. While students had GPT-4 access, their practice grades rose. When access was taken away, students who'd used the plain ChatGPT-style version did 17% worse than students who never had it. A version built with learning safeguards mostly avoided that drop. *The PNAS site blocked my reader, so I worked from the abstract and confirmed the DOI in Crossref. PNAS published a correction in August 2025 that I couldn't read. Check what it changed.*

2. **Peer-reviewed conference paper.** Vanzo, A., Pal Chowdhury, S., & Sachan, M. (2025). GPT-4 as a homework tutor can improve student engagement and learning outcomes. In *Proceedings of the 63rd Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (Volume 1: Long Papers)* (pp. 31119–31136). [aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1502](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1502/)
   This was a randomized trial in four high school English-as-a-second-language classes, where the treatment classes did their homework with a GPT-4 tutor. Learning outcomes didn't suffer, one group made significantly larger gains, and the students were more satisfied. *I could only read the abstract, which doesn't give the number of students.*

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Fütterer, T., Bardach, L., Kuhn, J., Keller, S. D., & Gerjets, P. (2026). Enhancing school students' self-regulated learning through generative AI support: A randomized controlled trial. *Educational Psychology Review, 38*(1), Article 42. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-026-10133-8
   371 students in grades 7–9 were randomly assigned to one of two specially designed AI supports or to standard ChatGPT, for six class sessions. Neither designed support beat standard ChatGPT on the main outcomes listed in the abstract. *Abstract only. The publisher page needed a login.*

4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Klarin, J., Hoff, E., Larsson, A., & Daukantaitė, D. (2024). Adolescents' use and perceived usefulness of generative AI for schoolwork: Exploring their relationships with executive functioning and academic achievement. *Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, 7*, Article 1415782. https://doi.org/10.3389/frai.2024.1415782
   These were surveys of two groups of Swedish adolescents, one averaging age 14 and one averaging 17. Students with more executive-function difficulties rated chatbots as more useful for finishing assignments. In the younger group, AI use wasn't linked to grades. It's correlational, so it can't show cause.

5. **Peer-reviewed meta-analysis.** Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y., & Liu, S. (2025). Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies. *Computers & Education, 227*, Article 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224
   This review pools 69 experimental studies. Overall, ChatGPT improved academic performance and reduced mental effort. Most of the studies were done at universities, and the authors warn about weak methods in many of them. *Abstract only.*

6. **Peer-reviewed article.** Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544
   117 university students were randomly assigned to write with ChatGPT, a human expert, writing tools, or no help, and the researchers tracked how they managed their own learning. These are university students, not high schoolers. *Abstract only. The publisher blocked my reader.*

**Working papers and preprints (not peer reviewed)**

7. **Working paper, not peer reviewed.** Strömberg, D., Lei, V., & Wu, Y. (2026). *The generative AI learning penalty: Evidence from Chinese secondary education* [Working paper]. SSRN.
   This study followed about 26,000 Chinese middle and high school students for 30 months. AI use raised homework scores, but monthly exam scores fell. According to news coverage, about 81% of AI users finished homework unusually fast, which the authors treat as outsourcing it. The other 19% spent about as much time as non-users, and their exam scores held steady. *SSRN blocked my reader, so I couldn't open the paper. These details come from a news report I did open, a [South China Morning Post article reprinted in The Star](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/07/17/ai-homework-tools-cut-exam-scores-by-20-study-of-26000-chinese-students-finds). It's observational, not randomized, so it shows a pattern rather than proving cause. Read the paper itself before you rely on it.*

8. **Preprint, not peer reviewed.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2025). *AI meets the classroom: When do large language models harm learning?* (arXiv:2409.09047) [Preprint]. arXiv. [arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047)
   This combines a university field study with two lab experiments on learning to code. Students who used the AI to generate solutions covered more topics but understood each one less. Students who asked it for explanations understood more. It also reports that AI widened the gap between students with low and high prior knowledge. These are university and lab participants, not teens.

9. **Preprint, not peer reviewed.** Contractor, Z., & Reyes, G. (2026). *Experimental evidence on the learning impact of generative AI* (arXiv:2607.08849) [Preprint]. arXiv. [arxiv.org/abs/2607.08849](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08849)
   This was a proctored randomized experiment with undergraduates. AI access raised test scores, and a smaller gain was still there a week later. The lasting gains showed up for students who used AI as a tutor and faded for students who had it write for them. *Abstract only. The sample size isn't given there.*

**Survey**

10. **Research organization report.** McClain, C., Anderson, M., Sidoti, O., & Bishop, W. (2026, February 24). *How teens use and view AI*. Pew Research Center. [pewresearch.org](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/)
    This is a fall 2025 survey of 1,458 U.S. teens. 54% had used chatbots for schoolwork help, and 10% said they do all or most of their schoolwork with them. It tells you how common and what kind of use is happening, not whether that use helps teens learn.

## Questions the results raise

- Most of these studies sort students by what they ask the chatbot for. None sorts them by whether they check their own work afterward.
- Several studies find that the tool's design (guardrails, tutor prompts) matters, not just the student's habits.
- Prior knowledge and executive function come up again and again as factors that might explain who uses chatbots well.
