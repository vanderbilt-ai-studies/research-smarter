## Second briefing: four-day school weeks

### Key debates

**1. Students: less time in school versus test scores.** Four-day districts make each day longer, but students still lose time over the year. RAND's study of four-day districts in several states found 146 school days instead of 171, which works out to **58 fewer hours a year**. Test scores in those districts still went up, just more slowly than in similar five-day districts, and the gap grew over time. Eight years after switching, students were 0.145–0.229 standard deviations behind in English and 0.144–0.189 behind in math. RAND says those gaps are "larger than the average effect sizes of most educational interventions."
- Not every study agrees. A 2021 paper by Paul Thompson says Anderson and Walker (2015) found *positive* effects on achievement in Colorado before the Great Recession. I couldn't open that study myself.

**2. Teachers: does a three-day weekend keep them from quitting?** This is where the evidence splits most.
- A 2025 University of Arkansas working paper on Texas found teacher retention went up about **3 percentage points**, and 4.4 points for teachers with five or fewer years of experience. The effect showed up in the second year after switching.
- Education Week (January 2026) reported on what it called the largest study yet, also in Texas. It found turnover fell about **2.7 points**, but more than 1 in 5 teachers still left each year. Districts also didn't end up with more experienced or more qualified teachers.
- The Arkansas paper's own review of earlier studies describes small, uncertain effects in Missouri and small negative or insignificant effects in Colorado.

**3. Money: do districts actually save?** Saving money is the main reason districts switch. Thompson's national study in the *National Tax Journal* found the switch itself saves only about **1–2%** of spending. The biggest cut was transportation, about 10.7% lower. Most districts had already cut teaching budgets *before* switching, which suggests the four-day week often comes alongside other cuts rather than replacing them.

**4. The day off: supervision and crime.** Fischer and Argyle studied Colorado from 1997 to 2014 and found youth crime rose nearly 20%, mostly property crime. I only read a news report of that study, not the paper. A 2026 peer-reviewed study (Najam and Thompson) found increases in property and violent juvenile crime, but mainly in **non-rural** areas, plus a *decrease* in drug and alcohol offenses during school hours.

### Major voices
- **Economists of education** produce most of the research. A few names appear across many papers, especially Paul Thompson (Oregon State) and Emily Morton. With so few groups doing the work, it's worth noticing when studies share authors and when they don't.
- **RAND**, a research organization that describes itself as nonprofit and nonpartisan in the brief I opened.
- **Families and district leaders.** In RAND's surveys they strongly favor the schedule: 69% of elementary parents said they'd "definitely" choose four days.

### Patterns
- **What people feel versus what's measured.** In RAND's study, families and staff loved the schedule, and leaders believed it helped keep teachers. The test-score data and the absence data (no statistical difference in absences) told a more mixed story.
- **Effects change with time.** The achievement gaps grew over the years. The Texas retention gains took about two years to appear.
- **Where matters.** Most four-day districts are small and rural. Some effects, like crime, show up mainly outside rural areas.
- **Students and teachers may land in different places.** The evidence for each group points in different directions, and your question names both.

### Open questions in the research
- Does it matter what districts do with the fifth day? RAND found schools rarely offered academic activities on that day.
- When one district switches, does it just pull teachers away from neighboring districts? The Arkansas author flags this as a reason his retention estimate may be too high.
- How should a community weigh outcomes against each other? RAND's conclusion says "each community must ultimately decide how to weigh which outcomes matter the most."
