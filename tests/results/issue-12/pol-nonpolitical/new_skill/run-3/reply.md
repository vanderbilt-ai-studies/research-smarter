1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Gould JL. 1980. Sun compensation by bees. Science. 207(4430):545–547. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.207.4430.545
   Bees were kept from seeing the sun for 2 hours. When they flew again, they compensated by extrapolating: they assumed the sun was still moving at the rate it had been moving when they last saw it. Gould argues this points to a strategy of averaging over time. *A short, older paper. I could only read the abstract because the full text is paywalled.*

2. **Peer-reviewed article.** Dyer FC, Dickinson JA. 1994. Development of sun compensation by honeybees: how partially experienced bees estimate the sun's course. Proc Natl Acad Sci USA. 91(10):4471–4474. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.91.10.4471 (free full text on PubMed Central: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC43807/)
   Bees raised in an incubator had only ever seen the late-afternoon sun. When they were tested on a cloudy morning, their dances treated the sun as roughly stationary and about 180° from where they'd seen it in the evenings. From about noon on, they switched to the evening position. The authors read this as evidence that bees inherit a general template of how the sun moves.

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Towne WF, Moscrip H. 2008. The connection between landscapes and the solar ephemeris in honeybees. J Exp Biol. 211(23):3729–3736. https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.022970
   A hive was moved to a differently oriented "twin" landscape under overcast skies. The bees still danced as if the sun held its usual relationship to their home landscape. The results suggest bees store the sun's course relative to the landscape panorama, possibly the skyline in particular.
