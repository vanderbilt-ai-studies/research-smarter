Here are three peer-reviewed studies on how bees account for the sun moving across the sky. All three count toward your requirement of at least 4 peer-reviewed sources. Citations are in CSE name-year format.

**Peer-reviewed articles**

1. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*). Dyer FC, Dickinson JA. 1994. Development of sun compensation by honeybees: how partially experienced bees estimate the sun's course. Proc Natl Acad Sci USA. 91(10):4471–4474. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.91.10.4471
   The researchers raised bees in an incubator and let them see the sun only in the late afternoon. They then tested the bees on a cloudy morning by watching which way their waggle dances pointed. All morning, the bees acted as if the sun sat roughly still, about 180° from the evening position they had seen. From around noon on, they switched to the evening position. The authors argue that bees are born knowing the general pattern of the sun's movement.

2. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Journal of Experimental Biology*). Towne WF, Moscrip H. 2008. The connection between landscapes and the solar ephemeris in honeybees. J Exp Biol. 211(23):3729–3736. https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.022970
   Bees learn how the sun's daily path lines up with the landscape around their hive. On overcast days they can work out where the sun is from the landscape alone. The researchers moved a hive to a similar landscape facing a different direction. The bees still danced as if they were in their original landscape, even for flight routes they had never flown. The authors suggest the skyline may be the main reference bees use for this.

3. **Peer-reviewed article** (*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*). Cheeseman JF, Millar CD, Greggers U, Lehmann K, Pawley MDM, Gallistel CR, Warman GR, Menzel R. 2014. Way-finding in displaced clock-shifted bees proves bees use a cognitive map. Proc Natl Acad Sci USA. 111(24):8949–8954. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1408039111
   The researchers used general anesthesia to shift bees' internal clocks, which threw off their sun compass. Then they released the bees away from the hive. After the bees noticed their first heading was wrong, they flew home just as accurately and quickly as untreated bees. The authors say this rules out bees homing only by a sun-based direction tied to remembered landmarks, and supports the idea that bees have a mental map. Two critical comments on this paper were published in the same journal later in 2014. They're worth tracking down.
