Welcome back. You've finished Phases 0–4. Your question is "Do vaccines cause autism?" but you haven't written a working position or counterargument yet. Last time you were checking a source you found yourself.

**I can't give you two equal sides, because the evidence isn't split.** Researchers have studied this question heavily, and it's settled: vaccines don't cause autism. If your essay presents it as an even debate, it will misstate the evidence. Being fair means weighing each side by how strong its evidence is, not giving both the same amount of space. Here's what I opened and checked this session:

- **Peer-reviewed systematic review.** Di Pietrantonj, Carlo, et al. "Vaccines for Measles, Mumps, Rubella, and Varicella in Children." *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews*, no. 11, 2021, CD004407. https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD004407.pub5 ([PMC page](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8607336/)). This review pooled the research on these vaccines. It found "no evidence of an association" between the MMR vaccine and autism spectrum disorders, based on 2 studies covering 1,194,764 children. The authors rated their certainty "moderate."
- **News article in a medical journal.** Eggertson, Laura. "Lancet Retracts 12-Year-Old Article Linking Autism to MMR Vaccines." *CMAJ*, vol. 182, no. 4, 2010, pp. E199–E200. https://doi.org/10.1503/cmaj.109-3179 ([PMC page](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2831678)). This reports how the 1998 Wakefield paper, which started the vaccine–autism claim, was retracted after UK regulators found Wakefield had acted unethically. It's a news report, not research, so it's useful for the history.
- **News article.** Stobbe, Mike. "CDC 'Vaccine Safety' Webpage Changed to Contradict Scientific Conclusion That Vaccines Don't Cause Autism." *PBS NewsHour*, Associated Press, 20 Nov. 2025, [link](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/health/cdc-vaccine-safety-webpage-changed-to-contradict-scientific-conclusion-that-vaccines-dont-cause-autism). You may come across this page while researching. In November 2025 the CDC's page was changed to say that "vaccines do not cause autism" is "not an evidence-based claim." The AP reports that the change cited no new research and that scientists criticized it. When a government page says something, that alone doesn't make it evidence. Check what research the page relies on.

I also found a 2019 Danish study of 657,461 children in *Annals of Internal Medicine* (Hviid et al.). It found no increased autism risk after the MMR vaccine. The PubMed page wouldn't load for me, so I haven't linked it. Find it through your library before you cite it.

**There are real debates on this topic.** For example: why the myth spread and why it lasted, how doctors or schools should respond to parents who hesitate about vaccines, and what happens when health agencies change their messaging. Any of these would let you argue a thesis and still be fair to the evidence. Which direction you take is your call.
