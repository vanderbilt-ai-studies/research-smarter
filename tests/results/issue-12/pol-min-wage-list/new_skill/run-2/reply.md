## Sources

**Peer-reviewed articles.** All eight count toward your "at least 3 peer-reviewed" requirement. I confirmed each journal through Crossref and OpenAlex and checked that every DOI resolves. I read the abstracts, not the full papers.

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Cengiz, D., Dube, A., Lindner, A., & Zipperer, B. (2019). The effect of minimum wages on low-wage jobs. *The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 134*(3), 1405–1454. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjz014
   Looks at 138 state minimum wage increases from 1979 to 2016. Finds the number of low-wage jobs "remained essentially unchanged" over five years, with no evidence of job loss at higher minimum wage levels either, though there was some reduced employment in tradeable sectors.

2. **Peer-reviewed article.** Jardim, E., Long, M. C., Plotnick, R., van Inwegen, E., Vigdor, J., & Wething, H. (2022). Minimum-wage increases and low-wage employment: Evidence from Seattle. *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 14*(2), 263–314. https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20180578
   Uses state administrative data on Seattle's increases to $11 and then $13. Workers were no less likely to stay employed, but their hours fell significantly, especially for less experienced workers, and hiring into low-wage jobs declined.

3. **Peer-reviewed article (review of the literature).** Neumark, D., & Shirley, P. (2022). Myth or measurement: What does the new minimum wage research say about minimum wages and job loss in the United States? *Industrial Relations, 61*(4), 384–417. https://doi.org/10.1111/irel.12306
   Collects the core estimates from published U.S. studies. Concludes there's "a clear preponderance of negative estimates," strongest for teens, young adults, and less-educated workers, and less one-sided in studies of low-wage industries.

4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Clemens, J., & Wither, M. (2019). The minimum wage and the Great Recession: Evidence of effects on the employment and income trajectories of low-skilled workers. *Journal of Public Economics, 170*, 53–67. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2019.01.004
   Compares states bound by the 2007–2009 federal increases with states that weren't. Reports significant negative effects on the employment and income growth of targeted low-skilled workers. I read only summaries of this abstract (from [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:pubeco:v:170:y:2019:i:c:p:53-67) and the [NBER working paper page](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20724)), not the journal's own page.

5. **Peer-reviewed article.** Godøy, A., & Reich, M. (2021). Are minimum wage effects greater in low-wage areas? *Industrial Relations, 60*(1), 36–83. https://doi.org/10.1111/irel.12267
   Studies low-wage counties where the minimum wage was as high as 82% of the local median wage. Finds wage gains, no detectable drop in employment or hours, and "substantial declines in household and child poverty" in the counties where the minimum wage had the biggest effect.

6. **Peer-reviewed article.** Dube, A. (2019). Minimum wages and the distribution of family incomes. *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 11*(4), 268–304. https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20170085
   Finds higher minimum wages raise family incomes at the bottom and lower poverty. Reduced public assistance offsets part of those gains: once tax credits and noncash benefits are counted, the gains are about two-thirds as large.

7. **Peer-reviewed article (historical).** Derenoncourt, E., & Montialoux, C. (2021). Minimum wages and racial inequality. *The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 136*(1), 169–228. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjaa031
   Studies the 1966 extension of the federal minimum wage to new industries. Earnings rose sharply, nearly twice as much for Black workers as for white workers, with no aggregate employment effect. Crossref lists December 2020 for the issue, so check which year your APA citation needs.

8. **Peer-reviewed article (historical).** Bailey, M. J., DiNardo, J., & Stuart, B. A. (2021). The economic impact of a high national minimum wage: Evidence from the 1966 Fair Labor Standards Act. *Journal of Labor Economics, 39*(S2), S329–S367. https://doi.org/10.1086/712554
   Studies the same 1966 law as #7, which raised the national minimum to its highest level of the 20th century. Finds wages rose sharply and total employment fell "only modestly," but job losses were significantly larger among African American men. The design differs from #7, so the two papers are worth reading together.
