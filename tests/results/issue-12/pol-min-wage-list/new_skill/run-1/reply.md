## Sources (10 in this list, 4 more in your log)

**Peer-reviewed journal articles** (these 5 meet your requirement of 3 peer-reviewed sources)

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Cengiz, D., Dube, A., Lindner, A., & Zipperer, B. (2019). The effect of minimum wages on low-wage jobs. *The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 134*(3), 1405–1454. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjz014
   Looks at 138 state minimum wage increases from 1979 to 2016. It finds the number of low-wage jobs stayed about the same over the following five years, and it finds no job losses at higher minimum wage levels either. It does find some job loss in tradeable sectors (industries whose goods can be sold elsewhere, like manufacturing).
2. **Peer-reviewed article.** Jardim, E., Long, M. C., Plotnick, R., van Inwegen, E., Vigdor, J., & Wething, H. (2022). Minimum-wage increases and low-wage employment: Evidence from Seattle. *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 14*(2), 263–314. https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20180578
   Studies Seattle's increases to as much as $13. Workers were no less likely to stay employed, but their hours fell significantly, especially for less experienced workers, and hiring into low-wage jobs dropped. The authors say their citywide estimate probably overstates the job effects.
3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Neumark, D., & Shirley, P. (2022). Myth or measurement: What does the new minimum wage research say about minimum wages and job loss in the United States? *Industrial Relations, 61*(4), 384–417. https://doi.org/10.1111/irel.12306
   A review of published US studies. It reports "a clear preponderance of negative estimates," with stronger evidence for teens, young adults, and less-educated workers, and less one-sided results in studies of low-wage industries. I read the abstract on the NBER working-paper page; the publisher's site wasn't checked.
4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Dube, A. (2019). Minimum wages and the distribution of family incomes. *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 11*(4), 268–304. https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20170085
   Finds that higher minimum wages raise family incomes at the bottom and lower the poverty rate over three or more years. Reduced public assistance offsets part of those gains.
5. **Peer-reviewed article.** Derenoncourt, E., & Montialoux, C. (2021). Minimum wages and racial inequality. *The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 136*(1), 169–228. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjaa031
   Studies the 1966 extension of the federal minimum wage to new industries. Earnings rose sharply, about twice as much for Black workers, and the authors find no overall job loss. It's historical evidence, so its relevance to today's increases is something you'd have to judge.

**Journal review articles.** Both come from the *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, an American Economic Association journal. I believe it commissions articles instead of running standard peer review, but I haven't confirmed that, so check before counting these as peer reviewed.

6. Manning, A. (2021). The elusive employment effect of the minimum wage. *Journal of Economic Perspectives, 35*(1), 3–26. https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.35.1.3
   Argues that negative job effects are hard to find while effects on wages are easy to find, and gives reasons, including employers having more wage-setting power than simple models assume. It's an argument built on existing evidence, not a new study.
7. Dube, A., & Lindner, A. (2021). City limits: What do local-area minimum wages do? *Journal of Economic Perspectives, 35*(1), 27–50. https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.35.1.27
   Covers city minimum wages, which went from 3 cities in 2010 to 42 in 2020. It finds early effects on wages and jobs broadly similar to the effects of state and federal increases.

9. **Working paper, not yet peer reviewed.** Clemens, J., Edwards, O., Meer, J., & Nguyen, J. D. (2025). *Employment effects of California's fast food minimum wage: Evidence from the QCEW and QWI* (NBER Working Paper No. 34033). National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w34033
   Estimates that fast-food employment was about 3.5% lower after two years than it would have been without the increase. The paper was revised in October 2026.
