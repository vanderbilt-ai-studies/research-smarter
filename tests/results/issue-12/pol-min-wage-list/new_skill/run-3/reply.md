## Sources (8 shown, grouped by kind)

**Peer-reviewed articles.** All six meet your three-peer-reviewed requirement. I confirmed each journal through Crossref and checked that each DOI works.

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Cengiz, D., Dube, A., Lindner, A., & Zipperer, B. (2019). The effect of minimum wages on low-wage jobs. *The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 134*(3), 1405–1454. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjz014
   Uses 138 state minimum wage increases from 1979 to 2016. Finds the number of low-wage jobs "remained essentially unchanged" for five years afterward, finds no job loss at higher minimum wage levels either, and finds some reduced employment in tradeable sectors.

2. **Peer-reviewed article.** Jardim, E., Long, M. C., Plotnick, R., van Inwegen, E., Vigdor, J., & Wething, H. (2022). Minimum-wage increases and low-wage employment: Evidence from Seattle. *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 14*(2), 263–314. https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20180578
   Studies Seattle's increases to $11 and then $13 using state administrative data. Finds no change in whether existing workers stayed employed, but significant cuts in hours (especially for less experienced workers) and less hiring into low-wage jobs.

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Wiltshire, J. C., McPherson, C., Reich, M., & Sosinskiy, D. (2026). Minimum wage effects and monopsony explanations. *Journal of Labor Economics, 44*(4), 1339–1381. https://doi.org/10.1086/735551
   Studies the near-doubling to $15 in California and New York (2013–2022), mostly fast-food workers and teens in 47 counties. Reports higher earnings and positive employment effects. *Watch for:* I couldn't open the journal's page. This summary comes from a 2023 description of the working paper by the Washington Center for Equitable Growth, a policy nonprofit that calls itself nonpartisan and aims to "promote shared prosperity" (per its About page). The published version may differ.

4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Clemens, J., & Wither, M. (2019). The minimum wage and the Great Recession: Evidence of effects on the employment and income trajectories of low-skilled workers. *Journal of Public Economics, 170*, 53–67. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2019.01.004
   Compares low-wage workers directly affected by the late-2000s federal increases with slightly higher-paid workers. Finds significant negative effects on their employment and income growth over three years. *Watch for:* I read the working-paper abstract, not the journal's.

5. **Peer-reviewed article (review of the research).** Neumark, D., & Shirley, P. (2022). Myth or measurement: What does the new minimum wage research say about minimum wages and job loss in the United States? *Industrial Relations, 61*(4), 384–417. https://doi.org/10.1111/irel.12306
   Collects the core estimates from published U.S. studies. Concludes there is "a clear preponderance of negative estimates," strongest for teens, young adults and less-educated workers, and less one-sided in studies of low-wage industries. *Watch for:* this is a review that weighs other studies, not new data. I read the working-paper abstract (NBER).

6. **Peer-reviewed article.** Dube, A. (2019). Minimum wages and the distribution of family incomes. *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 11*(4), 268–304. https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20170085
   Finds higher minimum wages raise family incomes at the bottom and lower the poverty rate over the long run. Reduced public assistance offsets part of the gain, leaving about 66% of it.

## Questions these results raise

- What counts as "large" isn't fixed. Seattle went to $13 and California and New York went to $15, and several studies talk about the minimum relative to local wages rather than in dollars.
- Some studies measure *hours* and *hiring* rather than jobs lost. Whether fewer hours counts as "costing jobs" is a call your thesis will need to make.
- Several findings differ by group (teens vs. adults, inexperienced vs. experienced workers) and by time frame (one year vs. five).
