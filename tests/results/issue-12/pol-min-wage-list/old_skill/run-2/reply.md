**Research articles**

1. **Peer-reviewed article** · Cengiz, D., Dube, A., Lindner, A., & Zipperer, B. (2019). The effect of minimum wages on low-wage jobs. *The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 134*(3), 1405–1454. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjz014 · [Abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v134y2019i3p1405-1454..html)
   Looks at 138 state minimum wage increases from 1979 to 2016. It finds the number of low-wage jobs "remained essentially unchanged" over the five years after each increase. Full text is paywalled; I read only the abstract.

2. **Peer-reviewed article** · Jardim, E., Long, M. C., Plotnick, R., van Inwegen, E., Vigdor, J., & Wething, H. (2022). Minimum-wage increases and low-wage employment: Evidence from Seattle. *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 14*(2), 263–314. https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20180578 · [Abstract](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20180578)
   Studies Seattle's increases to $11 in 2015 and $13 in 2016. Low-wage employment fell overall. Workers who already had jobs were no less likely to keep them, but their hours dropped significantly, especially for less experienced workers.

3. **Peer-reviewed article** · Dube, A. (2019). Minimum wages and the distribution of family incomes. *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 11*(4), 268–304. https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20170085 · [Abstract](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.20170085)
   Finds that higher minimum wages raise family incomes at the bottom and reduce poverty over the long run. Because families lose some public assistance, the net gains are about "66 percent as large" once that is counted.

4. **Peer-reviewed article** · Godøy, A., & Reich, M. (2021). Are minimum wage effects greater in low-wage areas? *Industrial Relations, 60*(1), 36–83. https://doi.org/10.1111/irel.12267 · [Abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/indres/v60y2021i1p36-83.html)
   Looks at low-wage US counties where the minimum wage reached as much as 82% of the local median wage. It finds higher wages and no detectable drop in employment, weekly hours or weeks worked. It also finds "substantial declines in household and child poverty."

5. **Peer-reviewed article** · Clemens, J., & Wither, M. (2019). The minimum wage and the Great Recession: Evidence of effects on the employment and income trajectories of low-skilled workers. *Journal of Public Economics, 170*, 53–67. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2019.01.004 · [Abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:pubeco:v:170:y:2019:i:c:p:53-67)
   Finds that the 2007–2009 federal increases reduced employment and income growth among the workers they targeted. It estimates employment fell "by at least half of a percentage point" in the states those increases affected. Watch for this: at least one critic argues the result depends heavily on which control variables are used.

**Reviews of the research**

6. **Peer-reviewed review article** · Neumark, D., & Shirley, P. (2022). Myth or measurement: What does the new minimum wage research say about minimum wages and job loss in the United States? *Industrial Relations, 61*(4), 384–417. https://doi.org/10.1111/irel.12306 · [Abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:indres:v:61:y:2022:i:4:p:384-417)
   Collects the main estimates from published US studies. It reports "a clear preponderance of negative estimates," strongest for teens, young adults and less-educated workers, and "less one-sided" evidence for low-wage industries.

7. **Journal review article** · Manning, A. (2021). The elusive employment effect of the minimum wage. *Journal of Economic Perspectives, 35*(1), 3–26. https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.35.1.3 · [Abstract](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.35.1.3)
   Argues that job losses from minimum wages are hard to detect, while wage gains are easy to see. It traces this mainly to imperfect competition in labor markets. This is an invited essay by a leading labor economist. I couldn't confirm it went through standard peer review, so don't count it toward your three peer-reviewed sources.

8. **Government report** · Congressional Budget Office. (2019). *The effects on employment and family income of increasing the federal minimum wage* (Report No. 55410). [Listing and summary](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cbo:report:55410)
   Models raising the federal minimum to $10, $12 or $15 by 2025. It finds most low-wage workers would earn more and some families would rise out of poverty, while other workers would lose their jobs. cbo.gov blocked my access, so I read only this summary. **Working papers (not peer reviewed)**

9. **Working paper, not yet peer reviewed** · Clemens, J., Edwards, O., Meer, J., & Nguyen, J. D. (2025). *Employment effects of California's fast food minimum wage: Evidence from the QCEW and QWI* (NBER Working Paper No. 34033). [NBER page](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34033)

## Questions these results raise

- These studies define "large" very differently: Seattle's $13, counties where the minimum reached 82% of the local median wage, and California's $20 for one industry. Your thesis depends on where you draw that line.
- Seattle (#2) measured cuts to hours, not only lost jobs. "Cost some of them their jobs" may not cover that.
- Several studies find stronger effects for teens and young adults than for low-wage workers in general.
