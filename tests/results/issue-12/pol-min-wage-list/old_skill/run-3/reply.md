## Sources

**Peer-reviewed articles.** All six meet your requirement of at least 3 peer-reviewed sources. For each one I checked the journal and the abstract, but I didn't read the full text.

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Cengiz, D., Dube, A., Lindner, A., & Zipperer, B. (2019). The effect of minimum wages on low-wage jobs. *The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 134*(3), 1405–1454. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjz014
   Looks at 138 state minimum wage increases from 1979 to 2016. It finds the number of low-wage jobs stayed about the same over the five years after each increase, and pay rose a little for workers just above the new minimum. It reports no job losses at higher minimum wage levels either, but some reduced employment in tradeable sectors.

2. **Peer-reviewed article.** Jardim, E., Long, M. C., Plotnick, R., van Inwegen, E., Vigdor, J., & Wething, H. (2022). Minimum-wage increases and low-wage employment: Evidence from Seattle. *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 14*(2), 263–314. https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20180578
   Studies Seattle's increase to as much as $13 in 2016 using state administrative data. Workers were no less likely to keep a job, but their hours fell significantly, especially for less experienced workers, and hiring into low-wage jobs dropped. It's a single-city study, and the authors say their citywide analysis probably overstates the effects.

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Neumark, D., & Shirley, P. (2022). Myth or measurement: What does the new minimum wage research say about minimum wages and job loss in the United States? *Industrial Relations: A Journal of Economy and Society, 61*(4), 384–417. https://doi.org/10.1111/irel.12306
   A review of every published U.S. study using state and local variation. It finds that most estimates are negative, especially for teens, young adults, the less educated, and directly affected workers. Studies of low-wage industries are less one-sided.

4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Godoey, A., & Reich, M. (2021). Are minimum wage effects greater in low-wage areas? *Industrial Relations: A Journal of Economy and Society, 60*(1), 36–83. https://doi.org/10.1111/irel.12267
   Looks at low-wage counties, where the minimum wage reached up to 82% of the local median wage. It finds pay went up but detects no drop in employment, weekly hours, or weeks worked. In the counties most affected, it finds large declines in household and child poverty.

5. **Peer-reviewed article.** Dube, A. (2019). Minimum wages and the distribution of family incomes. *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 11*(4), 268–304. https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20170085
   Finds that higher minimum wages raise incomes for families at the bottom and reduce poverty over three or more years. Reduced public assistance offsets part of the gain.

6. **Peer-reviewed article.** Derenoncourt, E., & Montialoux, C. (2021). Minimum wages and racial inequality. *The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 136*(1), 169–228. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjaa031
   A historical study of the 1966 law that extended the federal minimum wage to agriculture, restaurants, and other services. Earnings rose sharply, nearly twice as much for Black workers as for white workers. The authors find no overall effect on employment.

**Working papers (not yet peer reviewed).** These come from the National Bureau of Economic Research. Its papers are by established economists but haven't been through journal review, and the findings can change before publication.

7. **Working paper, not peer reviewed.** Clemens, J., & Strain, M. R. (2021). *The heterogeneous effects of large and small minimum wage changes: Evidence over the short and medium run using a pre-analysis plan* (NBER Working Paper No. 29264). National Bureau of Economic Research. https://www.nber.org/papers/w29264
   Uses Census survey data and sets its methods in advance. Large increases cut employment among low-skilled people by just over 2.5 percentage points, while the effects of small increases can't be told apart from zero. The same authors published a related peer-reviewed article on hours worked in *Economics Letters* (2026). It's in your log, but the publisher's site blocked me, so I couldn't read its findings.

8. **Working paper, not peer reviewed.** Dube, A. (2026). *Labor market effects of California's $20 fast-food minimum wage* (NBER Working Paper No. 35171). National Bureau of Economic Research. https://www.nber.org/papers/w35171
   Very recent, on California's 2024 fast-food minimum, which was about 77% of the state's median wage. It finds a wage increase of about 7% and small employment effects that vary by method. A competing 2026 paper by Neumark and colleagues finds job losses concentrated in small chains (it's in your log).

## Questions the results raise

- What counts as "large" isn't settled. Some studies measure it in dollars, some as a share of the local median wage, and some as a share of the old minimum.
- Several studies find fewer hours or less hiring rather than layoffs. That's worth weighing against "cost some of them their jobs."
- The studies disagree about what the evidence says overall, not just in their own results, so it matters how each one picks its evidence.
