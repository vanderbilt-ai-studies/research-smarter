## Sources

**Peer-reviewed articles.** I confirmed each journal through Crossref this session. All six count toward your "at least 3 peer-reviewed."

1. **Peer-reviewed article.** Cengiz, D., Dube, A., Lindner, A., & Zipperer, B. (2019). The effect of minimum wages on low-wage jobs. *The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 134*(3), 1405–1454. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjz014
   The authors studied 138 state minimum wage increases from 1979 to 2016. They found the total number of low-wage jobs stayed about the same over the five years after each increase, and pay rose a little for workers just above the new minimum. The abstract says they found no job loss at higher minimum wage levels either, though they did find some job loss in "tradeable" industries (ones that sell goods outside their local area).

2. **Peer-reviewed article.** Jardim, E., Long, M. C., Plotnick, R., van Inwegen, E., Vigdor, J., & Wething, H. (2022). Minimum-wage increases and low-wage employment: Evidence from Seattle. *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 14*(2), 263–314. https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20180578
   Seattle raised its minimum to as much as $11 in 2015 and $13 in 2016. Using state records, the authors found workers kept their jobs at the same rate, but their hours fell significantly, especially for less experienced workers. Hiring into low-wage jobs also dropped.

3. **Peer-reviewed article.** Dube, A. (2019). Minimum wages and the distribution of family incomes. *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 11*(4), 268–304. https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20170085
   Higher minimum wages raised family incomes at the bottom and lowered poverty rates over three or more years. Lost public assistance cancelled out part of the gain: once tax credits and benefits are counted, the gains average about 66% as large.

4. **Peer-reviewed article.** Neumark, D., & Shirley, P. (2022). Myth or measurement: What does the new minimum wage research say about minimum wages and job loss in the United States? *Industrial Relations, 61*(4), 384–417. https://doi.org/10.1111/irel.12306
   This is a review of all published U.S. studies that compare minimum wages across states or cities. It reports "a clear preponderance of negative estimates" for jobs. Those estimates are strongest for teens, young adults, the less educated, and the workers directly affected, and less one-sided in studies of low-wage industries.

5. **Peer-reviewed article (meta-analysis, which statistically combines many earlier studies).** Doucouliagos, H., & Stanley, T. D. (2009). Publication selection bias in minimum-wage research? A meta-regression analysis. *British Journal of Industrial Relations, 47*(2), 406–428. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8543.2009.00723.x
   The authors combined 64 U.S. studies and argue that journals favored publishing negative results. After correcting for that, they find "little or no evidence" that minimum wages reduce employment. It's older than the other studies here, so it doesn't cover anything since about 2008.

6. **Peer-reviewed article.** Wiltshire, J. C., McPherson, C., Reich, M., & Sosinskiy, D. (2026). Minimum wage effects and monopsony explanations. *Journal of Labor Economics, 44*(4), 1339–1381. https://doi.org/10.1086/735551

**Government reports**

7. **Government report.** Congressional Budget Office. (2019). *The effects on employment and family income of increasing the federal minimum wage* (Report No. 55410). Listing and abstract: https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cbo:report:55410 (cbo.gov blocked my request, so look for the full report on CBO's site).
   CBO estimated what raising the federal minimum to $10, $12, or $15 by 2025 would do. Its summary says most low-wage workers would earn more, lifting some families out of poverty, but other low-wage workers would lose their jobs. *Why it's here:* this is a forecast from a model, not a study of what actually happened, and CBO says its estimates are very uncertain.

8. **Government report.** U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. (2025). *Characteristics of minimum wage workers, 2024* (Report 1115). https://www.bls.gov/opub/reports/minimum-wage/2024/home.htm
   In 2024, about 82,000 hourly workers earned exactly $7.25 and 760,000 earned less, or 1.0% of hourly workers. That's down from 13.4% in 1979. *Why it's here:* it's descriptive data on who earns at or below the federal minimum. It doesn't test any effects. It also misses the many workers covered by higher state minimums.

**Working papers (not yet peer reviewed)**

9. **Preprint, not yet peer reviewed (NBER working paper).** Clemens, J., Edwards, O., Meer, J., & Nguyen, J. D. (2025). *Employment effects of California's fast food minimum wage: Evidence from the QCEW and QWI* (NBER Working Paper No. 34033). National Bureau of Economic Research. https://www.nber.org/papers/w34033
   This paper studies California's fast-food minimum wage rise from $16 to $20. ## Questions the results raise

- The studies disagree about what counts as a "large" increase. Some measure it in dollars, and some as a share of local wages (the $20 California wage is about 69% of the state's median full-time wage, according to the Berkeley paper). How you define "large" will shape what counts as evidence for your position.
- Jardim et al. separate losing a job from losing hours. Your position as written talks only about jobs.
- Two reviews (Neumark & Shirley, and Doucouliagos & Stanley) look at largely the same body of research and reach opposite conclusions. How a field summarizes itself is an open question in its own right.
