## Briefing: Do vouchers help the students who use them?

### Key debates

**1. Test scores: early evidence looked mildly positive, but large recent programs show losses.**
- *Peer-reviewed article,* American Economic Journal: Applied Economics (2018). Abdulkadiroğlu, Pathak and Walters, ["Free to Choose: Can School Choice Reduce Student Achievement?"](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aejapp:v:10:y:2018:i:1:p:175-206). They used admission lotteries, which work like a randomized experiment, to study the Louisiana Scholarship Program. Attending a voucher school lowered math scores by 0.4 standard deviations, which is a large drop. Reading, science and social studies scores fell too. The authors suggest that weaker, low-tuition private schools were the ones that joined the program. ([NBER working-paper version](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21839))
- *Peer-reviewed article,* Journal of Policy Analysis and Management (2018). Waddington and Berends, ["Impact of the Indiana Choice Scholarship Program"](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jpamgt/v37y2018i4p783-808.html). They compared voucher users with similar students who stayed in public school; this was a matched comparison, not a lottery. Math scores fell about 0.15 standard deviations in the first year and stayed lower. Reading showed no meaningful effect.
- *Research organization report,* Thomas B. Fordham Institute, [foreword to Figlio's evaluation of Ohio's EdChoice program](https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/commentary/evaluation-ohios-edchoice-scholarship-program-foreword) (2016). Voucher users did worse, especially in math, and the losses persisted. Public schools facing voucher competition improved modestly. Fordham's own [About page](https://fordhaminstitute.org/about) lists "educational choice" among its goals, so this finding goes against the publisher's own stated position.

**2. College attainment may tell a different story from test scores.**
- *Research organization report,* Urban Institute (2019). Chingos, Monarrez and Kuehn, ["The Effects of the Florida Tax Credit Scholarship Program on College Enrollment and Graduation"](https://www.urban.org/research/publication/effects-florida-tax-credit-scholarship-program-college-enrollment-and-graduation). It compares scholarship students with similar public school students; it is not a lottery study. 57% of scholarship students enrolled in college, compared with 51% of the comparison group, and the effects grew the longer students stayed in the program. Urban's About page doesn't state a political leaning, and I couldn't confirm one.
- *Critique of that study,* National Education Policy Center. Jaekyung Lee, ["NEPC Review"](https://nepc.colorado.edu/node/9706) (2019). Lee questions some of the study's methods and conclusions and warns voucher advocates against using it without understanding its limits. NEPC is based at CU Boulder and describes its mission as supporting "democratic deliberation about education policy." I couldn't confirm who funds it.
- *Working paper, not peer reviewed,* Harvard Program on Education Policy and Governance (May 2025). Chingos, Figlio and Karbownik, ["The Effects of Ohio's EdChoice Voucher Program on College Enrollment and Graduation"](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/centers/taubman/publications/effects-ohios-edchoice-voucher-program-college-enrollment-and). These are the same Ohio students whose test scores fell, now tracked into college: more than 6,000 voucher users compared with similar public school students. The page I opened describes the design but not the results. Search summaries reported higher college enrollment for voucher users, but I haven't confirmed that, so check it in the paper itself.

**3. What do the experiments show when pooled together?**
- *Peer-reviewed article,* School Effectiveness and School Improvement (2021). Shakeel, Anderson and Wolf, "The participant effects of private school vouchers around the globe: a meta-analytic and systematic review," DOI 10.1080/09243453.2021.1906283. The publisher's page blocked me, so I'm working only from the abstract in the OpenAlex database. The review combines 21 randomized trials covering 11 programs. It finds "moderate" positive effects on achievement, with large differences between programs and years. It also argues vouchers can be cost-effective even when they have no effect on test scores. Wolf has long been one of the main researchers on voucher programs; he co-led the [2009 federal DC evaluation](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/pubs/20094050/), among other work.
- *Government reports,* Institute of Education Sciences, U.S. Department of Education. The same DC program was evaluated twice and the results differed. The [2009 report](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/pubs/20094050/) found gains in reading but not math. The [2019 report](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/pubs/20194006/), studying later lottery groups, found no effect on reading or math after three years, but students reported more satisfaction with their schools and felt safer there.

### Patterns
- **Results depend on which outcome you measure.** The same program (Ohio) can lower test scores and still raise college enrollment. Whether vouchers "improve outcomes" depends a lot on which outcomes you mean.
- **Results depend on the program and the period.** Smaller, older programs (DC, plus the experiments in the meta-analysis) look mixed to positive. Larger statewide programs from the 2010s (Louisiana, Indiana, Ohio) show test-score losses, at least at first.
- **The strength of the research design varies.** Some studies use lotteries, which work like experiments. Others use matched comparisons, where families who choose vouchers may differ from those who don't in ways the data can't see.

### The newest wrinkle: universal programs
- *News article,* Education Week (Jan 2026). Stone and Peetz Stephens, ["As School Choice Goes Universal, What New Research Is Showing"](https://www.edweek.org/policy-politics/as-school-choice-goes-universal-what-new-research-is-showing/2026/01). According to Education Week's own count, 18 states now make nearly all students eligible. Early research on these programs is mostly working papers and state test results, not completed studies of whether students learn more.
- *Research organization analysis,* Brookings Brown Center. Klinenberg, Valant and Zerbino, ["Arizona's 'universal' education savings account program has become a handout to the wealthy"](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/arizonas-universal-education-savings-account-program-has-become-a-handout-to-the-wealthy/) (2024). It argues against the program as designed. Using state data, it found about 75 participants per 1,000 children in the lowest-poverty areas, compared with about 14 per 1,000 in the highest-poverty areas. I didn't open Brookings' About page, so I haven't confirmed any affiliation. I didn't find a peer-reviewed study of learning outcomes in Arizona.

### Open questions in the field
- Why would test scores fall while college enrollment rises?
- Does program design, like tuition caps and which schools can join, explain the losses in Louisiana?
- Does evidence from programs for low-income students apply to universal programs, where many users were already in private school?
