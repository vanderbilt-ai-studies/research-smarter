## Briefing: Do vouchers help the students who use them?

**Key debates**
- **Which "outcomes" count.** Studies measured test scores, high school graduation and college enrollment, attendance, and how satisfied students and parents are. These often point in different directions. Which outcome you focus on will shape your answer, so it's a choice you'll need to make deliberately.
- **Older programs vs. newer ones.** Early experiments, like a privately funded 1997 New York City lottery, found mostly neutral or positive results. Larger state programs from the 2010s found test-score drops:
  - **Louisiana:** a lottery study found math scores fell by 0.4 standard deviations, and reading, science, and social studies scores dropped too (Abdulkadiroğlu, Pathak & Walters, peer-reviewed, *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*, 2018). [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aejapp:v:10:y:2018:i:1:p:175-206) · [NBER version](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21839)
  - **Indiana:** a math loss of about 0.15 standard deviations that persisted, with no clear effect in English (Waddington & Berends, peer-reviewed, *Journal of Policy Analysis and Management*, 2018). This study had no random lottery, so it compared voucher students with matched public school peers. [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jpamgt/v37y2018i4p783-808.html)
- **Test scores vs. long-term results.** Supporters argue that graduation and college results matter more than scores, and look better. In the NYC lottery, the voucher offer had no significant effect on college enrollment or degrees overall. There were substantial effects for minority students (only marginally significant) and large effects for children of U.S.-born mothers (Chingos & Peterson, peer-reviewed, *Journal of Public Economics*, 2015). [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:pubeco:v:122:y:2015:i:c:p:1-12)

**Major voices**
- **Economists running lottery studies** (MIT, Duke, Berkeley), whose papers aren't tied to either side.
- **The federal government.** The Institute of Education Sciences' evaluation of the DC Opportunity Scholarship Program found no effect on math or reading after three years. Students, though not parents, reported more satisfaction and a greater sense of safety (Government report, Webber et al., 2019). [IES report page](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/pubs/20194006/)
- **University of Arkansas, Department of Education Reform.** Patrick Wolf, who holds an endowed chair "in School Choice" there, writes in *Education Next* that attainment effects are "larger and more consistent" than test-score effects. *Education Next* is sponsored by Harvard's Program on Education Policy and Governance and says it "partakes of no program, campaign, or ideology." These are magazine essays summarizing research, not studies. [Forum essay, 2018](https://www.educationnext.org/programs-benefit-disadvantaged-students-forum-private-school-choice/) · [Louisiana essay, 2019](https://www.educationnext.org/what-happened-bayou-examing-effects-louisiana-scholarship-program/) (in this one, Wolf reports that his team also found no effect on college entrance in Louisiana)
- **Critics.** The National Education Policy Center (University of Colorado) published a critique by Christopher Lubienski of the Arkansas team's 2016 meta-analysis of voucher lottery studies. The critique argues that nearly half the studies came from the authors or their associates. I found this in search results but couldn't read the PDF, so treat it as a lead. The meta-analysis itself was later published in *School Effectiveness and School Improvement* (2021), but I couldn't open the publisher's page either.

**Patterns**
- The strongest studies use lotteries, which can only cover oversubscribed schools.
- Early losses sometimes shrink over time. In DC, math gaps had closed by year three.
- Authors often propose program design as an explanation, for example that weaker private schools tend to join.

**Open questions**
- **Universal programs.** In Arizona's universal education savings account (ESA) program, most early users were already in private school or homeschooled (RAND case study, 2025, sponsored by the Walton Family Foundation per the report page). [RAND report](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA3431-2.html)
  - EdChoice, an organization whose stated mission is to "advance educational freedom and choice," reports that the share of users coming from public schools rose to 39%. [EdChoice article](https://www.edchoice.org/2025-arizona-and-the-diffusion-of-private-education/)
  - The Learning Policy Institute focuses on the cost, which rose to at least $708.5 million in 2023–24. [LPI report](https://learningpolicyinstitute.org/product/understanding-cost-universal-vouchers-report)
  - None of the three measures student achievement, and I didn't find outcome studies of universal programs yet.
- **Ohio.** A 2025 Harvard working paper tracks college results for Ohio's EdChoice program, but its abstract page doesn't state the findings. [Working paper page](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/centers/taubman/publications/effects-ohios-edchoice-voucher-program-college-enrollment-and)

Before you rely on a number here, like the 0.4 math drop in Louisiana, find it in the study's own abstract. You can also ask me how I know what the Chingos and Peterson paper found for minority students.
